HOW WE SIMULATED THEM
Nobody alive has heard these languages spoken in everyday life, so every voice in this video is an AI simulation, not a recording. The method:
1. A real surviving text: every line is a real text or word list that survives, not invented sentences.
2. Reconstructed pronunciation: linguists rebuild how a language sounded from its descendants (Coptic for Egyptian, Icelandic for Old Norse), from how foreigners spelled it (Babylonian scribes wrote Egyptian names with vowels), from ancient grammarians (Romans described their own v and c), and from poetry, rhyme and spelling mistakes. We wrote each line in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) following these standard reconstructions.
3. AI voice reading the sounds directly: open-source Piper text-to-speech voices were fed the IPA sounds themselves, so they say exactly what the reconstruction says instead of reading the words in their own language. Each language got a voice from a modern language with similar sounds: Mexican Spanish for Classical Nahuatl, Indonesian for Old Javanese, European Portuguese for Galician-Portuguese, Kazakh for Middle Mongolian, German for Middle High German, Spanish for Old Spanish, Hungarian for Old Hungarian, Ukrainian for Old East Slavic, Georgian for Classical Armenian, Hindi for Pali.
Limits: the modern voice's accent comes through, sounds a voice was never trained on are replaced with the closest one, and these are older forms of languages that are still alive today in a changed form, so nobody speaks them like this anymore; details such as the vowels of Middle Mongolian and the old Slavic short vowels are educated guesses. Think of it as a best guess built on real scholarship.

Texts: the opening of the Nican Mopohua (1649), the motto line from Mpu Tantular's Sutasoma, Martin Codax's Ondas do mar de Vigo, the opening of the Secret History of the Mongols, the opening of the Nibelungenlied, a line from the Cantar de mio Cid, the Funeral Sermon and Prayer, the opening of the Primary Chronicle, the first sentence written in the Armenian alphabet (Proverbs 1:2), and the first verse of the Dhammapada.
