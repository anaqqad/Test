# A German POW Escaped to Detroit. The Man Who Fed Him Was Sentenced to Hang.

<!-- One paragraph = one scene. Lines starting with "##" are act markers and are not narrated. Write numbers as spoken words; years as digits are converted by tools/tts_*.py. -->

## 1. The birthday

On the afternoon of April eighteenth, 1942, a restaurant owner in Detroit told a young man at one of his tables that it was his birthday, and they were going on a pleasure trip. He gave him a necktie and a wallet, bought him a traveling bag, and drove him from bar to bar. At each stop, he introduced his guest as a friend from Milwaukee, or as one of the Meyers boys.

The guest was not from Milwaukee. He was Lieutenant Hans Peter Krug, a bomber pilot of the German air force. A few days earlier, he had been behind barbed wire, in a prisoner of war camp in Canada.

The restaurant owner was Max Stephan, an American citizen for almost seven years. Within four months, a federal judge would sentence him to hang. This is the documented story of the German prisoner who escaped into America, and of the man who spent one weekend helping him.

## 2. The pilot

Krug testified that in August 1940, during the Battle of Britain, he was shot down while flying a bomber over England. In January 1941 he was shipped across the Atlantic to a camp in northern Ontario, and later to a camp for officers at Bowmanville, east of Toronto.

Bowmanville had been a training school for boys. The Canadians ringed it with two barbed wire fences and nine guard towers. For an officer, trying to escape was a duty, and Krug knew it could be done. The year before, another German pilot, Franz von Werra, had jumped from a prison train in Canada, crossed the frozen Saint Lawrence River into the neutral United States, and made it all the way home.

## 3. The escape

In the middle of April 1942, Krug got out. In Toronto, military police stopped him. He showed them forged papers that said he was a carpenter from the crew of the French liner Normandie, and they let him go. A Catholic priest, who believed his stories, bought him a bus ticket to Windsor, across the river from Detroit.

In Windsor, he stole a rowboat and rowed across the Detroit River. At around nine at night, he landed on Belle Isle, the city's island park. He was now inside a country that had been at war with Germany for four months. He spent the night in a doorway, and in a vegetable cart.

## 4. The address

Other prisoners had given Krug an address. Margareta Bertelmann was born in Germany and had never become an American citizen. She knitted for the German prisoners in Canada, and her packages had to carry her name and address.

At about half past nine on Saturday morning, a man in coveralls knocked at her door. He showed her the shoulder boards of a German officer. She let him in, and telephoned a friend: Max Stephan.

## 5. The restaurant owner

Stephan was born in Cologne in 1892. He was wounded in the German army in the First World War, and later became a policeman. In 1928 he moved to Windsor, and in 1933 he crossed to Detroit. In 1935 he became an American citizen, and swore to renounce all allegiance to any foreign state, and particularly to Germany. His restaurant on Jefferson Avenue hosted meetings of the German American Bund, a pro-Nazi group. Stephan denied that he was ever a member.

## 6. The kitchen

Stephan arrived within about a quarter of an hour. Mrs. Bertelmann was frightened. She testified that Stephan told her: what are you shaking for? You're crazy. Krug remembered him sitting down in the kitchen, smiling. She heard Stephan ask the pilot why he did not give up. Krug answered that he had to try.

Then the help began. One of Krug's shoes had a broken sole. Mrs. Bertelmann found him another pair, socks she had darned for her husband, and underwear she had meant to send to the prisoners in Canada. Twenty dollars of hers lay on the table near Stephan. When the men left, it was gone. Stephan walked the pilot out to his car.

## 7. The pleasure trip

At the restaurant, Stephan went in through the back, and told Krug to come in the front, like any customer. Krug ate, and did not pay. Then the escaped enemy officer went for a walk, alone, through downtown Detroit. When he came back, Stephan said: now I have nothing to do, and it is your birthday today, and we will take a pleasure trip.

At Haller's Cafe on Randolph Street, Stephan introduced the pilot as one of the Meyers boys. At the Progressive Club, they had beer. Then they went to the shop of Theodore Donay, an importer. Here Krug gave his real rank, and told the story of his escape, and of his plan to get back to Germany and fly again. Stephan bought him candy. Twenty dollars came out of Donay's cash drawer for Krug. A clerk named Dietrich Rintelen watched the three men talking close together.

## 8. The night

Back at the restaurant, Krug ate dinner and sat in a booth until nine. When a young couple sat down at his table, Stephan pointed him out as a friend from Milwaukee. A waitress offered the pilot a spare room in her house. Stephan said no: he could sleep at the Field Hotel.

At eight the next morning, Sunday, Krug was back at the restaurant door, as arranged. Stephan had looked up the bus schedule. They had breakfast together downtown. Then Stephan bought him a bus ticket to Chicago, and Krug was gone.

## 9. The clerk

The next day, the clerk, Rintelen, found a slip in Donay's cash drawer for twenty dollars and sixty-eight cents. He went to the FBI. On Monday morning, April twentieth, agents arrested Max Stephan. That same day he signed a statement. He admitted he had checked the bus schedules, bought the bag, and bought the ticket.

## 10. The run

Krug was heading for Mexico, which was still neutral. According to one later account, people in Chicago gave him a new suit and a hundred dollars and sent him east, to Philadelphia and New York, where he hoped to stow away on a Swedish ship. When that failed, he turned south: Pittsburgh, Cincinnati, Nashville, Dallas, toward the border crossing at Laredo.

On May first, 1942, twelve days after he left Detroit, Krug was arrested in San Antonio, Texas, about a hundred fifty miles from the border. One account says the woman who ran his hotel heard his accent, and called the FBI. On him, agents found a revolver, a Greyhound bus map, and an atlas of the world at war.

## 11. The charge

Hiding an escaped prisoner was a crime. But the prosecutors wanted more. In June, a grand jury indicted Max Stephan for treason. The Constitution defines treason in one sentence: waging war against the United States, or adhering to their enemies, giving them aid and comfort. And it adds a rule found nowhere else. No one can be convicted unless two witnesses testify to the same overt act. The indictment listed twelve acts, all from that one weekend.

## 12. The witness

The trial opened on June twenty-ninth, 1942, before Judge Arthur Tuttle. The government's main witness was Krug himself, brought from Canada under guard, in the uniform of the German air force. Under the rules of war, he could not be forced to testify. He chose to.

Then, in the afternoon, he turned to the judge. Someone on Stephan's side had told him he was testifying against the man. I don't intend to testify against Stephan, he said. I never can testify against a man who helped me. The judge told him to tell the truth, no matter whom it helped or harmed.

Stephan never took the stand. His lawyer compared him to the Good Samaritan. The prosecutor told the jury it was Max Stephan who tried to get this pilot back to Germany, so he could climb into a bomber and bomb the United States.

## 13. The verdict

Judge Tuttle told the jury that helping Krug out of friendship or sympathy was not treason. They could convict only if Stephan helped him on purpose, to help Germany win the war. On July second, after an hour and twenty-three minutes, the jury found him guilty. By most accounts, he was the first American convicted of treason in a civilian federal court since the Whiskey Rebellion of the seventeen nineties.

On August sixth, Judge Tuttle sentenced him to death by hanging. The life of a traitor, he said, was less valuable than the lives of the country's loyal sons.

## 14. The gallows

Appeals pushed the date back again and again. In February 1943, the Court of Appeals went through the twelve acts one by one. Some had never been proved by two witnesses: the drive to the house, the twenty dollars, the candy. But others had: the walk to the car, the drinks at Haller's Cafe, the dinner, the hotel. The conviction stood. The Supreme Court declined to hear the case.

Some people told the President that Stephan had only felt sorry for a young man far from home. Judge Tuttle answered that there should be no trifling with traitors, and set the hanging for July second, 1943, at the federal prison at Milan, Michigan. A scaffold was built.

## 15. Nine hours

At four fifty-five in the afternoon on July first, about nine hours before the hanging, word reached the prison. President Roosevelt had commuted the sentence to life in prison. He believed death was too harsh.

The White House statement gave Stephan no comfort. The man was properly convicted of treason, it said. He was guilty. The President believed he was pro-German or pro-Nazi, and hoped that none of his successors would ever commute the life sentence.

## 16. What happened to them

Max Stephan spent eight years in the federal penitentiary in Atlanta, and was moved to the prison hospital at Springfield, Missouri. He died there of cancer in January 1952. His wife Agnes was interned, and later deported to Germany. So was Margareta Bertelmann. Theodore Donay was later sent to prison too.

Hans Peter Krug was sent back to Canada. In August 1943 he escaped again, and was caught again. After the war he went home, and sold steel in West Germany.

## 17. Echo

The old boys' school at Bowmanville still stands. In 2013, Canada made it a national historic site. And in the federal law books, the case is still there: one weekend in Detroit, written up as a list. A necktie, a wallet, a bag, two bars, a box of candy, a dinner, a hotel room, breakfast, and a bus ticket to Chicago. Twelve acts, each one sorted by whether two people had seen it.
