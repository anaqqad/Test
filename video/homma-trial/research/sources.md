# Sources: The Trial of Lt. Gen. Masaharu Homma, Manila, 19 Dec 1945 – 3 Apr 1946

Working title (suggestion only): "Japan Expected MacArthur to Forget Bataan. Then the Survivors Testified Against Homma."
Research date: 20 Nov 2026. Free sources only. "No source, no line."

Reliability key used below: **[P]** primary/official record or eyewitness, **[S]** solid secondary (book, government
history, archival study), **[W]** weak or derivative (pop-history site, wiki, repeats another source), **[1x]** found in
only one source, **[M]** comes from a single memoir/oral history/diary (say so in the script: "he later wrote…").

How the sources were read:
- **The trial transcript itself was found and read** (search terms only, not all 3,365 pages): *U.S.A. vs Masaharu Homma*,
  UNWCC microfilm, archive.org item `USMilitaryCommissionRecordsMicrofilm`, Reel 83 (vols. I–XIII, 19 Dec 1945–16 Jan 1946) and
  Reel 84 (vols. XIV–XXX, 17 Jan–11 Feb 1946), case no. 184 [1]. OCR is rough; quotes below were checked in context and
  OCR slips corrected only where obvious ("Honma" → "Homma"). Transcript page numbers are given as "tr. p.".
- 1946 wire stories were read as OCR full texts of US newspapers on archive.org (St. Louis Post-Dispatch, Washington Post,
  Gettysburg Times, Dixon Evening Telegraph, Boston American, Circleville Herald, Cumberland News, Hope Star, Kingston
  Daily Freeman, the AP's own clippings files). Cook 1996 [20] and Herman 2002 [23] were read in full on archive.org.
  Morton [2] was read as the archive.org full text. Lending-only books (MacArthur's *Reminiscences*, Taylor, Norman &
  Norman) were used only through archive.org full-text search snippets: short fragments, marked as such.
- Supreme Court texts: Wikisource raw text and Cornell LII (public domain).

## Editor's notes (20 Nov 2026)

- **Title premise.** "Japan expected MacArthur to forget Bataan" is a WWII Box-style hook, not a sourced fact. The script
  can support it only indirectly: Japan's own cabinet secretary Narahashi said the Japanese people agreed with the verdict
  and "would mourn Homma" [14]; Homma said at the end "Justice is not applicable to the defeated" (diary, via Sides [21],
  [1x]). Do not put "Japan expected…" in narration as a fact; use it as a question the film answers.
- **Fujiko Homma's famous line** now has a verbatim primary source: the transcript, 7 Feb 1946, tr. p. 3281 [1]. Use that
  wording, not the press paraphrases (Disagreements 6).
- **Homma's three key lines** are verbatim from the transcript: "I was not looking for them particularly" (5 Feb, tr. p.
  3079), "I am morally responsible for whatever happened in anything under my command" (6 Feb, tr. p. 3143), "I came to know
  for the first time in the court of such atrocities, and I am ashamed of myself should these atrocities have happened"
  (7 Feb, tr. p. 3199) [1].
- **The verdict took five minutes**: the commission met at 1500 on 11 Feb 1946 and the trial "was concluded" at 1505 [1].
- **Death toll**: say "thousands died on the march itself – historians' estimates run from about 5,000 to more than
  10,000 – and many thousands more in the first weeks at Camp O'Donnell" and attribute any single number
  (Disagreements 1-2). In 1946 the AP used "17,200"; the prosecution "1,200 Americans and 16,000 Filipinos".
- **Witnesses**: build the survivor scenes on men who are both in the transcript and in the press: M/Sgt. James
  Baldassarre (9 Jan), Col. John H. Ball (10 and 14 Jan), Capt. Pedro L. Felix (10 Jan, showed his four bayonet scars),
  Maj. Eduardo T. Vargas (11 Jan, Pantingan), Maj. Achille C. Tisdelle (21 Jan, King's aide), Col. John R. Pugh (21 Jan,
  Wainwright's aide).
- **Execution time**: "just before 1 a.m., 3 April 1946" (AP "about 1 a.m."; UP 12:57 a.m.). It was 2 April in the U.S.
  (Disagreements 9). Homma made no final statement at the post (AP, UP) – do not invent last words.
- **Copyright caution**: Cook 1996, Sides 2007, Connor 2022 are copyrighted magazine articles: one short quote each at
  most, prefer the primary documents they quote (transcript, AP).

## Sources

### PART A: COURT RECORDS, OFFICIAL TEXTS, ARMY HISTORIES

[1] **Record of trial, *United States of America vs. Masaharu Homma*, Military Commission convened by the Commanding
General, U.S. Army Forces Western Pacific, High Commissioner's Residence, Manila, 19 Dec 1945 – 11 Feb 1946. UNWCC
microfilm, case no. 184, Reels 83–84 (Reel 85 holds exhibits, "either illegible or partly legible").** Public domain
(archive.org PD mark). **[P]**
URL: https://archive.org/details/USMilitaryCommissionRecordsMicrofilm (files "(Reel 83) US military trial report 183-184",
"(Reel 84) US military trial report 184 (cont)"; text versions `_djvu.txt`, 3.3 MB and 2.1 MB).
- Size: "The foregoing 3365 pages, contained in Volumes I to XXX inclusive (together with Prosecution Exhibits 1 to 425
  inclusive, Commission Exhibit 1, and Defense Exhibits 'A' to 'Y' inclusive)", certified by Donovan 13 Feb 1946.
- Commission (as listed 11 Feb 1946): "MAJOR GENERAL LEO DONOVAN, GSC, Presiding Officer and Law Member; MAJOR GENERAL
  BASILIO J. VALDES, Philippine Army; BRIGADIER GENERAL ROBERT G. GARD, USA; BRIGADIER GENERAL ARTHUR G. TRUDEAU, USA;
  BRIGADIER GENERAL WARREN H. McNAUGHT, USA". Chief interpreter: Maj. Harry D. Pratt, USMC. Reporters Lorenz H. Winter,
  Mack M. Racklin, S. Cornelia Peveto.
- Appointed by "paragraph 31, Special Orders 176, Headquarters United States Army Forces, Western Pacific, dated 15
  December 1945, as amended by paragraph 39, Special Orders 186 … 28 December 1945".
- Prosecution heard in court: Col. Frank E. Meek (chief), Lt. (later Capt.) Abram Raff, Lt. Schwartz, Col. Lim (Philippine
  Army; Fort Santiago and civilian cases). Defense heard in court: Maj. John H. Skeen Jr. (chief), Capt. George Furness,
  Capt. George Ott, Capt. Frank Coder, Lt. Robert Pelz.
- The charge (read 11 Feb): Homma, "between 8 December 1941 and 15 August 1942 … did unlawfully disregard and fail to
  discharge his duties as such commander to control the operations of the members of his command, permitting them to
  commit brutal atrocities and other high crimes against the People of the United States of America, its allies and
  dependencies, particularly the Philippines". Additional Charge: "on 6 May 1942 unlawfully refuse to grant quarter to
  the armed forces of the United States of America and its allies in Manila Bay".
- Verdict, 11 Feb 1946, 1500 hours: "Upon secret written ballot, two-thirds or more of the members concurring, the
  Commission finds you: Of the Charge: Guilty", with the dates narrowed to "10 December 1941 and 5 August 1942"; "Of the
  Additional Charge: Not Guilty."
- Homma's only statement: "I wish to thank the gentlemen on the Commission for the courteous ways which I have been
  treated all through and during my trial. I thank you very much."
- Sentence: "Upon secret written ballot, two-thirds or more of the members concurring, the Commission sentences you to
  be shot to death with musketry. The Accused will be escorted from the courtroom. The Commission adjourns, sine die."
  "(Whereupon, at 1505 hours, 11 February 1946, the trial was concluded.)"
- Arraignment 19 Dec 1945 ended "at 0850 hours … a recess was taken until 0830 hours, 3 January 1946."
- **Takatsu (3 Jan, 1st witness):** Maj. Gen. Toshimitsu Takatsu put Homma's Balanga headquarters "Approximately 500
  meters east of the road"; on O'Donnell in late May 1942 the dying numbered "About 300 people" a day.
- **Baldassarre (9 Jan, afternoon, tr. pp. ~826–890):** "Master Sergeant James Baldassarre, United States Regular Army";
  born "1894, May 29"; in the Army "Twenty-eight years, two months, and eight days", 21 years in the Philippines;
  captured at "151.2 kilometer post", Lamao, 9 April. Lt. Raff: "here on this large chart I have a map of Bataan"; the
  sergeant was told to "take this pointer and point to the chart". From Mariveles to Balanga: "No, sir; no food, no
  water." Between Balanga and Lubao: bodies – "Many of them; hundreds of them. God knows how many of them!" Identification
  (tr. p. 876): "There is one right in this place now, right now, that I can recognize who was riding in an official car, a
  Japanese official car" – "Lieutenant General Homma"; also in San Fernando, "a Japanese car with some kind of yellow
  sticker on front."
- **Col. John H. Ball (10 Jan, tr. ~pp. 890–930):** lost his helmet when kicked on the march from Lamao, "So I marched
  without any head covering at all until I got to Orani"; a Filipino diving for ditch water was bayoneted by a sentry:
  "This Filipino made a noise — that is one of the things you can't forget — made a noise like a baby crying."
  San Fernando school area: "'Filthy,' describes it."
- **Capt. Pedro L. Felix (10 Jan, tr. ~pp. 1010–1020):** Japanese soldiers "stuck cigarettes into our mouths and lighted
  them for us. But on a given signal … they started bayoneting and beheading us"; "I was bayoneted four times, sir."
  Donovan had him "pull his shirt off": "(The witness rose, removed his shirt and exhibited his back to the Commission.)"
  He named "General Nara" as the general whose car came by just before.
- **Maj. Eduardo T. Vargas (11 Jan, tr. ~pp. 1050–1090):** asked their "last wish", "We asked for cigarettes"; after the
  killing, "I didn't move, and even tried not to breathe so that they wouldn't notice that I was alive." (Pantingan River
  massacre of Filipino officers and NCOs.)
- **Maj. Achille C. Tisdelle (21 Jan, morning, tr. p. 2299 ff.):** "aide-de-camp to Major General King"; "Approximately 43
  per cent of the armed forces on Bataan were ill with malaria, dysentery and/or debilitation"; the best King got from the
  Japanese staff officer was "After all, we are not barbarians!"; at the table an aide "knocked the cigarette out of my
  hand … and said, 'Sit at attention.'"
- **Col. John R. Pugh (21 Jan, afternoon, tr. p. 2364 ff.):** "Colonel, Cavalry", aide-de-camp to Wainwright: "Corregidor
  at that time was a nightmare which I have tried to forget for four years, which I now realize I shall never forget."
  He identified a photo of the 6 May 1942 Wainwright–Homma meeting ("General Homma is the most prominent figure there");
  the truce flag was "about as big as a bedsheet."
- **Homma on the stand (5–7 Feb, vols. XXVI–XXVIII):** tr. p. 3079 (5 Feb): "From the testimony I heard in the court it
  appeared to me that there were many dead bodies lying along the roadside, but I do not see how it could be so, why I did
  not see any bodies lying on the road on my whole trip. However, I was not looking for them particularly, to find
  them." Tr. p. 3143 (6 Feb), to Meek: "General Kawane came to report to me that more than 100 a day were dying" at
  O'Donnell; "I am morally responsible for whatever happened in anything under my command." Tr. p. 3199 (7 Feb): "I came
  to know for the first time in the court of such atrocities, and I am ashamed of myself should these atrocities have
  happened." On King's surrender: "There was no terms." / "Unconditionally, was reported."
- **Fujiko Homma (7 Feb, last defense witness, tr. pp. 3277–3282; examined by Capt. Coder):** spelled "Fuijoko Homma" by
  the reporter. Married "6 October 1926"; children: "One by his former wife, a boy; then I have one girl and one boy.
  Altogether, three children." His views: "The Military Force is a thing which should be used to defend the Mother Land,
  and work in harmony and try to preserve the world peace, and should never be used to invade the other countries."
  "He disagreed with this war from the beginning." "Japan had politicians but no statesmen." **The line (tr. p.
  3281):** "I have come from Tokyo to here and I am proud of the fact that I am the wife of General Homma. I have one
  daughter, and my wish for her is that some day I wish that she will marry a man like my husband, Masaharu Homma."
  Hobbies: reading, poetry, tennis "under the blue sky"; "He does not indulge in hunting or fishing." Meek: "The
  Prosecution has no questions." Skeen: "with the testimony of this witness, the Defense rests."
- **King's sworn statement (Prosecution Exhibit 425, taken Washington 14 Jan 1946, read 7 Feb):** the surrender envoys went
  forward "At about 2:00 a. m., April 9, 1942"; "It must have been close to 10:00 o'clock when I reached Lamao"; on
  treatment of his men: "Finally, he said to me, through the interpreter, 'The Imperial Japanese Army are not
  barbarians.' With that assurance I had to content myself and surrender." Rations: "one-fourth rations"; at surrender
  "two days of issue on that limited basis". On Homma: "I have never seen him in my life so far as I know."
- **Closing, 9 Feb:** Skeen: "This entire case is an indictment not of an individual but of the system and background of
  the Japanese Army and Japanese theory of waging war." … "Should his life be taken the world will have lost a man who
  could do much toward the continuation of peace." Pelz: the 14th Army commander "was damned if he did and was damned if he
  didn't." Meek: "never will there be a blot in human history to compare in my estimation with the Death March. Someone is
  to blame for these marches, and that someone is the Accused in this court today." … "his headquarters during all of that
  time was within 500 yards of that Death March, as these 70,000 Americans and Filipinos dragged themselves day after day
  past his headquarters … If he would have cared to listen he could have heard the screams of the wounded and the dying."
  "water is as free as the air you breathe."

[2] **Louis Morton, *The Fall of the Philippines* (U.S. Army in World War II: The War in the Pacific; Washington: Office of
the Chief of Military History, 1953; CMH Pub 5-2-1), ch. XXV "Surrender", pp. 463–467.** Public domain. **[S-official]**
URL: https://archive.org/details/TheFallOfThePhilippines (full text `_djvu.txt`); CMH: https://history.army.mil/books/wwii/5-2/
- King was responsible "for the fate of the 78,000 men on Bataan"; about 2,000 escaped to Corregidor; "The remainder of
  General King's force of 78,000 was left behind to the tender mercy of the Japanese."
- King expected history to remember him as the commander "who had surrendered the largest force the United States had
  ever lost" (from Col. Alexander's recollections).
- 9 April: King went forward "about 0900, in his last clean uniform"; his party was strafed despite white flags; "two hours
  traveling a distance of about three miles"; met Gen. Nagano at about 1100 near Lamao; then Col. Motoo Nakayama, 14th
  Army senior operations officer, arrived in "a shiny Cadillac".
- Nakayama's first question: "You are General Wainwright?" King "finally agreed at about 1230 to surrender
  unconditionally"; he had left his saber in Manila, so he laid his pistol on the table. Nakayama later: "The surrender
  . . . was accomplished by the voluntary and unconditional surrender of each individual or each unit. The negotiations for
  the cessation of hostilities failed." King and party were "in fact, hostages and not prisoners of war."
- "the horrors and atrocities of the 65-mile 'death march' from Mariveles to San Fernando" – "clubbed, beaten, and
  bayoneted". Morton deliberately does not cover the march: its documents "can be found among the prosecution exhibits
  and in the testimony of the trial of General Homma" (fn. 52; cites Falk's thesis). Morton cites "USA vs. Homma, pp. 2305,
  3143" for the surrender talks.
- Wainwright to MacArthur, 9 April: King "without my knowledge or approval sent a flag of truce"; "Physical exhaustion and
  sickness due to a long period of insufficient food is the real cause of terrible disaster."

[3] ***Homma v. Patterson*, 327 U.S. 759 (decided 11 Feb 1946), with memoranda of Justices Murphy and Rutledge.** Public
domain. **[P]**
URLs: https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Homma_v._Patterson ; https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/327/759
- Order: leave to file for habeas corpus and prohibition denied, certiorari denied "on authority of Application of
  Yamashita, and Yamashita v. Styer, 327 U.S. 1". Counsel: "Captain George W. Ott, JAGD, of Chicago, Ill., for petitioner";
  Solicitor General J. Howard McGrath for respondent. Vote 6-2 (AP [11]).
- Murphy: "This nation's very honor, as well as its hopes for the future, is at stake. Either we conduct such a trial as
  this in the noble spirit and atmosphere of our Constitution or we abandon all pretense to justice, let the ages slip away
  and descend to the level of revengeful blood purges. Apparently the die has been cast in favor of the latter course. But
  I, for one, shall have no part in it, not even through silent acquiescence."
- Murphy: "Hasty, revengeful action is not the American way." … "They are the very life blood of our civilization."
- Murphy: "Today the lives of Yamashita and Homma, leaders of enemy forces vanquished in the field of battle, are taken
  without regard to due process of law. There will be few to protest. But tomorrow the precedent here established can be
  turned against others. A procession of judicial lynchings without due process of law may now follow."
- Murphy: "A nation must not perish because, in the natural frenzy of the aftermath of war, it abandoned its central theme
  of the dignity of the human personality and due process of law." Also: the evidence of guilt "may well be … more direct
  and clear than in the case of General Yamashita".
- Rutledge: the directive "allows forced confessions to be received in evidence"; "These provisions of the directive
  ordering the creation of the commission in my judgment vitiate the entire proceeding." Preparation time "was cut from
  the three weeks given to Yamashita to fifteen days between arraignment and the beginning of trial."
- Rutledge's footnote 4 is a dated chronology: directive 5 Dec 1945; order to Gen. Styer to appoint the commission 6 Dec;
  Homma moved from Japan to Manila 12 Dec; defense counsel appointed 15 Dec; charge served 17 Dec, "containing 47
  specifications … together with a supplemental charge" (6 May 1942, Manila Bay); arraignment and plea of not guilty 19
  Dec; 30-day motion denied; trial began afternoon of 3 Jan 1946, ten-day continuance denied; Skeen's affidavit: "each of
  the 48 specifications requires a detailed investigation and that eighteen days have proved insufficient time to
  accomplish even a small portion of this investigation"; petition to the Philippine Supreme Court 16 Jan, denied 23 Jan
  "without argument"; filed in Washington 7 Feb 1946.
- (Note: the Thomas Paine quotation that Cook [20] attributes to Rutledge comes from Rutledge's *Yamashita* dissent, which
  Homma's incorporates by reference – do not present it as a line written in the Homma memorandum.)

[4] **MacArthur's review and confirmation of the sentence, full text as moved by the AP, Tokyo, 20/21 March 1946.** Read in
the AP's own clippings file and in US papers of 21 March 1946. **[P – OCR damaged in places; only clean sentences quoted]**
URLs: https://archive.org/details/sim_associated-press-clippings-file-europe-disorders_1946-03 ;

- Opening: "I am again confronted with the repugnant duty of passing final judgment on a former adversary in a major
  military campaign. The proceedings show that the defendant lacked the basic firmness of character and moral fortitude
  essential to officers charged with the high command of military forces in the field."
- "No nation can safely trust its martial honor to leaders who do not maintain the universal code which distinguishes
  between those things that are right and those things that are wrong."
- "The savageries which resulted have shocked the world. They have become synonyms of horror and mark the lowest ebb of
  depravity of modern times. There are few parallels in infamy and tragedy with the brutalization of troops who in good

- On the victims (partly garbled in OCR; clean in the AP April story [13]): "Of all fighting men of all time, none deserved
  more the honors of war in their final hour of final agony."

  treatment of defeated opponents will forever shame the memory of the victorious troops. I can find no circumstances of
  extenuation, although I have searched for some instance upon which to base palliation."
- On the dissents: "I have carefully considered the minority views presented by the distinguished Justices of the United
  States Supreme Court". Those who oppose the method "either advocate arbitrariness of process above factual realism or
  who inherently shrink from the stern rigidity of capital punishment." "A strange jurisprudence it would be which, for
  whatever reason, defeated the fundamental purpose of justice—to rectify wrong, to protect right and produce order,
  safety and well-being."

  process ever was freer from prejudice." "the trial was conducted in the unshaded light of truth, the whole truth and
  nothing but the truth."
- Command doctrine: "Soldiers of an army invariably reflect the attitude of their general. The leader is the essence.
  Isolated cases of rapine may well be exceptional, but widespread and continuing abuse can only be the fixed
  responsibility of the highest field authority. The resultant liability is commensurate with the resultant crime."
- The commander "still remains responsible before the bar of universal justice."
- "If this defendant does not deserve his judicial fate, none in jurisdictional history ever did."
- "I approve the finding of guilt and direct the commanding general…" (sentence to be executed; uniform not stripped –
  "Homma, therefore, will die a soldier's death" per the AP story).
- Same text also printed in MacArthur's *A Soldier Speaks* (1965) and *Reminiscences* (1964) [24] (snippets confirm
  "repugnant duty … former adversary in a major military campaign" and "judicial fate").

### PART B: 1946 PRESS (wire reports, read as OCR full text on archive.org)

[9] **AP, Manila, 3–5 Jan 1946** (St. Louis Post-Dispatch 3, 4, 5 Jan). **[P-press]**
URLs: https://archive.org/details/per_st-louis-post-dispatch_1946-01-03_98_120 (and _01-04_98_121, _01-05_98_122)

## Disagreements

1. **How many surrendered on Bataan.** 78,000 on Bataan, ~2,000 escaped to Corregidor (Morton [2]); 76,000 (Falk via
   Herman [23]); 75,000 (Connor [22]); "between seventy and eighty thousand" (Cook [20]); 10,500 Americans + 74,800 Filipinos
   marched (prosecution, AP 9 Jan [10]); 70,000 prisoners (Meek's question to Homma [11], Meek's closing [1]); "more than
   60,000" appeared vs 30,000 expected (Takatsu [9]); plan for 40,000 (Herman [23]); 66,000 Filipino + 12,000 American (Wikipedia
   [27]). **Best:** "about 76,000–78,000 American and Filipino troops; roughly 12,000 Americans" and "the Japanese had planned
   for about half that number".
2. **Deaths on the march.** 5,000–10,000 (Falk via Herman [23]); 600–650 Americans + 5,000–10,000 Filipinos (Falk via [27]);
   ~7,000 (Cook [20]); ~10,000 (Connor [22], Sides [21]); 750 Am + 5,000 Fil (Sides via PBS [26]); 650 Am + 16,500 Fil (VA
   study, PBS [26]); 2,275 Am + 9,000–14,000 Fil (Johnson, PBS); 1,200 Am + 16,000 Fil (prosecution charge 1946 [10], UP
   [14]); "17,200" (AP 1946 [13][14]) – the 1946 figures probably include early O'Donnell deaths. **Best:** "Historians'
   estimates run from about 5,000 to more than 10,000 dead on the march itself; at the trial the prosecution put the toll
   at 17,000." Never give one number as fact.
3. **Deaths at Camp O'Donnell.** ~1,500 Am + 22,000 Fil (Provost Marshal [5], with variants up to 2,200 + 27,000); 27,169
   Filipinos 13 Apr–5 Aug 1942 (camp records, AP [10]); "18,000 … in the first six weeks" (Falk via [23]); "more than 25,000
   by June 2, 1942" (Connor [22]); Tisdelle's 2,400 Am + 29,000 Fil includes march, O'Donnell and Cabanatuan [11]. Daily
   rate: "300" (Takatsu [1][9]), "more than 100 a day" (Kawane's report to Homma, Homma's testimony [1]), "30 to 40
   Americans" daily (Ball [10]). **Best:** "In O'Donnell's first months more than 20,000 men died – about 1,500 Americans
   and well over 20,000 Filipinos."
4. **Distance.** "65-mile" Mariveles–San Fernando (Morton [2], PBS [26]); "some sixty miles" (Cook [20]); 60–69.6 mi
   (Wikipedia [27]); "60 to 120 kilometers" depending on start point (AP/prosecution [10]); Balanga–San Fernando 31 miles
   (Herman [23]). Capas–O'Donnell: "nine-mile walk" (Herman) vs "about 14 km" (Wikipedia). Train San Fernando–Capas: "twenty-
   five miles" (Herman). **Best:** "about 65 miles on foot from Mariveles to San Fernando, then boxcars to Capas, then the
   last miles on foot to Camp O'Donnell."
5. **Homma's headquarters and the road.** "Approximately 500 meters" (Takatsu, transcript [1]); "less than 500 yards" (AP's
   rendering [9]); "500 yards" (Meek's closing [1]); Sides [21] notes the "500 feet" vs "500 yards" mix-up. **Best:** "about
   500 meters – a few minutes' walk – from the road."
6. **Fujiko Homma's line.** Transcript [1]: "I have come from Tokyo to here and I am proud of the fact that I am the wife of
   General Homma. I have one daughter, and my wish for her is that some day I wish that she will marry a man like my husband,
   Masaharu Homma." AP [11]: "…my one wish is that some day she will marry a man like the general." Pelz diary via Cook [20]:
   "I wish that my daughter shall marry such a man as the General." Sides [21]: "Although my husband is in this court as an
   accused, yet I still feel honored that he chose me as his wife" [1x – not in the transcript passage read; may be from
   another statement]. **Best:** the transcript wording (she spoke through interpreters Pratt and Onodera, so even this is
   the interpreter's English).
7. **The MacArthur–Fujiko meeting.** Date: Monday 11 March 1946, Tokyo (AP [12], Connor [22]) vs "on the day of the Supreme
   Court announcement" (Sides [21] as summarized – that was 11 Feb; likely a summary error). Length: 40 minutes (AP).
   Purpose: AP/Furness – "not a plea for mercy", she asked for the trial records and said "my husband would be angry with me
   if I did" beg; MacArthur's *Reminiscences* [24] frames it as a personal plea and "one of the most trying hours of my life";
   Cook [20] quotes a press version "one of the most trying moments". Sides' "Never you mind about my job" exchange is [1x].
   **Best:** "She asked him to read the record again – and, by her own account, did not beg."
8. **Hanging vs firing squad – how the vote worked.** Trudeau (memoir via Cook [20]): hanging "took a unanimous verdict, and
   I would not vote to hang him." The record [1] says both findings and sentence were "Upon secret written ballot, two-thirds
   or more of the members concurring." Both can be true (the rules may have required unanimity for one mode) but this is not
   verified. **Best:** "One general later wrote that he refused to vote for the rope – so Homma would be shot, as a soldier."
9. **Date and time of execution.** 3 April 1946, Manila time: "about 1 a.m." (AP [14]); "12:57 a.m." (UP [14]); "1 a.m."
   (Connor [22]); "Shortly before 1:00 a.m." (Sides [21]); "April 2, 1946" (Cook [20] – U.S. date: AP said "noon Tuesday,
   Eastern Standard Time"). Distance of squad: 15 paces (AP) vs 30 paces (UP); 75 paces from Yamashita's gallows (UP).
   Escort: "A chaplain" (AP) vs "a priest of his own faith" (UP). **Best:** "a few minutes before one in the morning, 3 April
   1946".
10. **Last words.** AP and UP: no final statement at the post [14]. Sides [21]: the evening before, "Please congratulate me
    on the start of a new life" [1x]. The often-quoted lines "I want to go … to the place where Japanese soldiers fell and
    where American soldiers sleep" come from a message to Narahashi weeks earlier [13][14], not from the execution ground.
11. **Number of charges.** "47 specifications … together with a supplemental charge" (Rutledge [3]) = 48 (Skeen affidavit,
    Sides, AP); "47 separate acts" (Connor); "43 counts" (Totani via [27]); "42 counts" (UP [14]); 11 of 48 struck on 21 Jan
    (AP [11]) vs 13 attacked and 3 of them retained (Herman/Hanson [23]). The verdict [1] convicted on the single Charge
    (narrowed to 10 Dec 1941–5 Aug 1942) and acquitted on the Additional Charge (Corregidor quarter). **Best:** "one charge,
    48 specifications; guilty on the charge, not guilty of refusing quarter at Corregidor."
12. **Homma's birth date and age.** 27 Nov 1887 (Wikipedia [27], Commons) vs 28 Jan 1888 (Sides [21]); "57-year-old" in Dec
    1945 (Cook [20]); "62 years old" at death (UP [14], wrong). **Best:** "58 years old".
13. **His British years.** "spent eight years as a military attaché in the United Kingdom" (Wikipedia, uncited) vs "three
    years in England as a military attaché" (Cook [20]); Fujiko: they stayed in London "About a year and a half" [1]; 1918
    attachment to the British Army and Military Cross (Fuller via [27], unchecked); "Oxford" (PBS [26], unverified); "once a
    Cambridge student" (Bombay Chronicle 1946 headline snippet, unverified). **Best:** "trained with the British Army in
    the First World War and served as military attaché in London"; avoid Oxford/Cambridge.
14. **Baldassarre's hometown and age.** New Haven, Conn., 49 (AP [10]); Brooklyn (Cook [20], Sides [21]); born 29 May 1894
    per his own testimony [1] (so 51). **Best:** "a regular-army master sergeant with 28 years' service".
15. **Pantingan survivors.** Capt. Pedro L. Felix: "one of only two survivors of the massacre of 400 Filipino officers"
    (AP [10]); Maj. Eduardo T. Vargas also survived and testified (11 Jan [1]); Cook [20] puts Vargas "the next day" after
    Baldassarre (it was two days later); "more than three hundred" (Cook) vs 400 (AP) vs 350–400 (Wikipedia). **Best:** "several
    hundred Filipino officers and NCOs".
16. **MacArthur's review date.** Released Tokyo 21 March 1946 (AP Brines "Tokyo, March 21") vs dated 20 March (Sides [21];
    Bombay Chronicle snippet "London, March 20"). Time-zone difference. **Best:** "on 21 March 1946".
17. **Homma's children.** Fujiko: one son by his former wife, plus "one girl and one boy" [1]. Daughter named Hisako, 18
    (AP 21 Mar [13]; Cook [20]; Norman index) vs "Michiko" (Kyodo via AP 5 Apr [14]). Son Masahiko (Sides acknowledgements
    snippet). **Best:** "his daughter Hisako".
18. **Who called the court "revenge".** Murphy's "revengeful blood purges" [3]; later historians: D. Clayton James
    "miscarriage of justice", Manchester's "kangaroo court" (both via Connor [22]); Pelz in 1996: "I can't say with certainty
    that injustice was done" [20]. Present both sides.

## Timeline

- 1887, 27 Nov (or 1888, 28 Jan) — Masaharu Homma born on Sado Island [27][21].
- 1926, 6 Oct — marries Fujiko (his second marriage) [1][20].
- 1941, 22 Dec — Homma's 14th Army lands at Lingayen Gulf [1 – Homma testimony; Wikipedia].
- 1942, 9 Apr — King's envoys go forward "about 2:00 a.m."; King surrenders unconditionally to Col. Nakayama about 1230 [1][2].
- 1942, 10–11 Apr — prisoners start from Mariveles (10th) and Bagac (11th) [27]; march to San Fernando, boxcars to Capas, walk
  to Camp O'Donnell [23].
- 1942, 6 May — Corregidor surrenders; Wainwright meets Homma [1 – Pugh][2].
- 1942, Aug — Homma relieved and recalled to Japan [1][11].
- 1944, Jan — the War Department releases escapees' accounts of the march [23].
- 1945, Sept — Homma arrested in Japan [23][27].
- 1945, 5 Dec — MacArthur's directive for the trial issued; 6 Dec order to Gen. Styer [3].
- 1945, 12 Dec — Homma flown to Manila [3]; 15 Dec — commission appointed (SO 176) and defense counsel named [1][3].
- 1945, 17 Dec — charge served; 19 Dec — arraignment, not-guilty plea, recess to 3 Jan [1][3].
- 1946, 3 Jan — trial opens, High Commissioner's Residence, Manila [1].
- 1946, 9 Jan — Baldassarre identifies Homma [1][10]; 10 Jan — Felix shows his scars, Ball weeps [1][10].
- 1946, 14 Jan — Fujiko Homma arrives in Manila [10].
- 1946, 16 Jan — petition to the Philippine Supreme Court; denied 23 Jan [3].
- 1946, 21 Jan — Tisdelle and Pugh; prosecution rests; recess to 28 Jan [1][11].
- 1946, 4 Feb — U.S. Supreme Court rejects Yamashita 6-2 [20][11].
- 1946, 5–7 Feb — Homma testifies [1]; 7 Feb — Fujiko testifies; Homma petition filed in Washington [1][3].
- 1946, 9 Feb — closing arguments [1].
- 1946, 11 Feb — verdict and sentence 1500–1505; Supreme Court denies Homma 6-2 the same day [1][3][11].
- 1946, 23 Feb (approx.) — Yamashita hanged at Los Baños ("nearly six weeks" before 3 Apr, UP [14]).
- 1946, 11 Mar — Fujiko meets MacArthur in Tokyo, 40 minutes [12].
- 1946, 21 Mar — MacArthur confirms: "If this defendant does not deserve his judicial fate…" [4][13].
- 1946, 3 Apr, ~12:57–1:00 a.m. — Homma shot at Los Baños, Laguna [14].
- 1949, 12 Jun — march planners Kawane and Hirano hanged at Sugamo [27].
- 1973 — Hisako Homma places a memorial at the execution site [20].
- 1989 — first Bataan Memorial Death March (NMSU ROTC); at White Sands since 1992 [30].
- 2026, 21 Mar — 37th Bataan Memorial Death March, White Sands Missile Range [30].

## Added by the editor while writing the script (20 Nov 2026)

[31] **Morton [2], context lines.** Half rations ordered 5 January 1942 ("placed on half-rations", ~2,000 calories); ch. XXI
"The Battling Bastards" (the troops' own verse "We're the battling bastards of Bataan"); MacArthur ordered by the President to
leave Corregidor for Australia in March 1942 and his promise to return; Manila occupied by the Japanese at the start of
January 1942. MacArthur's landing on Leyte (October 1944) and Manila retaken early 1945: Robert Ross Smith, *Triumph in the
Philippines* (CMH 1963, used for the Los Baños episode) and the October 1944 United News reel (ARC-39033).

[32] **U.S. Army Signal Corps film ADC-5281, "WW2 POW Interviews: Chaplain Leslie Zimmerman, Alvin James Aubuchon, Alcide
'Bull' Benini…", Sendai POW Camp No. 3 (Hosokura), Japan, 1945, with sound.** Public domain (archive.org, PD mark). **[P – his own
voice]** URL: https://archive.org/details/ADC-5281
- Zimmerman (of Seattle; came to the Philippines in May 1941), transcribed from the soundtrack, 1:29–1:45: "Well, after the
  death march into Bataan, we could stand on almost anything. We have seen men die and not turn a hair. We have seen men
  beaten to death or brutally treated, and we don't think anything about it. We got rather numb to it." Played in the film
  as the original recording.

**Title basis.** "Homma Expected MacArthur's Revenge" rests on Homma's own letter to his defense team on the eve of the
verdict (Pelz papers via Cook [20], [M]): a fair trial "which I know is denied to me from the outset"; and on his diary line
"Justice is not applicable to the defeated" (Sides [21], [1x]). "Then the Bataan Survivors Took the Stand" = 9-21 Jan 1946 [1].
