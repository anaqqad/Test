# Homma Expected MacArthur's Revenge. Then the Bataan Survivors Took the Stand.

## 1. The sergeant with the pointer

Manila, the ninth of January, 1946. The ballroom of the old High Commissioner's Residence has been turned into a courtroom. Five generals sit at a long table under the flags. In front of them, a master sergeant of the United States Army stands beside a large chart of the Bataan peninsula, holding a pointer.

His name is James Baldassarre. He has served twenty-eight years in the Army, twenty-one of them in the Philippines. Four years earlier he walked the road drawn on that chart as a prisoner of war. The prosecutor asks him what he had to eat and drink between Mariveles and Balanga. "No, sir," he answers. "No food, no water."

He describes the bodies he passed between Balanga and Lubao. "Many of them," he says. "Hundreds of them. God knows how many of them." Then he is asked whether he saw any high Japanese officers on that road. And the sergeant turns.

"There is one right in this place now, right now," he says, "that I can recognize, who was riding in an official car." He names him. Lieutenant General Masaharu Homma, the commander who took Bataan. A reporter for the Associated Press wrote that every face in the courtroom turned toward Homma.

This is the documented story of the trial of General Homma: the men who survived the Bataan Death March and came back to face the general whose army marched them, the five generals who judged him, the young lawyers who defended him, the wife who stood up for him, a Supreme Court justice who called it the wrong kind of justice, and General Douglas MacArthur, who had the last word.

Most of what you will hear comes from the trial record itself, more than three thousand pages typed in that ballroom, and from the reporters who sat in it. Where the sources disagree, we will tell you. And where a scene rests on one man's diary or memoir, we will tell you whose.

## 2. The poet general

Masaharu Homma was not the kind of Japanese general American newspapers liked to draw. He was born on Sado Island in 1887. As a young officer in the First World War he was attached to the British Army, and he later served as military attaché in London. He wrote poetry. His wife said he read Galsworthy and Bernard Shaw.

In December 1941 he commanded Japan's Fourteenth Army. On the twenty-second of December his troops came ashore at Lingayen Gulf on Luzon. Within weeks Manila had fallen, and the American and Filipino army under General MacArthur had pulled back into the Bataan peninsula, a finger of jungle and mountains on the western side of Manila Bay.

They held Bataan for three months, on half rations and then on less. Malaria and dysentery spread through the lines. In March, on President Roosevelt's orders, MacArthur left for Australia and promised to return. The men he left behind called themselves the battling bastards of Bataan.

By early April the army on Bataan was finished. Major Achille Tisdelle, aide to the commander there, would later testify that about forty-three per cent of the troops were sick with malaria, dysentery or simple exhaustion. The men had been on quarter rations. There were two days of food left.

## 3. The surrender

The commander on Bataan was Major General Edward King. Before dawn on the ninth of April, 1942, he sent officers forward under a white flag. Later that morning he followed them himself, in his last clean uniform, according to the Army's official history. Japanese planes strafed his car anyway. It took two hours to travel about three miles.

Near the village of Lamao he met Colonel Motoo Nakayama, Homma's senior operations officer, who arrived in a shiny Cadillac. Nakayama's first question was whether King was General Wainwright. He was not. Wainwright commanded all the forces in the Philippines from the island fortress of Corregidor, and Nakayama wanted all of them.

King had come to surrender Bataan only. He had left his sword in Manila, so he laid his pistol on the table. He asked again and again what would happen to his men. In a sworn statement read at Homma's trial, King said the Japanese officer finally told him, through the interpreter: "The Imperial Japanese Army are not barbarians." With that assurance, King said, he had to content himself, and surrender.

At the table, Major Tisdelle reached for a cigarette. A Japanese aide knocked it out of his hand and told him to sit at attention. King and his staff, the Army's historian later wrote, were in fact hostages, not prisoners of war.

General King knew how history would remember him: as the man who surrendered the largest force the United States had ever lost. The Army's official history puts it at seventy-eight thousand men. Roughly twelve thousand of them were Americans. The rest were Filipinos.

## 4. Sixty-five miles

The Japanese plan for prisoners had been written in March, for about forty thousand men. Homma's staff officer Toshimitsu Takatsu later testified that they expected thirty thousand, and were surprised when more than sixty thousand appeared. Most of them were sick, and all of them were hungry.

The plan itself looked simple on paper. The prisoners would gather at Balanga and walk north to San Fernando. From there boxcars would carry them to Capas. Then a last walk would bring them to a Filipino army post called Camp O'Donnell. From Mariveles, at the tip of the peninsula, to San Fernando, it was about sixty-five miles on foot.

What it was like on the road, the trial record tells in the words of the men who walked it. Colonel John Ball, a regular officer with thirty years in the Army, was kicked by a guard and lost his helmet. He marched bareheaded under the April sun all the way to Orani.

Ball saw a Filipino soldier dive toward a ditch for water. A sentry bayoneted him. "This Filipino made a noise," Ball told the court. "That is one of the things you can't forget. Made a noise like a baby crying." The Associated Press reported that the colonel wept on the stand.

Lieutenant Colonel Willard Waterous, an Army doctor, testified that about two thousand Filipino patients from a Bataan hospital were driven onto the road, among them men whose legs had only just been amputated. Technical Sergeant Horace Clark described an American who collapsed by the road and was beheaded with two strokes of a sword.

The Filipino officers had it worst of all. Captain Pedro Felix was one of several hundred Filipino officers and sergeants marched to the Pantingan River. The guards put cigarettes in their mouths and lit them. Then, on a signal, they began bayoneting and beheading them. "I was bayoneted four times, sir," Felix said.

His countryman Major Eduardo Vargas survived the same killing ground. He lay among the bodies. "I didn't move," he told the court, "and even tried not to breathe, so that they wouldn't notice that I was alive."

Three years after Bataan, an American Army film crew met one of the march survivors in a prison camp in northern Japan, at the end of the war. He was a chaplain from Seattle named Leslie Zimmerman. Listen to how he described it, in his own voice, in 1945.

[CLIP pow_interviews_sendai_1945.mp4 89.4 105.0] Well, after the death march into Bataan, we could stand on almost anything. We have seen men die and not turn a hair. We have seen men beaten to death or brutally treated, and we don't think anything about it. We got rather numb to it.

## 5. Camp O'Donnell

The march ended at Camp O'Donnell, and the dying went on. Takatsu told the court that in late May about three hundred people a day were dying there. An Army report written in 1945 counted about fifteen hundred Americans and twenty-two thousand Filipinos dead at O'Donnell. Men collapsed from exhaustion, the report said, and were sometimes buried before they were actually dead.

How many died on the march itself, no one counted. Historians' estimates today run from about five thousand to more than ten thousand men. At the trial the prosecution put the toll of the march and the first camp together at seventeen thousand. You will hear many numbers for Bataan. None of them is exact, and that is part of the crime.

And where was the commanding general? Homma's headquarters was at Balanga, on the route of the march. Takatsu put it about five hundred meters from the road. In August 1942 Homma was relieved of his command and called home to Japan.

For almost two years Americans at home were told almost nothing. Only in January 1944, after a few prisoners escaped, did the War Department release their accounts. That was when the words Death March entered American newspapers.

## 6. The general comes back

MacArthur came back to the Philippines in October 1944. Manila was retaken in early 1945. In August Japan surrendered. In September, Homma was arrested in Japan.

On the fifth of December, 1945, a directive set up a military commission to try him. On the twelfth he was flown to Manila. Five generals were appointed by MacArthur's command to hear the case: Major General Leo Donovan, who presided, Brigadier Generals Robert Gard, Arthur Trudeau and Warren McNaught, and Major General Basilio Valdes of the Philippine Army.

The charge was one charge with forty-eight specifications. It said Homma had failed to control the men under his command and permitted them to commit brutal atrocities. Nobody claimed he had personally killed anyone. The question in front of the commission was a commander's question: what a general owes for what his army does.

His defense was given to six young Army lawyers. The chief was Major John Skeen, a twenty-seven-year-old admiralty lawyer from Baltimore. According to a 1996 history of the trial, Skeen had never argued a case in court. One of his team, Lieutenant Robert Pelz, kept a diary.

Pelz wrote that at their first meeting Homma looked like a tired-out grandfather who had girded his loins for a last battle. The lawyers had fifteen days between the arraignment and the trial. Skeen swore that eighteen days had not been enough to investigate even a small part of forty-eight specifications.

The defense also objected to something else. The man who had ordered the trial, whose command had chosen the court, and who would review its sentence was MacArthur, the general Homma had driven off Luzon. Their motion said no one man should be accuser, prosecutor, judge, jury and court of final appeal. The trial went ahead.

## 7. The ballroom

The trial opened on the third of January, 1946. Its first witness was a Japanese general, Takatsu, Homma's own former staff officer. He told the court he was honor bound not to speak badly of his old commander in his presence. He then described the plan, the numbers, and the deaths at O'Donnell.

Over the next three weeks the prosecution called one hundred and thirty-six witnesses and put in more than three hundred exhibits, most of them sworn statements. Americans and Filipinos, soldiers and civilians, came to the ballroom to testify.

The historian Lawrence Taylor later noted that not one of them tied Homma himself to any particular atrocity. That was not what the prosecution was trying to prove. Its case was that a commander who let such things happen on that scale, on his own road, was responsible for them.

## 8. The witnesses

On the ninth of January came Sergeant Baldassarre and his pointer, and the moment he turned toward the defendant. The reporters wrote that Homma grinned nervously. Outside the courtroom, Baldassarre told a reporter what he thought of the whole proceeding. They should never give him a trial, he said. He never gave us no trial.

The next day it was Captain Felix. The presiding general asked him to take off his shirt. He stood, turned, and showed the five generals his back and the scars of four bayonet wounds. The Associated Press reported that spectators gasped. On the same day Colonel Ball wept as he described the road to Orani.

On the fourteenth of January a Japanese woman in a kimono arrived by plane from Tokyo. She was Fujiko Homma, the general's wife.

On the twenty-first, the last two prosecution witnesses took the stand. One was Major Tisdelle, General King's aide, who had seen the surrender at Lamao. The other was Colonel John Pugh, General Wainwright's aide on Corregidor. Pugh was shown a captured Japanese photograph of Wainwright sitting across a table from Homma on the sixth of May, 1942.

General Homma, Pugh said, was the most prominent figure in the picture. Of those last days on Corregidor he said: "Corregidor at that time was a nightmare which I have tried to forget for four years, which I now realize I shall never forget." With that, the prosecution rested.

## 9. The defense

The defense called Japanese officers first. Homma's chief of staff, Lieutenant General Takeji Wachi, testified that there had been no cases of willful atrocities. A Japanese censor called Homma more of a desk general than a field commander.

Then, on the fifth of February, Homma himself took the stand. He told the court he had been removed in 1942 because of his pro-British leanings and his disagreements with Tojo. He had trained in England, he said, and his time with the British had given him a broad outlook on life.

He was asked about the bodies along the road. He had driven that road, he said, and seen none. "However," he added, "I was not looking for them particularly, to find them."

The next day the chief prosecutor, Colonel Frank Meek, took over. You knew you had seventy thousand prisoners of war at the fall of Bataan? Yes. Did you inquire as to the physical condition of the prisoners? "I did not."

Homma admitted that a general had come to tell him more than a hundred men a day were dying at O'Donnell. And he said a sentence the prosecution would remember. "I am morally responsible for whatever happened in anything under my command."

On the morning of the seventh he finished. "I came to know for the first time in the court of such atrocities," he said, "and I am ashamed of myself, should these atrocities have happened."

## 10. The wife

That afternoon the defense called its last witness. Fujiko Homma, through two interpreters, told the commission she had married the general in 1926. He had disagreed with this war from the beginning, she said. Japan had politicians, he used to say, but no statesmen.

Military force, she said he believed, should be used to defend the homeland and keep the peace, and never to invade other countries. He liked reading, and poetry, and tennis under the blue sky. He did not hunt or fish. Then she said the line that went around the world.

"I have come from Tokyo to here, and I am proud of the fact that I am the wife of General Homma. I have one daughter, and my wish for her is that some day I wish that she will marry a man like my husband, Masaharu Homma."

Lieutenant Pelz wrote in his diary that the general sobbed as she said it, and that all of them had tears in their eyes. Colonel Meek said: "The Prosecution has no questions." Major Skeen announced that, with this witness, the defense rested.

A few minutes later the prosecution read the sworn statement of General King, taken in Washington three weeks before. In it King said of the man on trial: "I have never seen him in my life, so far as I know."

## 11. Closing

The closing arguments were made on the ninth of February. Major Skeen told the commission that the whole case was an indictment, not of an individual, but of the system and background of the Japanese Army. Lieutenant Pelz said the commander of the Fourteenth Army was damned if he did, and damned if he didn't. Homma, in civilian clothes, wept openly as Skeen spoke.

Then Colonel Meek answered. Never would there be a blot in human history, he said, to compare with the Death March. "Someone is to blame for these marches, and that someone is the Accused in this court today."

Meek reminded the court that Homma's headquarters stood a short walk from the road while seventy thousand Americans and Filipinos dragged themselves past it, day after day. If he had cared to listen, Meek said, he could have heard the screams of the wounded and the dying. And water, he said, is as free as the air you breathe.

## 12. Five minutes

On the eleventh of February, 1946, at three in the afternoon, Homma stood before the five generals with his senior counsel and his interpreter. The record shows that the commission found him guilty of the charge, and not guilty of the extra charge that his men had refused quarter on Corregidor.

Homma was allowed to speak. He thanked the gentlemen of the commission for the courtesy with which he had been treated all through his trial. Then General Donovan read the sentence: to be shot to death with musketry. The record notes that at five minutes past three the trial was concluded.

A firing squad, not the rope. One of his lawyers, Captain Frank Coder, told reporters that death before a firing squad is an honorable death. Years later one of the five judges, General Trudeau, wrote that he had thought Homma an outstanding soldier, and that he would not vote to hang him. That is his memory. The trial record says only that two thirds or more of the members agreed.

Major Tisdelle was in the courtroom that afternoon. He told a reporter: "There are twenty-four hundred Americans and twenty-nine thousand Filipinos in this court room, too, but you can't see them."

## 13. The dissent

On the very same day, in Washington, the Supreme Court of the United States refused to hear Homma's appeal, six votes to two. Two justices wrote that it should have been heard.

Justice Frank Murphy wrote: "Either we conduct such a trial as this in the noble spirit and atmosphere of our Constitution, or we abandon all pretense to justice, let the ages slip away, and descend to the level of revengeful blood purges."

Murphy did not say Homma was innocent. He wrote that the evidence against him might well be clearer than in the case of General Yamashita, tried in the same city a few weeks earlier. What troubled him was the method. "Today the lives of Yamashita and Homma, leaders of enemy forces vanquished in the field of battle, are taken without regard to due process of law," he wrote. "But tomorrow the precedent here established can be turned against others."

Justice Wiley Rutledge pointed to the rules the commission had worked under, written for it by MacArthur's command. They allowed forced confessions to be received in evidence. In his judgment, he wrote, those provisions spoiled the entire proceeding.

## 14. Forty minutes in Tokyo

There was one man left who could stop the execution in the Pacific. The sentence had to be reviewed and confirmed by General MacArthur, now ruling occupied Japan from Tokyo.

On the eleventh of March, 1946, Fujiko Homma was received in his office. MacArthur gave her forty minutes. According to the Associated Press, she told him in broken English that it would be a great loss to the world if her husband were executed. But she did not ask for mercy, she said, because her husband would be angry with her if she did.

She asked him to look again at the record of the trial. Captain George Furness, one of the defense lawyers, came with her. He told reporters it was not a plea for mercy. MacArthur, he said, promised to consider the record carefully, and said he understood and sympathized with her position.

MacArthur's own statement to the press called the meeting one of the most trying moments of his life. No incident, he said, could more deeply illustrate the utter evil of war and its dreadful consequences upon those like her, who had no part in it.

## 15. The repugnant duty

Ten days later, on the twenty-first of March, MacArthur's review was released in Tokyo. It began: "I am again confronted with the repugnant duty of passing final judgment on a former adversary in a major military campaign."

The proceedings showed, he wrote, that Homma lacked the basic firmness of character and moral fortitude needed by officers in high command. Of the men who had surrendered on Bataan he wrote: "Of all fighting men of all time, none deserved more the honors of war in their final hour of final agony."

Then he set down the doctrine that would outlive them all. "Soldiers of an army invariably reflect the attitude of their general. The leader is the essence." Isolated crimes might be exceptions, he wrote, but widespread and continuing abuse could only be the responsibility of the highest authority in the field.

He answered the two justices too. No trial, he wrote, could have been fairer than this one. And then the sentence that closed the case. "If this defendant does not deserve his judicial fate, none in jurisdictional history ever did."

MacArthur confirmed the sentence. Homma would keep his uniform and die by a firing squad, a soldier's death, as the Associated Press put it. In Tokyo the chief cabinet secretary said the decision taught the Japanese a grave lesson: a commanding general must answer for the acts of his men, even when he gives no order himself.

Homma's daughter Hisako, eighteen years old, spoke to reporters for her mother, who was ill in bed. "Today's verdict," she said, "is of human making, not of God's."

## 16. Los Baños

Homma had already written to his lawyers, on the eve of the verdict, that they had left no stone unturned to give him a fair trial, which, he wrote, he knew was denied to him from the outset. And he wrote that he respected Americans all the more because of them.

Through the Japanese government he sent one more message home. "I know I cannot escape the death sentence," he wrote. "I want to go, the earlier the better, to the place where Japanese soldiers fell and where American soldiers sleep."

He was taken to Los Baños, south of Manila, where the Americans now held their condemned Japanese prisoners. About six weeks earlier, General Yamashita had been hanged there.

Shortly before one in the morning on the third of April, 1946, Homma walked out between a double rank of guards, a chaplain beside him, his arms bound behind his back. An officer read the charge, the finding and the sentence. A black hood was put over his head and a round white target, four inches across, was pinned over his heart.

He made no final statement. Twelve riflemen stood fifteen paces away. Some of the rifles held blanks. The officer in charge raised his right arm. The Associated Press wrote that Homma died calm and silent to the end. Not far away, an ambulance crew stood waiting.

## 17. What the court left behind

Homma was not the only one. The two officers who had planned the movement of the prisoners, Yoshitake Kawane and Kurataro Hirano, were tried later in Yokohama and hanged in 1949.

Was it justice, or revenge? The argument has never stopped. MacArthur's biographer D. Clayton James called the trial a miscarriage of justice. The survivors had their own answer, the one Sergeant Baldassarre gave outside the courtroom: he never gave us no trial. Robert Pelz, the young lawyer who wept in that ballroom, said fifty years later that he could not say with certainty that injustice was done.

What lasted was the idea at the heart of the case, the one MacArthur wrote down and Japan's own government repeated: that a general answers for what his army does, whether or not he gives the order.

In 1973 Homma's daughter put up a small memorial where her father was shot, and she wrote to one of his American lawyers. In his last letter, she told him, her father had written: Hisako, never forget there were the honorable gentlemen, the lawyers who fought for me at the Manila trial.

## 19. The road today

Every spring, in the desert of New Mexico, soldiers and civilians walk across the White Sands Missile Range in the Bataan Memorial Death March. In March 2026 they walked it for the thirty-seventh time. New Mexico remembers Bataan because of the two hundredth Coast Artillery, its National Guard regiment, about eighteen hundred men strong in 1941. The Army says eight hundred and twenty-nine of them died or went missing.

The trial record survives, all three thousand pages of it, and in it the words of the men who came back from that road to tell five generals what had happened there. As Major Tisdelle said on the day of the sentence, there were thousands more in that courtroom. You just couldn't see them.
