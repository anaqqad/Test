# Sources: "Hitler's Generals in Mississippi. One Escaped Just to Mail a Letter."

No source, no line. Every fact in `script.md` maps to one of the entries below. Where sources disagree, the note says
which one the script follows.

## Why this topic (competitor research, 28 Oct 2026)
YouTube search sorted by views: the "[POWs] were shocked by America" format dominates (WW2 Tales "German POWs Were Shocked By
America's Industrial Might" 1.5M; "When German POWs Reached America…" 622K). "German POW Generals Were Shocked When They
First Saw America" (WW2 PUB) had 263K views in two weeks, with similar videos by Canadians At War (342K) and WW Vanguard
(327K). Many of these channels tell loosely sourced stories; ours is the documented version of the generals topic.

## Main sources
1. **Derek R. Mallett, *Hitler's Generals in America: Nazi POWs and Allied Military Intelligence* (University Press of
   Kentucky, 2013)**, as cited on Wikipedia ("Hermann-Bernhard Ramcke", "Battle for Brest") and summarised in Paul J.
   Springer's review (FPRI, 6 Jan. 2016):
   - 55 German generals were held as POWs in the United States, 1943–46 (review).
   - Americans at first showed little interest: a few generals questioned for weeks, the rest treated like other POWs; the
     British understood the value of interrogating them better (review).
   - Interest rose sharply as the war ended, above all in generals who had fought on the Eastern Front; questioning went
     on into the Cold War (review).
   - Afrika Korps prisoners often stayed loyal to the regime; generals captured after Normandy were more pessimistic;
     generals who doubted Hitler were more likely to get preferential treatment (review).
   - High-ranking POWs combed captured documents and wrote studies; these programs were kept secret because they violated
     the Geneva Convention (review).
   - Ramcke: an American interrogator judged him "an egotistical, conceited Nazi" (Mallett p. 44); a Trent Park report:
     "if there [was] to be such a thing as a list of especially dangerous men to be kept under surveillance [after the
     war], General Ramcke ought to qualify as one of the first candidates" (p. 44).
   - **1 Jan. 1946: Ramcke briefly escaped from Camp Clinton to post letters to Byron Price, director of the Office of
     Censorship, and Senator James Eastland, protesting American propaganda and the withdrawal of tobacco and other
     luxuries (pp. 128–129). After the escape was discovered he was moved to Camp Shelby and held in solitary confinement
     on bread and water for four days (p. 129).**
   - Returned to Europe March 1946, then the London Cage (p. 174); convicted by a French court 21 March 1951, five years
     and six months; released after about three months (p. 175; Hellwinkell 2014, p. 158).
2. **John Ray Skates Jr., "German Prisoners of War in Mississippi 1943–1946," *Mississippi History Now*, MDAH, Sept. 2001**:
   - Tunisia, May 1943: von Arnim captured with 275,000 German and Italian soldiers.
   - A German soldier's diary: surrendered 13 May 1943; a desert "cage"; shipped from Oran; docked at Norfolk, Virginia,
     4 Aug. 1943; prisoners bound for Camp Clinton expected a slow freight train but boarded a comfortable passenger
     train; reached Camp Clinton two days later.
   - Camp Clinton held the highest-ranking German officers; "twenty-five" generals; generals had special housing, lower
     ranks small apartments; von Arnim lived in a house with a car and driver; "some people claimed" he went to movies in
     Jackson because the theater was the only air-conditioned place in town.
   - Camp populations: McCain 7,700, Clinton 3,400, Shelby 5,300, Como.
   - Food: roast pork, potato salad, corn flakes, meat loaf; beer in the canteen; English classes most popular; soccer.
   - Prisoners kept in camps after V-E Day; some went home only in mid-1946. "In a strange way the camps saved their lives."
3. **"Camp Clinton," Jackson *Clarion-Ledger*, 1996 (reprinted on the Hinds County MSGenWeb site)**:
   - Camp Clinton the only U.S. camp holding German general officers; generals in private bungalows, two to each, one
     aide between them; not required to work.
   - Jazz band, theater group, symphony orchestra; more than 20,000 German prisoners in Mississippi.
   - A general sawed through bars in a culvert, checked into the Heidelberg Hotel in uniform so no one would see the PW
     markings, wrote a scathing letter to the State Department on hotel stationery, and returned to camp; no copy survives;
     the general is not named.
   - Prisoners planted bulbs; yellow tulips still bloomed at the front gate (1996).
4. **Wikipedia, "Mississippi River Basin Model"**: proposed 1941 by Eugene Reybold; construction from Jan. 1943; first
   ~200 Afrika Korps POWs Aug. 1943, almost 1,800 by December; 90 cents for 8 hours; officers not required to work;
   about 200 acres; horizontal scale 1:2000; in use 1949–1973; 1952 Missouri River floods, about $65 million in damage
   avoided; City of Jackson took over in 1993, abandoned and overgrown; ASCE National Historic Civil Engineering Landmark
   2018; volunteers clearing it (Buddy Butts Park). MDAH (Skates) adds: hundreds of wheelbarrows and shovels, drainage
   ditches, miniature streets and bridges.
5. **Wikipedia, "Camp Clinton"**: several dozen German generals and admirals, including Hans-Jürgen von Arnim, Ferdinand
   Neuling and Dietrich von Choltitz; about 3,000 POWs, mostly Afrika Korps.
6. **Wikipedia, "Hans-Jürgen von Arnim"**: surrendered 12 May 1943; held at Latimer House and Trent Park; interned with
   24 other German general officers at Camp Clinton; released 1 July 1947; died 1962.
7. **Wikipedia, "Dietrich von Choltitz"**: Hitler's order of 23 Aug. 1944 that Paris must not fall into enemy hands except
   as a field of ruins; explosives placed on bridges; surrendered 25 Aug. to the Free French; memoir claims he saved Paris,
   historians doubt parts of it; Trent Park, later Camp Clinton; released 1947; died 1966.
8. **Wikipedia, "Trent Park"**: hidden microphones ("M" rooms); 84 generals passed through; hospitable treatment, whisky
   rations, walks; more than 1,300 transcripts.
9. **Wikipedia, "Hermann-Bernhard Ramcke"**: paratroop commander on Crete (1941) and in North Africa; the British at Trent Park considered him one of the most vocal Nazi supporters they had met; boasted to von Choltitz about destroying Brest, which Choltitz considered a war crime (Hellwinkell 2014, p. 158).
10. **Wikipedia, "Battle for Brest"**: 7 Aug.–20 Sept. 1944; Hitler ordered Ramcke to fight to the last man; old city razed;
   Ramcke surrendered 19 Sept.; to Brig. Gen. Charles Canham, who answered his request for credentials, "These are my
   credentials"; 38,000 Germans captured.

## Disagreements
- **Generals at Camp Clinton:** 25 (MDAH; von Arnim "with 24 others"), "several dozen" (Wikipedia), 35 of 40 (Clinton
  town site). Script: "dozens"; 55 in the whole U.S. (Mallett).
- **Camp size:** 3,000 (Wikipedia) vs 3,400 (MDAH). Script: "more than three thousand".
- **The escape:** Mallett (letters to Price and Eastland, 1 Jan. 1946) vs the 1996 Clarion-Ledger story (unnamed general,
  Heidelberg Hotel, letter to the State Department). Script tells Mallett's version as fact and the newspaper's as a local
  story, without naming its general.
