# Hitler's Generals in Mississippi. One Escaped Just to Mail a Letter.

## 1. Cold open

On New Year's Day, 1946, a German general slipped out of a prisoner-of-war camp in Mississippi. He was a paratrooper, a veteran of Crete and North Africa, and one of the most committed Nazis the Allies had captured. He was not trying to get home. He was carrying letters.

One was addressed to James Eastland, a United States senator from Mississippi. Another went to Byron Price, the director of the Office of Censorship in Washington. The letters complained about American propaganda in the camps, and about the loss of tobacco and other small comforts. He mailed them. Then the Americans found out.

His name was Hermann-Bernhard Ramcke, and he was one of dozens of German generals held in a small town outside Jackson. This is the documented story of how Hitler's generals ended up in Mississippi, what the Americans wanted from them, and what their men built while they waited.

## 2. Tunisia

It began with a surrender. In May 1943, the German and Italian armies in Tunisia collapsed. About two hundred and seventy-five thousand soldiers were taken prisoner. Their commander, General Hans-Jürgen von Arnim, surrendered on May twelfth.

The most senior prisoners went first to England, to a country house north of London called Trent Park. They were treated almost like guests. There were walks in the grounds and a ration of whisky. And in the rooms where they talked, the British had hidden microphones. By the end of the war, listeners had written more than thirteen hundred transcripts.

Many of the generals were then shipped across the Atlantic. Between 1943 and 1946, fifty-five German generals were held as prisoners in the United States. Most of them ended up in the same place.

## 3. The crossing

Their soldiers crossed too. One young German from the Afrika Korps kept a diary. He surrendered on May thirteenth, 1943, and was held in a barbed-wire enclosure in the desert that the Americans called a cage. Four weeks later, he was put on a ship at Oran.

The ship docked at Norfolk, Virginia, on August fourth. The prisoners bound for Mississippi expected a slow freight train. Instead, they boarded a comfortable passenger train. Two days later, they arrived at Camp Clinton.

Camp Clinton stood a few miles west of Jackson. It held more than three thousand prisoners, most of them from the Afrika Korps. It was one of four large camps in the state, and before the war ended, more than twenty thousand German prisoners would pass through Mississippi.

## 4. The generals' compound

Inside the camp was a separate compound for the generals. They lived in private bungalows, two generals to each, with one aide between them. They were not required to work. Lower-ranking officers made do with small apartments.

Von Arnim, the senior general, lived in a house, with a car and a driver. Some people in Jackson claimed he went to the movies in town, because the theater was the only air-conditioned building around. It may only be a story.

The rest of the camp ate what American soldiers ate. Roast pork, potato salad, corn flakes and meat loaf. There was beer in the canteen, a jazz band, a theater group and a symphony orchestra. The most popular class in camp was English.

## 5. The river

Their men were put to work. The Army Corps of Engineers wanted a better way to predict the Mississippi's floods. It wanted to build a working model of the entire river basin, big enough to reproduce a flood, and it needed labor that the war had taken away.

In August 1943, the first two hundred prisoners from Rommel's Afrika Korps arrived at the site. By December, there were almost eighteen hundred. Enlisted men were paid ninety cents for eight hours of work. Officers could not be made to work at all.

With hundreds of wheelbarrows and shovels, the prisoners dug drainage ditches, shaped the land, and built miniature streets and bridges. The finished model covered about two hundred acres. The river's path was laid out at one two-thousandth of its real size.

It worked. In 1952, the model's Missouri River section helped engineers predict a major flood. By one estimate, it saved about sixty-five million dollars in damage. The men who dug it were long gone by then.

## 6. What the Americans wanted

At first, the Americans did not know what to do with their generals. A few were questioned for a few weeks. The rest were treated much like any other prisoner. The British, with their microphones, had been listening far more closely.

As the war ended, that changed. The Americans grew interested in one kind of general above all: the ones who had fought the Red Army. The Soviet Union was about to become a rival, and these men knew how it fought.

The generals were not all alike. Those captured in Africa in 1943 often stayed loyal to the regime. Those captured after the Normandy landings were far gloomier about Germany's chances. Generals who doubted Hitler were more likely to get better treatment. Some were set to work writing studies for the Americans, in programs kept secret, because they broke the Geneva Convention.

## 7. Paris

One of the men who ended up at Camp Clinton had been in charge of Paris. In August 1944, Hitler ordered General Dietrich von Choltitz that the city must not fall into enemy hands, except as a field of ruins. Explosives were placed on the bridges.

On August twenty-fifth, Choltitz surrendered Paris to the Free French, with the city still standing. In his memoir, he said he had chosen to save it. Historians have been more doubtful about his motives. Either way, he too was later sent to Mississippi.

## 8. Brest

Ramcke had made a different choice. In the summer of 1944, he commanded the German garrison at the French port of Brest. Hitler ordered him to fight to the last man. The siege lasted six weeks, and the old city was razed to the ground.

On September nineteenth, Ramcke surrendered. When he asked the American general Charles Canham to show his credentials, Canham pointed to his soldiers. These are my credentials, he said. Thirty-eight thousand German soldiers were captured at Brest.

An American interrogator called Ramcke an egotistical, conceited Nazi. At Trent Park, the British wrote that if there were ever a list of especially dangerous men to keep under watch after the war, Ramcke should be one of the first names on it. There, he boasted to von Choltitz about destroying Brest. Choltitz thought it was a war crime.

## 9. The letters

By the end of 1945, the war was over, and the generals were still behind the wire in Mississippi. The comforts were shrinking. Tobacco and other small luxuries had been withdrawn.

On January first, 1946, Ramcke got out and posted his complaints to a senator and to Washington's chief censor. When the camp learned what he had done, he was moved to Camp Shelby, near Hattiesburg, and spent four days in solitary confinement on bread and water.

A local newspaper later told the story another way. In its version, a general sawed through the bars of a culvert under the camp, checked into the Heidelberg Hotel in Jackson wearing his own uniform so nobody would see the letters PW, wrote a scathing letter to the State Department on the hotel's stationery, and then went back to camp to sleep. No copy of that letter has ever been found, and the story never named the general.

## 10. Aftermath

In March 1946, Ramcke was sent back to Europe. France put him on trial for the fighting at Brest, on charges that included the execution of civilians and the destruction of their homes. In March 1951, he was convicted and sentenced to five and a half years. He was released after about three months.

Von Arnim was freed in July 1947. Choltitz was released the same year. Many of the ordinary prisoners in Mississippi did not go home until 1946, kept on as laborers long after the fighting stopped. One state historian wrote that in a strange way, the camps had saved their lives.

The river model stayed in use until 1973. The city of Jackson took it over in 1993, but could not afford to maintain it, and it was abandoned to the weeds. In 2018, the engineers' professional society named it a national historic landmark. Volunteers are still clearing it.

## 11. Echo

In 1996, a newspaper reported that the prisoners had once planted bulbs at the camp, and that yellow tulips still came up every spring by the front gate. The bungalows are gone. The generals are gone. The river they never saw finished is still out there, under the trees.
