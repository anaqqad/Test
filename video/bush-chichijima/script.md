# Navy File Shows What George H.W. Bush Really Did in WW2

## 1. The raft

The Pacific Ocean, the second of September, 1944. A few miles off a rocky Japanese island called Chichi Jima, a twenty-year-old American pilot sits alone in a small rubber raft. Minutes earlier he jumped from a burning plane and struck its tail on the way out. He paddles as hard as he can, away from the island, whose guns have just brought his plane down.

Above him, American fighter planes circle low, to keep Japanese boats away. Then, a little before noon, something rises out of the sea a few hundred yards away. First a periscope, then a black hull streaming water. It is an American submarine, the U S S Finback. Sailors run out on the deck, and up on the conning tower, someone is holding a small movie camera.

The submarine's captain recorded the rescue in his patrol report. The page survives in the National Archives. It gives the time, four minutes to noon, the pilot's name, Lieutenant junior grade George H. W. Bush, and one more line. Bush told them that he failed to see his crew's parachutes and believed they had jumped when plane was still over Chichi Jima, or they had gone down with plane.

Two young men had been in that plane with him. They never came home. And on the island behind him, the Japanese garrison was already holding American airmen prisoner. What happened to those men stayed hidden from their families for decades.

This is the documented story of what George Herbert Walker Bush really did in the Second World War, told from his Navy file: the squadron's secret action report, the submarine's patrol report, the pilot's own logbook in his own hand, and the record of a war crimes trial held by the United States Navy on Guam. Where the papers agree, you will see it. Where they disagree, you will see that too.

## 2. The file

A man who later became President has a long paper trail. But the papers that matter here were written in 1944, by men who had no idea who he would become. To them he was one pilot among thousands, file number one seven three, four six four.

The first is Form A C A one, the Aircraft Action Report of Torpedo Squadron Fifty-One for the second of September, 1944, stamped secret, five sheets, typed on the carrier a few days after the mission. The second is the war patrol report of the submarine Finback, signed by her captain. The third is a small brown book with a cloth spine, the Aviator's Flight Log Book, where Bush wrote every flight he made from his first lesson to his last landing.

The fourth file has nothing to do with Bush himself. It is eight hundred and twenty-one pages from the Navy's war crimes trials on Guam, a case called United States against Yoshio Tachibana and others. Bush never appears in it. But it describes the island he was bombing that morning, and what happened there to the men who were not picked up.

## 3. Seaman second class

The story starts on the seventh of December, 1941. Bush was seventeen, a schoolboy at Phillips Academy in Andover, Massachusetts. His family lived in Greenwich, Connecticut. He heard the news on a Sunday walk across the campus. Fifty years later, as President, he stood at Pearl Harbor and told a crowd of veterans what he decided that day.

[CLIP bush_kilo8_1991.mp4 8.5 26.2] Like all American kids back then, I was swept up in it. I decided that very day to go into the Navy, to become a Navy pilot. And so on my eighteenth birthday, June 12, 1942, I was sworn into the Navy as a seaman second class.

Seaman second class is the lowest rank in the Navy. He had signed up for flight training, and within a year the Navy would make him an officer and a pilot.

## 4. Wings

The Navy turned schoolboys into pilots in about a year. Bush started at the preflight school at the University of North Carolina in Chapel Hill. Then came primary training in a biplane, the Stearman. His logbook shows his first solo flight on the fifteenth of December, 1942.

From there he went to Naval Air Station Corpus Christi in Texas. On the ninth of June, 1943, three days before his nineteenth birthday, he was commissioned an ensign in the Naval Reserve and pinned on his wings. He was one of the youngest pilots in the Navy.

He was sent to fly the Grumman T B F Avenger, the Navy's torpedo bomber. He learned it at Fort Lauderdale, Florida. In August 1943 he made his first carrier landings, on the U S S Sable, a training carrier. A stamp in his logbook records it: qualified this date in carrier landings aboard the U S S Sable, the twenty-fourth of August, 1943.

That September he was assigned to a brand-new squadron, Torpedo Squadron Fifty-One, known in Navy shorthand as V T fifty-one. Ensign Bush became its photographic officer.

## 5. San Jacinto

The squadron's ship was the U S S San Jacinto, commissioned at Philadelphia on the fifteenth of November, 1943. She was a light carrier of the Independence class, built on a cruiser hull: fast, narrow, with a short flight deck that made every landing a test.

The Avenger carried three men. The pilot sat up front, alone. Behind him, in a glass ball turret with a fifty-caliber machine gun, sat the gunner. Down in the belly, below and behind, sat the radioman, who also fired a gun out of the tail. The pilot could not see either of them. He talked to them on the intercom.

Bush's regular crew were two enlisted men. His radioman was John Delaney, aviation radioman second class, from Rhode Island, whom everyone called Del. His turret gunner was Leo Nadeau. The three of them flew together for months.

In December 1943, before he left for the Pacific, Bush became engaged to a girl he had met at a Christmas dance two years earlier, Barbara Pierce. He named his planes after her.

## 6. First blood

San Jacinto reached the war in the spring of 1944, as part of the fast carrier task force. Bush's logbook marks his first combat on the twenty-third of May, 1944, in two words: strike, Wake Island.

The same operation took his roommate. Forty-seven years later, at the memorial in Honolulu where the names of the missing are carved in stone, Bush told the story himself.

[CLIP bush_pearl_1991.mp4 10.1 26.1] My roommate aboard the carrier San Jacinto, C V L thirty, was a guy named Jim Wykes, and as we were about to go into combat for the first time, a strike over Wake Island, Jim Wykes and his crew were sent out on a search mission from which they never returned.

In June the carriers moved on the Marianas: Saipan, Tinian, Guam. On the fifteenth of June, Bush wrote in his logbook, in a different ink, Marines invaded Saipan.

## 7. The first ditching

Four days later came the largest carrier battle of the war, the Battle of the Philippine Sea. American fighter pilots called it the Great Marianas Turkey Shoot. For Bush it was the day he lost his first plane.

His logbook entry for the nineteenth of June, 1944, is written in red ink. Avenger number two five one two three, one and a half hours, crew Delaney and Nadeau. Emergency crash landing in sea near Guam. Self and two crewmen picked up by destroyer Bronson.

He set the crippled plane down on the water, and all three men got out. The destroyer U S S Clarence K. Bronson picked them up. For the next four days the logbook has the same two words on each line: sack time.

Then it was back to work: strikes on Guam, on Rota, on the Palau islands. On the twenty-fifth of July, Bush and another pilot were credited with sinking a small Japanese cargo ship near Palau. On the first of August he was promoted to lieutenant junior grade. He had flown dozens of missions. He had already been lucky once.

## 8. Chichi Jima

At the end of August the carriers turned north, toward a group of small volcanic islands six hundred miles south of Tokyo: the Bonin Islands. The Japanese called them Ogasawara. The largest was Chichi Jima, and it mattered for one reason. It had radio stations, and the Japanese used it as a link for communications and supplies to their garrison on Iwo Jima, less than two hundred miles further south.

The island was a fortress. An Army brigade under Major General Yoshio Tachibana dug guns into the hills around the harbor of Futami. The Navy had a base there under Rear Admiral Kunizo Mori. The prosecutor's opening statement at the later trial on Guam records that the first American air raids over Chichi Jima came in June of 1944.

The same record shows that by the time Bush flew over the island, the garrison had already taken American airmen. On or about the fourth of July, 1944, an aviator was captured on Ani Jima, a small island next to Chichi Jima. He was Lloyd Richard Woellhof, aviation radioman second class, from Clay Center, Kansas, the gunner of a dive bomber from the carrier Yorktown.

The record says he was kept in a garage for about a month. Then, on or about the seventh of August, 1944, on orders of General Tachibana, Woellhof and another American, whose name we do not know, were killed with bayonets by Japanese soldiers. Nobody in Bush's squadron knew any of this. To the pilots, Chichi Jima was a target on a chart, with very heavy flak.

## 9. Lifeguard

The Navy did know one thing: pilots shot down near Japanese islands could be saved, if someone was waiting for them. So when the carriers struck, submarines lay off the islands on what was called lifeguard duty.

On the sixteenth of August, 1944, the submarine Finback left Majuro in the Marshall Islands on her tenth war patrol, with orders to stand by off the Bonins. She had a new captain, Lieutenant Commander Robert R. Williams Junior, who had taken command two weeks earlier.

On the first of September, the first day of the strikes, Finback picked up her first airmen: Ensign Thomas Keene and two enlisted men from the carrier Franklin, found in a raft near Chichi Jima. The same day, Bush flew his first mission against the island, with Delaney and Nadeau, and came back safely. His logbook says: strike on Bonin Islands, two point seven hours.

## 10. Strike Baker

On the morning of the second of September, Torpedo Squadron Fifty-One drew a second strike on the island, called Strike Baker. The target was a radio station: four buildings and a tower on the hills above the harbor. Four Avengers would make the attack, each with four five-hundred-pound bombs, escorted by twelve Hellcat fighters and eight Helldiver dive bombers from the carrier Enterprise.

That morning Bush's crew changed. Leo Nadeau, his gunner, stayed on the ship. In his place in the turret was an officer, Lieutenant junior grade William Gardner White, the squadron's ordnance officer, called Ted. He was twenty-six, a Yale man from Minnesota, and he had asked to come along to see the island for himself. Del Delaney took his usual seat as radioman.

According to the action report, the four planes left San Jacinto at a quarter past seven. In the lead was the squadron's commanding officer, Lieutenant Commander D. J. Melvin. Bush was third in line. The weather was good.

## 11. The dive

At about twenty-five minutes past eight by the squadron's clock, the four Avengers reached the island at eight thousand feet and pushed over into their dive, from south to north. The report describes the anti-aircraft fire as very intense, heavy and medium, and of accurate variety.

Melvin and the second pilot put all eight of their bombs on the station. Then the report turns to Bush, and this is the line his whole Navy legend rests on. The report says: Lieutenant junior grade Bush was piloting the third plane over the target. Bush's plane was hit in the engine shortly after final push over at eight thousand feet. In spite of this hit which caused his engine to smoke and catch on fire, Lieutenant junior grade Bush continued in his dive, releasing his bombs on the radio station to score damaging hits.

In other words, with his engine burning, he did not pull out. He held a diving plane steady, through the flak, all the way down to the release point, and dropped his four bombs on the target before he tried to save himself. That is what his Distinguished Flying Cross was for. Its citation, signed that December, says the same: in spite of smoke and flames from the fire in his plane, he continued in his dive.

## 12. Bail out

After the bombs fell, the report says, Bush turned sharply to the east to clear the island, smoke and flames enveloping his engine and spreading aft, his plane losing altitude. He told his skipper on the radio that he had to bail out. He also ordered his crew to bail out. In the language of the day, he told them to hit the silk.

Then the report says something that would be argued over for the rest of his life. At a point about nine miles north-east of the small island of Minami Jima, Bush and one other person were seen to bail out, from about three thousand feet. Bush's chute opened. The chute of the other person, either Lieutenant junior grade White or J. L. Delaney, who bailed out, did not open.

The report adds a careful note in brackets. Bush had not yet been returned to the squadron by the rescue submarine, so this information is incomplete. And it closes with the words every family dreaded: while White and Delaney are reported missing in action, it is believed that both were killed.

Bush himself had hit the tail of his plane as he jumped. He later said he pulled the ripcord too early. He came down in the sea with a cut forehead, swallowed salt water, found the raft, and climbed in.

## 13. Four minutes to noon

From the air, the fighters of Fighting Squadron Twenty watched him paddle, and stayed over his raft to protect him, as the action report says. Somewhere off the island, the Finback got the call.

Her patrol report gives the times in its own time zone. At nine thirty-three, the submarine received word of a plane down nine miles north-east of Minami Jima. She started around the southern end of Chichi Jima, keeping at least seven and a half miles off the coast and its guns.

At four minutes to noon, the boat came up beside the raft. A sailor named Donnet Kohler helped pull the pilot aboard, and someone on the conning tower filmed it. This is that film. It runs less than a minute, and it is the only moving picture of the moment.

How long was Bush in the water? Popular accounts say about four hours. The two reports, read together, suggest it was closer to two and a half. Either way, he was alone, and he was close enough to the island to see it.

The submarine did not leave. Her report says that once Bush was aboard, she commenced a search of the area, on the chance his crew had jumped over water. She found nothing.

## 14. Three versions

So, what does the file really say happened to Ted White and Del Delaney? The honest answer is that the papers of 1944 do not agree with each other.

The squadron report, written from what the other pilots saw, says Bush and one other person jumped, and the second chute failed. The submarine's report, written the same day from Bush's own words, says he never saw any parachutes at all, and believed his crew had jumped over the island, or gone down with the plane.

And there is a third witness. That same afternoon, Finback rescued a fighter pilot under fire off the next island, Haha Jima: Ensign James Beckman, who held on to the periscope while the submarine towed him out to sea. Once aboard, Beckman added a line to the record. The report says it was known that only one man had parachuted from Bush's plane.

Bush's own logbook says the least of all. On the line for the second of September he wrote the plane's number, four six two one four, one point seven hours, the names Delaney and Lieutenant junior grade White, and then: crash landing in sea near Bonin Island, enemy action. It was the standard phrase for a lost plane. Neither body was ever found.

## 15. A month underwater

Finback could not take Bush home. She was on patrol, and she stayed on patrol, with five rescued airmen aboard as passengers. So for about a month, the torpedo bomber pilot lived on a submarine.

He stood watches. He was there when the boat was depth-charged, and when she sank two small Japanese freighters. Forty-eight years later, accepting his party's nomination for President, he described standing watch at four in the morning on the bridge of the Finback, looking out at the dark sea and thinking about friends he had lost.

From the submarine, he wrote to his parents. The letter is quoted in his own collection of papers. He wrote that there was no sign of Del or Ted anywhere around. He said that he sat in his raft and sobbed for a while, and that he felt terribly responsible for their fate.

On the thirtieth of September, 1944, Finback tied up at Midway. A Navy photographer lined up the five rescued airmen with the submarine's officers on deck. Bush is kneeling, second from the left. In the back row stands Ensign Bill Edwards, the officer usually named as the man who filmed the rescue.

## 16. Back to the squadron

From Midway, Bush was flown to Pearl Harbor. Then, instead of going home, he went back to his squadron. He hitched rides across the Pacific, by Johnston Island, Kwajalein, Guam and Ulithi, and rejoined Torpedo Squadron Fifty-One in November 1944.

He flew eight more combat missions, including strikes on Japanese shipping in Manila Bay in the Philippines. By the end of its tour, the squadron had lost about half of its pilots.

In all, his file credits him with fifty-eight combat missions. He was awarded the Distinguished Flying Cross and three Air Medals, and San Jacinto received the Presidential Unit Citation. On the sixth of January, 1945, he married Barbara Pierce in Rye, New York, in his Navy uniform.

He spent the rest of the war training a new torpedo squadron for the invasion of Japan. The last page of totals in his logbook reads: one thousand two hundred and twenty-eight point three hours in the air, and one hundred and twenty-six carrier landings.

## 17. February 1945

In February 1945, the American fleet came back to the Bonins, this time to cover the invasion of Iwo Jima. On the eighteenth of February, carrier planes struck Chichi Jima again, and several were lost.

An Avenger from the carrier Randolph went down near the island. Its pilot, Ensign Floyd Ewing Hall, and his radioman, Marve Mershon, aviation radioman third class, were pulled from the water and taken prisoner. Their gunner, Glenn Frazier, hid on the island for several days before he was caught.

Another Avenger, from the carrier Bennington, was hit by a second American plane in the air. Its pilot ditched and was rescued. His two crewmen came down by parachute on Ani Jima and were captured: Grady York, aviation ordnanceman third class, from Jacksonville, Florida, and James Wesley Dye, radioman third class, from Mount Ephraim, New Jersey.

Then, on the twenty-third of February, a Marine fighter pilot from the Bennington, Second Lieutenant Warren Earl Vaughn of Childress, Texas, was shot down and captured. On the island he was guarded by a young Japanese radio operator who had been born in Hawaii, Nobuaki Iwatake. The two became friends.

## 18. What the record says

The trial record describes what followed in the plain language of a prosecutor, and it is hard to read. These are the facts, without the worst of the detail.

Hall and Mershon were taken to General Tachibana's headquarters and tied to trees. During a bombing raid, two Japanese officers began taking them to an air-raid shelter. The record says that on the way, they were stopped by Major General Tachibana and ordered not to give the prisoners shelter, food nor water.

On or about the twenty-third of February, 1945, Mershon was beheaded. York was put to death with bamboo spears and bayonets. Frazier was beaten to death. Dye was kept alive for a while to help decode messages, and then killed. Hall was executed in March, on the orders of Major Sueo Matoba, a battalion commander. So, in March, was Warren Vaughn.

The trial record also states that after some of the executions, the bodies were cut open, and that flesh was taken from them and eaten by Japanese officers at their headquarters, with sake to drink. Major Matoba ordered it. The prosecution said the general himself had asked for meat at one of those parties.

In all, eight American airmen were captured on and around Chichi Jima and killed there. Not one of them came home. Their families were told only that they were missing.

## 19. The cover story

When the war ended, the island's officers had a story ready. American investigators were told that the captured airmen had been killed in air raids, or sent away to Japan. Major Yoshitaka Horie, the general's chief of staff, later testified that Tachibana had ordered the records on the American flyers destroyed.

The story did not hold. Among the people of the islands were a few families descended from early Western settlers. One of them, Fred Savory, told the Americans what he knew. On the twenty-first of December, 1945, a Marine colonel, Presley Rixey, opened a board of investigation on Chichi Jima. Over the next months he questioned more than a hundred and twenty witnesses.

On the seventh of January, 1946, Major Horie admitted that his first statement had been false. Then Major Matoba testified. He admitted ordering executions, and he admitted taking part in the cannibalism. He said he had been a madman because of the war.

## 20. The trial on Guam

The cases were tried by a United States Navy military commission on Guam, where the Navy held its war crimes trials for the Pacific islands. The main case opened in August 1946, with fourteen defendants, among them General Tachibana, Admiral Mori and Major Matoba.

The charges were murder, violation of the laws and customs of war, and neglect of duty. There were thirty-eight specifications. One problem had no precedent. There was no law, anywhere in international law, against cannibalism. So the Navy lawyers charged it as the prevention of honorable burial.

The prosecutor, Lieutenant Daniel Flynn of the Naval Reserve, explained it in his opening argument. Several of the specifications, he said, allege the commission of acts which are so revolting to the human mind that man long ago decided it unnecessary to legislate directly against their commission.

He went on: there is no doubt in our minds that the series of offenses these defendants have committed rank with the most startling ever to be heard in a criminal court. It is our hope that never again will the like of these cases have to be presented.

It was not a secret trial. In September 1946, Time magazine reported Matoba's confession to the American public. But the Navy did not share the details with the families of the dead men, and the story faded from the newspapers within weeks.

## 21. The gallows

Of the fourteen defendants in the first case, thirteen were found guilty. General Tachibana was sentenced to death. Admiral Mori received a life sentence. He was later tried by a Dutch court for other crimes.

On the twenty-fourth of September, 1947, Yoshio Tachibana was hanged on Guam and buried in an unmarked grave. Matoba was hanged too. The Navy's own newspaper reported that five Japanese had been executed. The sources differ on the other names, so we leave them out.

Two years before, on the third of September, 1945, Tachibana had climbed aboard the American destroyer Dunlap off Chichi Jima and surrendered the Bonin Islands. A Navy cameraman filmed him saluting at the top of the ladder. It was one day after the first anniversary of the morning Bush was shot down.

## 22. The gunner

In 1988, when Bush was running for President, the story of the second of September came back. A former Torpedo Squadron Fifty-One gunner named Chester Mierzejewski told a New York newspaper that he had been in the turret of a plane flying about a hundred feet ahead of Bush's. He said the plane had not been on fire, that he saw only a puff of smoke, and that only one man got out. He believed Bush could have tried to land on the water and save his crew.

Bush's campaign called the claim absurd. Bush himself always said the plane was burning and that he had done what he had to do. In 2003 he told an interviewer that the plane was burning, and that he knew he had done the right thing.

So what does the file show? The two documents written in September 1944 both say the engine was hit and smoking or burning. The action report, written from the other pilots' reports, says smoke and flames. The medal citation says fire. Mierzejewski's account comes from one man, forty-four years later. On the fire, the documents are on Bush's side.

On the parachutes, the file cannot settle it, because its own pages disagree: one other man jumped, no parachutes seen, only one man jumped. Whether Ted White or Del Delaney ever left the plane, no record can say. That is the honest edge of the evidence.

## 23. What became of them

Ted White and John Delaney were officially declared dead on the third of September, 1945. Their names are carved on the Courts of the Missing at the National Memorial Cemetery of the Pacific in Honolulu. Leo Nadeau, the gunner who stayed on the ship that morning, survived the war.

Finback was decommissioned in 1950 and sold for scrap in 1959. San Jacinto was scrapped in 1971. Nobuaki Iwatake, Warren Vaughn's guard and friend, took the first name Warren after the war. He died in 2012.

On the second of September, 1984, forty years to the day, Bush took out his old logbook and had a page signed by the men who were there: Finback officers, the airmen she rescued, and his squadron mates. The signatures are still in the book.

In June 2002, at seventy-eight, Bush went back to Chichi Jima. He saw the ruins of the radio station. He dropped wreaths on the sea for Ted White and Del Delaney, and he met Warren Iwatake. A year later the author James Bradley published the story of the eight captured airmen, and their surviving relatives learned, for the first time, what had happened to them. Most of their parents had died without knowing.

George Herbert Walker Bush died on the thirtieth of November, 2018, at ninety-four.

## 25. The harbor today

Chichi Jima went back to Japan in 1968, after twenty-three years under the United States Navy. Today it belongs to the city of Tokyo. Boats come and go in the harbor of Futami, and in a bay on the island's south side, the wreck of a Japanese wartime cargo ship still lies in the shallows.

Somewhere off the north-east coast, about nine miles from Minami Jima, is the place where an Avenger with three men aboard went into the sea. There is no marker there. There is only the line in the logbook, in the pilot's own hand: crash landing in sea near Bonin Island, enemy action.
