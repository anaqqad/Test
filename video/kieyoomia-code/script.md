# The True Story of the Navajo Code — the Prisoner Japan Tortured to Break It

## 1. The parade ground

A winter day in a Japanese prison camp. A guard opens a cell and marches out an American prisoner, stripped naked. He walks him onto the parade ground and leaves him standing in the snow. The prisoner is told he can go back inside when he tells them the code.

He later said it was twenty-seven degrees, and the snow was about six inches deep. The guard told him that if he moved, he would shoot him. He stood there for an hour. When they finally pushed him back toward his cell, his bare feet had frozen to the ground. In his own words, I left my soles on that parade ground.

The prisoner was Joe Kieyoomia, a Navajo silversmith from the American Southwest, drafted into the United States Army in 1941. The code the Japanese wanted was being spoken on American radios across the Pacific, in his own language. And Joe Kieyoomia could not understand a word of it.

This is the true story of the Navajo code: the twenty-nine young men who built it, the Marines who carried it from Guadalcanal to Iwo Jima, and the one Navajo prisoner the Japanese tried to break it with. Most of what we know about Kieyoomia comes from his own interviews, given fifty years later, and on some points his accounts and the records disagree. We will tell you where.

## 2. A language they were told to forget

To understand this story, you have to start in the classrooms. In the years before the war, many Navajo children were sent to government boarding schools. Chester Nez, who would become one of the first code talkers, was sent away at eight. He remembered that the children were forbidden to speak Navajo at all.

The daughter of another code talker, the poet Laura Tohe, has described how her father's school punished students for using their own language. She calls what happened next a huge contradiction. Another code talker, Thomas Begay, did not learn English until he was thirteen, at boarding school.

In 1942, there were about fifty thousand Navajo people. Their language had no written alphabet of its own. Outside the tribe, very few people on earth could speak it. A Navy fact sheet later put the number of non-Navajos who understood it at the outbreak of war at fewer than thirty. None of them were Japanese.

Joe Kieyoomia grew up in that world. He was born on the Navajo reservation, most likely in Arizona. The year is uncertain: his Army record says 1917, other sources say 1919. In March 1941, at Santa Fe, New Mexico, he was drafted. The Army clerk filled in his card: single, three years of high school, American Indian, citizen. Civilian job: silversmith.

## 3. The two hundredth

Kieyoomia was sent to fill out a New Mexico National Guard regiment, the two hundredth Coast Artillery, an anti-aircraft unit. At Fort Bliss in Texas, draftees doubled it to about eighteen hundred men. Almost all of its men spoke Spanish. That was one reason the Army chose them for the Philippines.

They landed there in September 1941 and set up their guns around Clark Field, north of Manila. Kieyoomia served in the headquarters battery of the second battalion. On the eighth of December, a few hours after Pearl Harbor, Japanese bombers came over Clark Field. The New Mexico gunners fired back. State records still call them the first to fire.

In the weeks after Pearl Harbor, one of the regiment's battalions was split off in Manila to form a second unit, the five hundred and fifteenth Coast Artillery. New Mexico calls it the first American unit born in battle in the Second World War. By the state's count, the New Mexico gunners shot down eighty-six enemy aircraft before the end.

For four months, the Americans and Filipinos held out on the Bataan peninsula, hungry and sick, with no help on the way. On the ninth of April 1942, General Edward King surrendered Bataan. The two hundredth Coast Artillery, Joe Kieyoomia with it, became prisoners of the Japanese Fourteenth Army.

General King is said to have told his men to remember that they had not surrendered. They had no choice but to obey his order. It put the New Mexico regiment, and Joe Kieyoomia, in Japanese hands for the rest of the war.

What followed is known as the Bataan Death March. Around seventy-five thousand prisoners, most of them Filipino and about twelve thousand American, were marched north toward a camp called O'Donnell. Estimates of the dead on the road run from five thousand to eighteen thousand Filipinos and five hundred to six hundred and fifty Americans.

Kieyoomia survived the march. A Navy fact sheet from 1992 notes that about twenty Navajos were serving with the Army in the Philippines. Kieyoomia was one of them. Of the eighteen hundred New Mexicans who went to the Philippines, about half would never come home.

## 4. A missionary's son

Seven thousand miles away, in Los Angeles, a civil engineer named Philip Johnston was reading the newspaper. It was early 1942. The story said the Army had used a few Native American soldiers to send messages in their own languages during training exercises in Louisiana.

Johnston was the son of a missionary. He had grown up on the Navajo reservation near Leupp, Arizona, and learned Navajo playing with Navajo children. As a boy of about nine, he translated for a Navajo delegation that went to Washington to see President Theodore Roosevelt. He had served in France in the First World War. And he knew how hard Navajo was for outsiders.

He took his idea to the Navy, and was sent to the Marines at Camp Elliott, near San Diego. On the twenty-eighth of February 1942, he brought four Navajo men to a demonstration in front of the base's commanding general, Clayton Vogel.

Vogel's staff handed the Navajos six military messages. One read: Enemy expected to make tank and dive bomber attack at dawn. The Navajos passed it along in their language, and it came out the other end as: Enemy tank dive bomber expected to attack this morning. Close enough. In a later test, a three-line message went through in twenty seconds. The coding machines of the day needed about thirty minutes.

On the sixth of March, Vogel wrote to the Commandant of the Marine Corps. His letter survives in the National Archives. It passes on Johnston's claim that German students, posing as art students and anthropologists, had studied every tribal language in the country except Navajo. It says Navajo is completely unintelligible to all other tribes and all other people, except perhaps twenty-eight Americans who had studied it. And then: This dialect is thus equivalent to a secret code to the enemy. Vogel asked for two hundred Navajos. Headquarters approved thirty.

## 5. The first twenty-nine

On the morning of the fourth of May 1942, a group of young Navajo men climbed onto a bus at Fort Defiance, Arizona. At Fort Wingate, New Mexico, they were sworn into the United States Marine Corps. One had dropped out. Twenty-nine went on.

The youngest, it is recorded, was fifteen. The oldest, Carl Gorman, was thirty-five. Chester Nez had kept his enlistment from his family. In San Diego they became Platoon three eighty-two, the first all-Navajo platoon in the history of the Corps.

Nez's story was typical. He was born in 1921 at Chi Chil Tah, New Mexico. His mother died when he was three. At the government school, the teachers gave him a new name, Chester, after President Chester Arthur. He was recruited out of the boarding school at Tuba City, Arizona, the same kind of school where Navajo had been forbidden. Now the Marine Corps wanted him for exactly that.

Look at the dates. The twenty-nine were sworn in on the fourth of May. On the sixth of May, Corregidor surrendered, and the American war in the Philippines was over. Joe Kieyoomia's prisoner of war card gives his date of capture as the seventh of May. In the same week, one group of Navajos began building a secret, and another Navajo became the prisoner who would be tortured for it.

The platoon graduated at the end of June and was sent to Camp Elliott with one job: invent a code that the Japanese could never break, in a language that had never been written down.

## 6. Iron fish

They started with what they knew. The first code had two hundred and eleven words. Instead of inventing new terms, they gave military things the names of things from home.

A tank became chay-da-gahi, the tortoise. A submarine was besh-lo, the iron fish. A dive bomber was gini, the chicken hawk. A fighter plane was da-he-tih-hi, the hummingbird. A battleship was a whale. A destroyer was a shark.

Countries got names too. Britain was the land between waters. The Philippines were the floating island. Germany was the iron hat. And America, in the code, was ne-he-mah: our mother.

For words that had no code term, they spelled. Each English letter was a Navajo word. A could be wol-la-chee, the ant, or be-la-sana, the apple, or tse-nill, the axe. Using several words for each letter made the spelling harder to crack by counting how often a sound came up. The word Navy, for example, could go out as needle, ant, victor, yucca. So even a Navajo speaker listening in would hear words in his own language that meant nothing at all.

That was the double lock on the code. To an outsider, Navajo was a wall of sound. To a Navajo who had not been trained, it was a string of birds, fish and animals with no meaning. Remember that. It is exactly what Joe Kieyoomia would face.

The code grew as the war went on. It started with two hundred and eleven terms and reached more than four hundred. The code talkers learned it by heart. A Navy dictionary of the code survives from June 1945, and you can still read it today.

The spelling alphabet grew too, from twenty-six words to forty-four. As the Marines moved across the Pacific, men from each division met in Hawaii to agree on new words. Speed mattered as much as secrecy. One of the original twenty-nine, Carl Gorman, put it simply. You had to be able to use English and Navajo pretty fast. Everybody was shooting at each other, and you really had to be on the ball.

In one later test, run by a doubtful officer, the code talkers translated, sent and translated back a message in two and a half minutes. Without the code, he was told, the same job could take hours.

## 7. Guadalcanal

In August 1942, the Marines landed on Guadalcanal in the Solomon Islands. The first Navajo code talkers came ashore near Lunga Point in September.

Not every officer trusted them. The Marines' own director of recruiting had already objected to officers in combat depending on an order being translated. In the field, the code talkers had to prove themselves message by message.

They had another problem. In the jungle, some Marines took them for Japanese. Code talker Bill Toledo later described being stopped by his own side. After that, a radio operator named Richard Bonham was assigned to stay with him as a bodyguard. Bonham later said that eventually, all the Navajos had bodyguards.

There is a darker story often told about those bodyguards: that they had orders to shoot a code talker rather than let him be captured. Toledo said he was told so. A law passed by Congress in 2000 repeats it. But Bonham said no direct order was ever given, and the Marine Corps has always denied it. No written order has ever been found, and no code talker was ever shot by his own side.

Meanwhile, Philip Johnston himself had joined the Marines as a staff sergeant at age fifty, to help train new code talkers at Camp Elliott. In June 1943, a magazine article by a government official revealed that Navajos were serving as Marine radiomen. Johnston was reprimanded and threatened with a court-martial. The secret held anyway.

A Navajo communication school opened there, with formal classes from the seventh of December 1942, a year to the day after Pearl Harbor. The Marines asked for hundreds more Navajos, first at fifty a month, then at twenty-five.

## 8. The wrong face

Back in the Philippines, Joe Kieyoomia was a prisoner at Camp O'Donnell. In 1995, he told an Associated Press reporter that he lived on rice laced with weevils. He said he watched weakened Americans being ordered to dig their own graves, and then shot by the guards.

He said that at the end of one week, the guards told him he would be next. A few days later, instead, he was put on a ship to Japan. No record we have found names the ship or the date.

Why him? Kieyoomia believed the Japanese had singled him out because of his face and his name. They thought he was a Japanese American, a traitor fighting for the enemy. He said they beat him for months. In his words: I told them I was Navajo. They didn't believe me. The only thing they understood about Americans was black and white. I guess they didn't know about Indians.

In time, they accepted that he was Navajo. He said that was when things got worse.

## 9. Bird, turtle, water

By then, Japanese radio monitors across the Pacific were hearing something they could not place. Marines were talking on open radio in a language no one in Japanese intelligence could identify. A 1981 history of the code talkers says the Japanese came to suspect a Native American language, and questioned American Indian prisoners about it.

In Japan, they had a Navajo prisoner. In the accounts he gave later, Kieyoomia said he was brought before the camp commander and made to listen to recordings of American radio traffic. He said he could not believe what he was hearing. It sounded like Navajo.

It was Navajo. But it was the code. He heard words he knew, one after another, in an order that made no sense. A history of the code talkers describes it as a completely random jumble of words.

In the 1995 interview he described a different scene. Two Japanese women came to see him. They had written Navajo words in English letters, and they asked him what they meant. So he told them. This means bird, this means turtle, this means water. In the code, a bird could be a plane, a turtle a tank. Without the key, the words were exactly what he said they were.

He told them the truth. I didn't know about the code, he said. They did not believe that either.

## 10. Twenty-seven degrees

What followed, in his accounts, was punishment. In one version, recorded by the author Sally McClain, an interrogator hit him with a club and broke his ribs, then his wrist. He said a British prisoner doctor named Whitfield examined him, but all he could do was bind his ribs and give him aspirin.

Then came the winter day on the parade ground. It was twenty-seven degrees. One hour in the snow, naked, with a guard's rifle on him. When he was finally moved, the skin of his frozen feet tore away. His widow, years later, would put it in five words: he left his soles overseas.

He said the beatings went on almost daily. For a time he could not use his right leg. He said he tried to starve himself, and the guards beat him until he ate. I wanted to die, he said. Many times, I thought I was close.

There is one more detail in the accounts. The broadcasts he was forced to hear also gave him something. He could hear that his own people were out there on the radio, and that American forces were getting closer to Japan.

## 11. Saipan

We are about halfway through Joe Kieyoomia's story. If you want more prisoner of war stories told from the documents, subscribe to WWII Frontlines.

In June 1944, the Marines landed on Saipan in the Marianas. A Marine Corps photograph shows Navajo communication men on Saipan, and its caption says they landed with the first assault waves to hit the beach.

By then there were code talkers in every Marine division in the Pacific, and with the Raiders and the parachute units. A Navy history says they took part in every assault the Marines made in the Pacific from 1942 to 1945. At Bougainville, at Tarawa, at Guam, at Peleliu, the radio traffic of the Marine Corps was passing in Navajo.

Being mistaken for the enemy did not stop. A 1981 history says that at least once, Marines under friendly fire had to put a code talker on the radio before the shelling was lifted. To the men on the other end, a Navajo voice was proof enough.

## 12. Iwo Jima

On the nineteenth of February 1945, the Marines landed on Iwo Jima, an island of black volcanic ash. Major Howard Connor was the signal officer of the Fifth Marine Division.

According to Connor, six Navajo code talkers worked around the clock during the first two days of the battle. They sent and received more than eight hundred messages. Not one of them had an error.

Connor's verdict has been quoted ever since. Were it not for the Navajos, the Marines would never have taken Iwo Jima. A captain's report from the battle called the Navajo code the simplest, fastest and most reliable means of passing a message.

By the end of the war, about four hundred Navajos had served as code talkers. Thirteen were killed in action. One of them was Harry Tsosie, from Rough Rock, Arizona, one of the original twenty-nine.

They were not the only Native Americans in uniform. About forty-four thousand Native Americans served in the Second World War, by the White House count. One history puts the number of Navajos alone at about thirty-six hundred. Choctaw, Chippewa, Creek, Sioux and other men also passed messages in their languages. But the Navajo code was the one built for the whole Marine Corps, in every division in the Pacific.

And Japan never broke the code. After the war, Japan's chief of army intelligence, General Seizo Arisue, said that his men had been able to read codes used by the U.S. Army and the Army Air Forces. The Marines' code, he said, they never cracked.

## 13. Nagasaki

The ninth of August 1945. An American bomber dropped the second atomic bomb on Nagasaki. Where was Joe Kieyoomia?

Here the accounts split. In his 1995 interview, Kieyoomia said he was in a prison cell in Nagasaki when the bomb fell. He said the concrete walls of the cell protected him. He said he was left alone there for three days, until a Japanese officer came and set him free.

But in an earlier interview, published in 1994 by Sally McClain, he said something different. He said he had been moved to a camp he called Matishima, about sixty miles south of Nagasaki, the day before the bomb.

The American records add a third version. Kieyoomia's prisoner of war card lists him, at the end, at a camp in the Tokyo area. That may only be where his paperwork was closed. It does not settle the question.

So we cannot tell you for certain that Joe Kieyoomia lived through the atomic bomb. What we can tell you is that he lived through the war. His card says he returned to American control on the thirteenth of September 1945. By the calendar, he had been a prisoner for about forty-one months. He and his family always counted forty-three, perhaps to the day he got home.

## 14. Coming home

All across Japan that September, American planes and ships came for the prisoners. In Yokohama, Army cameramen filmed them: thin, grinning men in new uniforms, eating, smoking, climbing into the planes that would take them home.

According to the book written with code talker Samuel Holiday, Kieyoomia spent about four years in different veterans' hospitals before he was well enough to go home. His leg recovered. His feet healed. He went back to the reservation, and to silver.

The code talkers came home too, quietly. A National Archives history says they returned on buses, without parades. Their work was still classified. Bill Toledo said they just told him to keep his mouth shut about what he did. So they did. For years, many of their own families did not know.

Of the New Mexico gunners who went to the Philippines in 1941, about nine hundred came back alive. A state history says a third of those died within a year.

## 15. The secret

The Navajo code stayed classified for twenty-three years after the war. It was finally declassified in 1968. In 1971, President Nixon sent the code talkers a letter of thanks. In 1982, President Reagan declared the fourteenth of August National Navajo Code Talkers Day.

Reagan's proclamation went further than any general had. It called theirs the only foolproof, unbreakable code in the history of warfare. In 1976, the code talkers had marched in the nation's bicentennial parade. In 1981, the Navajo chairman and former Marine Peter MacDonald said of them: we can never thank them enough.

Kieyoomia said he only learned about the code talkers years after the war. And there is a story, printed in 1981 and again by the Navy in 1992, about a Navajo soldier who had been a prisoner from Bataan. After the war, he met one of the code talkers. The sources don't give his name. But what he said fits Joe Kieyoomia exactly: I never figured out what you guys who got me into all that trouble were saying.

He never blamed them. In 1995, sitting outside his home on the reservation in northwestern New Mexico, with his twelve service medals, he told the reporter: I salute the code talkers. And even if I knew about their code, I wouldn't tell the Japanese.

## 16. Silver

For the rest of his life, Joe Kieyoomia was a silversmith. In 1983, a National Geographic writer who came through the Shiprock area wrote that Kieyoomia agreed to show him his craft. He was listed for years in a directory of Native American artists: Joe L. Kieyoomia, Navajo, jewelry, Shiprock, New Mexico.

He was married to his wife Lita for forty-two years. They had eight children, and by the end there were fourteen grandchildren. He belonged to the veterans' post in Aztec, and to the association of the survivors of Bataan and Corregidor.

Joe Kieyoomia died on the seventeenth of February 1997, at the hospital in Shiprock. The Farmington paper gave his age as eighty-four, which does not match his Army card either. He was buried in Farmington, with a military service at the grave.

In 2002, at the dedication of the Bataan Memorial in Albuquerque, Lita Kieyoomia spoke about her husband. He left his soles overseas, she said.

## 17. Our mother

In December 2000, Congress passed a law to honor the code talkers. The original twenty-nine would receive the Congressional Gold Medal. Every man who had qualified as a code talker after them would receive a silver one.

On the twenty-sixth of July 2001, in the Rotunda of the Capitol, President George W. Bush presented the gold medals. By then, only five of the twenty-nine were alive, and four of them were able to come: John Brown, Chester Nez, Lloyd Oliver and Allen Dale June. Joe Palmer was represented by his son.

The President told the room that the code word for America had been our mother. He said they had given their country a service only they could give. And he said that at home, they had carried for decades the secret of their own heroism.

When John Brown spoke, he used a few words of Navajo. Then he added: Maybe Japan is listening.

Chester Nez, the last of the original twenty-nine, died in 2014 at the age of ninety-three. In an interview in 2002, he had summed up the war in one sentence. The Japanese pulled out all their hair, he said, but they never could break it.

As of August 2026, the Associated Press counts only two Navajo code talkers still alive: Thomas Begay, who is one hundred and one, and Peter MacDonald, who joined the Marines at fifteen.

Their story is still being argued over and remembered. In 2025, pages about the code talkers were removed from Defense Department websites, and then put back, with the Pentagon saying they had been erroneously removed. In August 2026, plans were announced for a code talkers museum in Farmington, New Mexico, the town where Joe Kieyoomia is buried.

## 19. The rock

On the high desert of northwestern New Mexico, a great dark rock rises out of the plain. In English it is called Shiprock. It gave its name to the town where Joe Kieyoomia made his silver, and where he died.

The language spoken on that land was once punished in its schools. In the war, it became the one code the enemy never broke. And of all the people who heard it during the war, the one who could have helped Japan the most was a Navajo prisoner standing in the snow, who knew every word, and told them the truth: that he did not understand it either.
