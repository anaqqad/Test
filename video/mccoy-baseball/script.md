# Japanese POWs Were Told to Die Before Capture. Then a Wisconsin Camp Handed Them Baseball Gloves.

<!-- One paragraph = one scene. Lines starting with "##" are act markers and are not narrated. -->

## 1. Cold open

On the morning after Pearl Harbor, a young Japanese naval officer woke up on a beach in Hawaii and found an American rifle pointed at his face. His name was Kazuo Sakamaki. The day before, he had tried to steer a midget submarine into the harbor. The submarine had failed. So had the charge he set to destroy it. He had jumped into the sea expecting to die, and the sea had thrown him back.

He asked his captors for one thing. He asked to be allowed to kill himself. They refused.

Ten young men had gone into Pearl Harbor in those five midget submarines. Japan would soon celebrate nine of them as heroes who had died for the Emperor. There was no place on that list for the tenth.

## 2. The rule and the question

Sakamaki had been taught a simple rule. The Japanese military field code told every soldier that rather than live and bear the shame of imprisonment by the enemy, he should die. A soldier was expected to save his last bullet for himself.

The rule worked. In the Burma campaign, Allied forces counted seventeen thousand four hundred and sixty-six Japanese dead and only one hundred and forty-two prisoners. Across the whole war, the United States held about five thousand four hundred Japanese prisoners on its own soil. So what do you do with men who believe they should already be dead? In Wisconsin, the answer would turn out to include a baseball diamond.

## 3. The journey

Sakamaki spent his first weeks as a prisoner in a wire enclosure on Sand Island, in Honolulu harbor. He later called it a birdcage, about ten feet by ten. He thought constantly about suicide.

On February twenty-ninth, nineteen forty-two, he was shipped to the mainland, held briefly at Angel Island in San Francisco Bay, and put on a train. Nobody told him where he was going. When he finally stepped down, in March, the air was cold and unfamiliar, and he had to ask where he was.

He was at Camp McCoy, near the small town of Sparta, Wisconsin. For the next six months, while German and Italian prisoners poured into Allied camps by the thousands, Sakamaki was the only Japanese military prisoner in American hands. In July nineteen forty-two, nine more arrived. By the summer of nineteen forty-five, there would be more than two thousand seven hundred.

## 4. The contradiction

Japanese soldiers had been told that capture meant torture and humiliation. What they found at McCoy was harder to explain. Steamed rice was served at least once a day. There was soy sauce with every meal. The Red Cross supplied chopsticks. The prisoners were allowed to plant their own vegetable gardens.

None of this made the shame go away. Sakamaki later wrote that their desire for suicide was thwarted on every hand. There were no knives and no ropes. Some men banged their heads against walls. Some refused to eat. We wanted to die, he wrote, and yet we could not die.

The Americans discovered that the shame could also be used. Prisoners were allowed to write home every week, but the Japanese almost never did. A letter would tell their families they were alive, which meant they had been captured. So when a Japanese prisoner caused trouble, the guards had a quiet threat. They could write to his family themselves. It usually ended the trouble.

## 5. The gloves

The camp commander was Lieutenant Colonel Horace Rogers. His approach was to keep the men busy. At first, many Japanese prisoners refused to take part in anything. Then boredom won.

They built a stage in each compound. They held concerts, poetry contests and, above all, plays. Up to three productions a month, with costumes and scenery made by hand. Rogers had one condition. He wanted to be allowed to watch, and he came to as many performances as he could. Without tools for wood, the prisoners made toys out of cigarette paper glued together with rice water.

Then the YMCA donated baseball equipment. Baseball became the prisoners' favourite outdoor activity. Teams formed in the two Japanese compounds, played a regular season, and met in a camp championship series. When inspectors toured McCoy in September nineteen forty-five, they reported more athletic equipment than at any other camp they had seen.

On Saturdays, the prisoners were marched outside the wire to watch American films. The War Department had expected the Japanese to seize any chance to break out. On the way to the movies, they never tried.

## 6. The complications

McCoy was not a holiday camp. There were double fences, guard towers with machine guns, and military police with war dogs. The Japanese prisoners complained constantly, and their spokesman was none other than Kazuo Sakamaki. They did not want to wash laundry where American women worked. They wanted coal instead of wood. On one morning in May nineteen forty-four, the enlisted men staged a sit-down strike on their officers' orders. Rogers sent in military police with fixed bayonets. The men went to work.

And some still ran. Across the whole country, Japanese prisoners made fourteen escape attempts. Every one of them was from Camp McCoy, and not one succeeded. In May nineteen forty-five, three young prisoners dug under a fence with a stolen Texaco road map. A farmer spotted one of them a week later, poling down the Mississippi River on a homemade raft. The other two were caught walking along a highway, still wearing uniforms with the letters P W stencilled in bright yellow on the front and back.

In July, another escapee knocked at a farmhouse door and rubbed his stomach to show he was hungry. The farm wife gave him bread and ran to the telephone. When the highway patrol arrived, he was still waiting politely at the back door for the next course.

The last Japanese prisoner to escape was Yuzo Ohashi, captured on Iwo Jima. He believed Mexico was about three hundred miles south of the camp. He walked for four days. He was caught on September second, nineteen forty-five, the day Japan formally surrendered. He told his guards that Japan had clearly lost, and that he could not go home in shame. It was escape, he said, or suicide.

## 7. The last fear

That was exactly what American officials feared. As the war ended, they expected mass suicides among the Japanese prisoners. A Japanese language newspaper in Denver even predicted one at Camp McCoy.

It never happened. Before the prisoners were sent home, Japanese American counselors spoke to them about the value of their lives. They told them that Japan itself had surrendered, and that no man needed to die to restore his family's honor. There were no suicides on the ships back to Japan.

## 8. Resolution

Kazuo Sakamaki went home in nineteen forty-six. Japan had treated him as if he had never existed. He became a lifelong pacifist, joined Toyota, and in nineteen sixty-nine became president of its Brazilian subsidiary. For decades he refused to talk about the war. He finally spoke in public in nineteen ninety-one, at a conference in Texas, half a century after he had asked his captors for permission to die.

In nineteen sixty-six, another former prisoner, Yasuichiro Hayashi, asked through the Japanese embassy whether he might visit Camp McCoy again. For him, he wrote, that place signified a second starting point in his life.

## 9. Echo close

The field code had told these men that a prisoner was already dead. In the middle of Wisconsin, some of them found out otherwise. Someone handed them a bat and a glove, and told them it was their turn.
