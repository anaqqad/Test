# Sources: "Japanese POWs Were Told to Die Before Capture. Then a Wisconsin Camp Handed Them Baseball Gloves."

## Main sources
1. **Brandon Jeffrey Scott, *The Untold Story of Camp McCoy: Japanese Prisoners of War in the Heart of Wisconsin During
   the Second World War*** (University of Wisconsin, 2010), https://minds.wisc.edu/items/d2d8d36d-8c11-4584-b8b1-62236841071c.
   It draws on State Department / War Department / Red Cross / YMCA inspection reports (RG 389), the Fort McCoy
   Historical Center and the Sparta Herald. Used for:
   - Sand Island "birdcage" (10 × 10 ft), about 2.5 months; transfer on Feb. 29, 1942; Angel Island; train; arrival at McCoy on Mar. 9, 1942;
     "cold, unwelcoming"; asked where he was.
   - Japanese POW totals on 1 Aug 1945: McCoy 2,762, the largest (PMGO semi-monthly report, Tab 116).
   - Burma: 142 captured vs. 17,466 dead (via Krammer).
   - Steamed rice at least once a day, soy sauce with all meals, chopsticks via the Red Cross, garden plots (Cardinaux / Schnyder reports).
   - Stages, concerts, poetry, up to three plays a month; Lt. Col. Horace I. Rogers asked to attend; paper toys from cigarette
     paper and rice water.
   - Baseball equipment donated by the YMCA; baseball was the favourite outdoor activity; teams in Compounds 1 and 2 with a regular
     season and a camp championship series; the Sept. 1945 inspection found more athletic equipment than at any other camp.
   - Saturday films outside the compound; the Japanese never attempted escape on the outings.
   - Letters seldom written home (shame); the threat to write to families used as discipline (via Krammer).
   - Double fences, 7 towers, MPs of the 667th, 12 war dogs.
   - Strike of May 31, 1944; MPs with fixed bayonets.
   - Fourteen escape attempts nationwide, all from Camp McCoy, none successful (Fournier, 2003).
   - Classes by Japanese American counselors before repatriation; no suicides on the ships home.
   - Letter of Sept. 21, 1966 from Yasuichiro Hayashi: "a second starting point in my life."
   - Note: the thesis wrongly says the surrender was signed on August 15 aboard USS Missouri. The signing was on Sept. 2, 1945; we do not use that passage.
2. **Arnold Krammer, "Japanese Prisoners of War in America," *Pacific Historical Review* 52, no. 1 (Feb. 1983): 67–91.**
   Internet Archive copy: https://archive.org/details/JapanesePrisonersOfWarInAmerica. Used for:
   - 5,424 Japanese POWs held in the U.S. (from PMGO records, RG 389).
   - Sakamaki captured at Waimanalo Beach; the only Japanese military captive for six months after Feb. 29, 1942;
     joined by nine others in July 1942.
   - Military Field Code: "rather than live and bear the shame of imprisonment by the enemy, he should die…"; save the last round.
   - Sakamaki as the Japanese spokesman at McCoy; complaints (laundry with American women, coal instead of wood…).
   - Sakamaki quote: "Our desire for suicide, however, was thwarted on every hand… We had no knives… no ropes… Some of us banged
     our heads… some men refused to eat. And yet we did not die… We wanted to die and yet we could not die." (I Attacked Pearl Harbor, 81–82)
   - Escape of May 22, 1945 (Nakamura, Tanaka, Hashimoto): Texaco road map, raft on the Mississippi near Prairie du Chien,
     two caught near West Salem in uniforms with "PW" stencilled in bright yellow (Monroe County Democrat, May 24, 1945; Sparta Herald, June 4, 1945).
   - Early July 1945: an escapee asks for food at a farm and waits "politely at the back door for the next course" (Sparta Herald, July 9, 1945).
   - Yuzo Ohashi (Iwo Jima), escaped Aug. 29, 1945, thought Mexico was about 300 miles south, caught Sept. 2, 1945 at Cashton: "escape … or suicide."
   - Rocky Shimpo (Denver) predicted mass suicide at McCoy; none occurred.
3. **Kazuo Sakamaki, *I Attacked Pearl Harbor*** (New York: Association Press, 1949), as quoted in 1 and 2.
4. **Wikipedia, "Kazuo Sakamaki"**: captured by Cpl. David Akui; asked permission to commit suicide (refused); name struck from records;
   repatriated 1946; pacifist; Toyota, president of Toyota do Brasil 1969; silent until the 1991 conference at the National Museum of
   the Pacific War, Fredericksburg, Texas; died Nov. 29, 1999.
5. The "nine war gods" (the nine dead crewmen of the five midget submarines honoured in Japan; the tenth, Sakamaki, omitted):
   Ulrich Straus, *The Anguish of Surrender* (2003); also Krammer and Wikipedia.

## Title note
"Baseball Gloves": the sources say "baseball equipment" donated by the YMCA, and that baseball became the favourite outdoor activity
with a camp championship. Gloves are standard baseball equipment; the script itself only says "baseball equipment".
