# German POWs Came From a Blacked-Out Continent. Then One Rode Into Chicago at Night.

<!-- One paragraph = one scene. Lines starting with "##" are act markers and are not narrated. -->

## 1. Cold open

Early one morning in September 1945, a German prisoner of war sat on an American bus with three dollars and seventy-five cents in his pocket. He had walked out of a guarded camp a few hours earlier. Nobody on the bus knew it. He wore ordinary clothes, he spoke good English, and he kept his face turned toward the window.

For six years, the cities he knew had gone dark at night. Since the first day of the war, German streets had been blacked out against bombers. Windows were covered. Streetlamps were hooded or switched off. A lit window could bring a fine, or worse.

Then the road reached the edge of Chicago. He later wrote that a million lights popped up everywhere.

## 2. Witness and thesis

His name was Reinhold Pabel. He told the story ten years later in a memoir called Enemies Are Human. A single lit skyline proves very little on its own. But Pabel had been watching America through windows for almost two years, and that night was the end of a long argument he had been having with himself.

## 3. Backstory

Pabel was born in Hamburg during the First World War. He studied, travelled, and wanted to become a pastor. In 1940 he was drafted into the army. He fought in the invasion of Russia, was wounded in the first winter, and in 1943 was sent south to Sicily and then to the Italian mainland.

Near the Volturno River, his squad stumbled into an American aid station. Pabel spoke English. He started talking with a lieutenant from Dallas named Paul Lindsey. For a few minutes, two enemies compared notes on the war. Soon after, Pabel was badly wounded jumping a dam under fire. He crawled back to the same Americans, and their medics saved his life.

By the end of November 1943 he was in a prisoner of war camp in North Africa. Then came a ship across the Atlantic. On the morning of January 2nd, 1944, the prisoners went ashore at Norfolk, Virginia.

## 4. The first window

The guards put them on a train heading west. Pabel wrote that, crossing Virginia and Kentucky, the men pressed their noses against the windowpanes. The first thing they noticed was the abundance of automobiles everywhere. Then the frame houses, mile after mile of them.

When a porter came through the carriage with coffee and sandwiches, and offered them politely, Pabel wrote that most of the men forgot, for a while, the anti-American feelings they had brought with them.

Their destination was Camp Grant, near Rockford, Illinois. It was Pabel's first permanent camp: barracks, wire, watch towers, and a library. He took language courses. He read American newspapers. Later he was chosen to translate and read out the daily news to the other prisoners.

## 5. The contradiction

The news from home was getting worse. Hamburg had been struck by a series of raids in the summer of 1943 that started a firestorm and killed tens of thousands of people. Pabel received a telegram saying his family had been bombed out again. It was the second time.

In Germany, darkness was not a mood. It was the law. Blackout rules had come into force on the first day of the war in 1939, and the night was for sirens, cellars and searchlights. Men who had lived under those rules for years learned to notice light.

America had its own restrictions. In 1942 the coastal cities were dimmed so that ships offshore would not be silhouetted for German submarines. In early 1945 a national dim-out to save fuel switched off neon signs and dimmed street lighting across the country.

But a dim-out was not a blackout. Nobody in Illinois was sheltering from bombers. And on V-E Day, May 8th, 1945, even the dim-out ended.

## 6. The decision

The war in Europe was over, but the prisoners were not going home. After May, men at Camp Ellis, where Pabel had been moved, were told to volunteer for work. In the first week of September 1945, he was sent to a small branch camp at Washington, Illinois, where a cannery needed hands for the corn season.

It was not much of a fortress. Pabel noticed that only one watch tower had been put up. Peoria was nineteen miles away, and from Peoria a bus line ran to Chicago. He asked to switch from the noon shift to the night shift, saying he did not feel well. During the change of shifts, he walked away.

An Associated Press report dated September 11th carried the headline, War Prisoner Flees Camp Near Peoria. The camp commander announced the escape. By then Pabel was already gone.

## 7. The ride

A driver gave him a lift on the highway to Peoria. At the bus depot on the public square, he waited, trying not to look like a man who had never bought a ticket in America. At two o'clock in the morning he asked for one ticket to Chicago. It cost more than half of everything he had. While he waited, he ate eggs and drank coffee. He called it his first meal in liberty.

He wrote that with every mile the coach ate up, he came one mile closer to his new life. Fields and small towns passed in the dark. Then came the suburbs, and the lights.

In Hamburg, a city that size at night would have been a target. Here it was simply a city. Nobody was hiding anything from the sky.

Downtown, Pabel saw brightly coloured lights and window displays. He later described the giant Merchandise Mart, with its thousand lights reflected in the Chicago River. So this is my new home, he wrote. Bewildered, he got off the bus on Randolph Street.

## 8. The complication

Freedom did not make him safe. An escaped prisoner had no papers, no history and no right to a job. Pabel took a new name, Phil Brick, short and forgettable. He washed dishes in Chicago restaurants, one of them on North Clark Street, and saved what he could.

Most escapes ended in days. The FBI published advice on how fugitives were caught, and Pabel had read it in the camp library. He tried to avoid every mistake it described. He saved enough to buy a used bookshop on the North Side, the Chicago Book Mart. He married an American woman named Avis. They had a child.

On the morning of March 9th, 1953, a customer walked into the shop and asked for Reinhold Pabel. The customer was an FBI agent. After almost eight years, the escape was over.

## 9. Resolution

The case became national news. The government wanted him deported, but there was a problem. Pabel had not entered the country illegally. The United States Army had brought him in. Old friends spoke up for him, among them Paul Lindsey, the lieutenant from the aid station, now a lawyer. Pabel left for Germany, waited about six months, and then returned legally to his wife, his child and his bookshop.

In his memoir he insisted that propaganda of any kind, German or American, does harm. What changed his mind was not a pamphlet. It was coffee from a porter, a library, and the view from a bus window.

## 10. Echo close

A blackout can hide a city from bombers. It can also hide a country from its own soldiers. Reinhold Pabel came from a country that had spent six years in the dark. At the edge of Chicago, a million lights popped up everywhere, and nobody bothered to cover them.
