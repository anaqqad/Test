# Japanese POWs Were Given Baseball Bats. At 2 A.M., They Charged the Guns.

## 1. The bugle
A few minutes before two o'clock on the morning of August fifth, 1944, it was freezing in the hills of New South Wales, in Australia. Inside a prisoner of war camp outside the country town of Cowra, more than a thousand Japanese prisoners lay awake in their huts, fully dressed.

Then a man ran out of the dark toward the gate of the Japanese compound, shouting at the sentries. Nobody outside the wire understood what he was shouting. A guard fired his rifle into the air. And somewhere in the compound, a single bugle sounded.

Two guards came out of their hut in the dark. Ben Hardy was forty-five years old. Ralph Jones was forty-three. They pulled their army greatcoats on over their pyjamas and ran for a machine gun that stood on a trailer beside the wire.

Behind them, hundreds of men were already coming over the fence. They carried knives from the mess hall, clubs studded with nails, and baseball bats.

This is the documented story of the Cowra breakout: the largest prison break of the Second World War, told from the Australian official history, from the findings of the military court of inquiry, from the citation that gave two guards the George Cross, and from the recorded words of the men who were there, guards and prisoners.

The man who blew that bugle had been living at Cowra for nineteen months under a name that was not his. To understand why, we have to go back two and a half years, to a beach on an island off the north coast of Australia.

## 2. The rule
In January 1941, the Japanese Army issued a code of conduct for every soldier, the Field Service Code. One line in it became famous. In the government's own English translation, it reads: "If alive, do not suffer the disgrace of becoming a prisoner."

The code belonged to the army, but the idea ran through the whole of the Japanese forces. A soldier who was captured had not just lost a fight. He had shamed his name, his family and his village. Many Japanese prisoners preferred that their families believe they were dead.

So Japanese prisoners were rare. By August 1944, Australia held two thousand two hundred and twenty-three of them, and five hundred and forty-four of those were merchant seamen, not soldiers. In the same camps there were more than fourteen thousand Italian prisoners.

The Australian official history set down the contrast plainly. Most of the twenty-one thousand Australian prisoners in Japanese hands were under-fed and over-worked, and many were dying. The Japanese prisoners in Australian camps, it says, were well fed, living in comfortable quarters, and thriving.

## 3. The first prisoner
On the morning of February nineteenth, 1942, Japanese carrier planes and bombers attacked the port of Darwin, in Australia's far north. It was the first air raid on Darwin. Bombs fell on the harbour, the town and the airfield.

One of the fighter pilots that morning was a young naval airman named Hajime Toyoshima. He flew a Zero from the aircraft carrier Hiryu. Over Darwin his plane was hit. With his engine losing oil pressure, he could not get back to the carrier.

He came down on Melville Island, north of Darwin, home of the Tiwi people. The crash landing threw him forward into his own gun breeches and his instrument panel, and his face was badly cut.

He wandered through the bush. According to the Tiwi account, he walked into a camp of Tiwi women and picked up a baby. Not far away, a Tiwi hunting party was out.

## 4. Stick 'em up
The man who caught him was a Tiwi man named Matthias Ulungura. Years later, his account was written down in his own words.

"I see him through the bushes and I go first," he said. "I told the other men to run away and then I sneak up quick and wait behind a tree." When the pilot walked past, Ulungura followed him, grabbed his wrist near the gun, and took his revolver. Then, he said, he walked backwards, pointing the pistol, and told him: "Stick 'em up."

Ulungura kept watch over his prisoner all night, and then handed him over to an Australian army sergeant named Leslie Powell. Powell was unarmed. So, the Australian War Memorial records, he marched the pilot into captivity with the pilot's own service pistol.

The Zero lay on Melville Island. It was the first intact Zero captured by the Allies. And its pilot was the first Japanese prisoner of war taken on Australian soil.

## 5. A false name
When the Australians questioned him, the pilot did not give his real name. He said he was Tadao Minami. Even his story was invented: the records the Memorial holds give two different versions of who he claimed to be and what he had flown.

He was not the only one. A Japanese survivor of Cowra, Marekuni Takahara, told the Memorial in 2004 that he had made up his false name in Darwin, and had told the Australians he was a steward on a fishing boat. "Many of us had false names," he said, "and we just didn't talk about the past."

A false name meant that no letter would reach his family, and no list would carry his name to Japan. As far as his village was concerned, Hajime Toyoshima could have died a hero over Darwin.

Under the name Minami, he went south. He was sent to a camp at Loveday, in South Australia, in March 1942, then to Hay, in New South Wales, that April. On January ninth, 1943, he reached a camp outside the town of Cowra.

## 6. Cowra
Cowra was the centre of a farming district in the central west of New South Wales. The camp stood three kilometres north-east of the town. It had been built in 1941 for Italian prisoners.

Its official name was Number Twelve Prisoner of War Compound. From the air, it looked like a wheel. The whole compound was an octagon about eight hundred yards across, cut into four camps by two roads that crossed in the middle. Each camp was fenced with thick barbed-wire entanglements about eight feet high.

The prisoners and guards called the long road through the middle Broadway. At night, six observation towers, each thirty feet high, swept the camps with their lights, and fixed lamps lit the wire and the roads.

Italians lived in one part of the compound. Camp B, one quarter of the wheel, held Japanese soldiers and sailors, the corporals, sergeants and privates. Their officers were kept in a separate camp across Broadway. By August 1944, there were one thousand one hundred and four Japanese prisoners at Cowra.

## 7. The guards
The camp was guarded by the twenty-second Garrison Battalion. Garrison battalions were for men who could not be sent to the front: men who were too old, or not fit enough. Many had fought in the First World War.

Charles Shepherd had been classed as fit only for base or garrison duty because, at his medical examination, he was found to be blind in his left eye. He was thirty-one, married, with three daughters.

Ben Hardy was a motor mechanic from Willoughby, in Sydney, born in 1898. He was a fine shot. He had been champion of his local rifle club, and he was known as an authority on the Vickers machine gun.

Ralph Jones had been born in Gorleston, on the English coast, in 1900, and had come to Australia in the 1920s. He was a labourer from the town of Crookwell. As a young soldier after the First World War, he had served with the army of occupation on the Rhine.

## 8. Rifles and two Vickers guns
A guard at Cowra, Sergeant Ashley Cooper, later described how they were armed. They carried point three-oh-three rifles. They had been shown how to work a Bren gun, a Sten gun and an Owen gun, but, he said, "we had no practical experience on a rifle range whatsoever."

On the edge of the Japanese compound stood two four-wheeled trailers, each carrying a Vickers machine gun. One of them, Cooper said, would become the gun of Hardy and Jones.

Most of the guards did not believe the Japanese would ever attack them. "We'd never ever thought they'd even try it," Cooper said. "It was such a ridiculous thought on our part."

## 9. Baseball
Life in Camp B was comfortable. A military court of inquiry later found that the Japanese were housed and fed on the same scale as Australian troops, and that they had made no complaint about their treatment.

The official history says that the prisoners grew stronger on their rations, and on "constant wrestling and baseball". They made their own equipment. A survivor, Masayoshi Yamada, remembered that they took apart their leather boots to make baseball gloves.

Another survivor, Teruo Murakami, later called camp life almost heavenly. Yamada said they lived very comfortably, with up to five cigarettes a day.

But comfort did not mean peace. A major of the garrison battalion, the novelist Edward Timms, wrote after the war that the prisoners read the Australians' humane treatment as a sign of weakness. In his words, they believed it "sprang from our secret fear of them."

## 10. The warning
In June 1944, two months before the breakout, a Korean prisoner told the camp's intelligence officer that he had heard Japanese prisoners talking about a plan: to attack the garrison, seize weapons and ammunition, and escape.

According to a newspaper account published decades later, the report was passed to the officer in charge of the Japanese compound, who dismissed it as nonsense.

The official history says that early in August, the camp's headquarters received word that the Japanese were discussing a mass outbreak. Extra precautions were taken. And the Australians made a decision that would set everything in motion.

The camp was overcrowded. The army decided to move the Japanese privates out of Cowra, to the big prisoner of war camp at Hay, far out on the plains to the west.

## 11. Twenty-four hours
The Geneva Convention required that prisoners be given twenty-four hours' notice of a move. So on Friday, August fourth, the Japanese camp leader was told that all the Japanese at Cowra, except the officers and the non-commissioned officers, would leave for Hay on the seventh.

To the Australians it was a transfer. To the men in Camp B it was something else. In the Japanese forces, the bond between the sergeants and their privates was often compared to that between older and younger brothers. The move would split them apart.

The Japanese leaders objected. One of the men who led the camp was a sergeant major named Akira Kanazawa. The Australian War Memorial records one order he gave that night: Australian soldiers were to be attacked, but no prisoner was to harm a civilian.

## 12. The vote
That night, the leaders met in secret, and the court of inquiry would later find that the attack followed a meeting held in the camp at midnight.

Then the men were asked to vote. According to survivors, the prisoners were split into small groups, and each man was handed a scrap of paper. One account says it was toilet paper. A circle meant yes. A cross meant no.

Teruo Murakami was twenty-three. Decades later he told the Memorial: "I said I voted yes, but to be honest, I didn't think it was a good idea. I wanted to vote no." Years after that, he told a reporter more simply: "I drew a circle."

Masayoshi Yamada said he had felt that he wanted to cling to life. But once the decision was made, he said, "I felt, okay, I come along with you." A Japanese survivor interviewed for a documentary in 2021 said there was a mood in which they had to say yes when they actually wanted to say otherwise.

The circles won. The breakout would begin that night, at two o'clock, at the sound of a bugle.

## 13. Bats, knives and blankets
The men had been preparing for a long time. According to the court of inquiry, they had readied extra clothing, gloves and padding to get through the wire. Some wore their homemade baseball mitts to protect their hands.

The official history lists their weapons: knives, baseball bats, clubs studded with nails and hooks, wire stilettos and garrotting cords. The blankets were for the barbed wire. They would throw them over the fence and climb across.

Yamada carried a baseball bat. He said it was not to attack anyone. He and a friend had made a pact. If one of them was shot and close to death, the other would strike him on the head, so that he would die quickly.

Marekuni Takahara was told that the signal would come at two. He was in the first group. His task was to cross Broadway and reach the Japanese officers' camp on the other side.

Murakami said he joined believing he would not see the end of it. Most of the men in Camp B expected to die before morning. That, a historian at the Australian War Memorial has said, was the point. They knew they would not succeed. It was the attempt that mattered.

## 14. Two o'clock
At about two in the morning, a Japanese prisoner ran to the camp gates and shouted what seemed to be a warning. One account describes him as weeping, and in great fear. The guard on Broadway, Private Alfred Rolls, fired two shots into the air, the signal for trouble.

Then the bugle sounded. It was blown, according to the Australian War Memorial, by the prisoner the camp knew as Tadao Minami: Hajime Toyoshima, the pilot from Darwin.

With a cry of Banzai, three great mobs of prisoners broke out of their huts and ran at the wire, one on the north side, one on the west and one on the south. Some crawled under it. Many, a Memorial historian said, simply threw themselves onto the wire so that others could climb over their bodies.

Behind them, men who stayed in the camp set fire to the huts. Eighteen of the twenty sleeping huts burned to the ground.

## 15. "The bastards have broken"
Sergeant Cooper woke to a terrific noise: machine guns going flat out, pistols going off, flares going up into the sky. "I thought, oh, the bastards have broke," he said, "and with that I dived into my trousers and jacket, over my pyjamas."

He could see the huts burning in the Japanese compound. It was, he said, like a big bonfire. The floodlights short-circuited. In the dark, he said, the prisoners used their own dead to walk over the wire.

Most of the guards who were off duty had gone to bed fully clothed, with their rifles and fifty rounds beside them. Now they ran out to reinforce the sentries.

Private Charles Shepherd came out of the guardroom and was stabbed in the chest. He died there. He was thirty-one years old.

## 16. The gun
The strongest group of prisoners, about four hundred men, broke through the wire on the north-west side. That was where Hardy and Jones had their gun.

The two men had pushed their way through the prisoners to reach it. Hardy fired. Jones fed the ammunition belt. They kept the Vickers firing into the mass of men coming at them, until they were overwhelmed.

Hardy was clubbed to death. Jones was stabbed. The prisoners took the gun and swung it round to fire on the Australians' huts. It would not fire.

Accounts differ on why. The official history says only that it jammed. Harry Gordon, the Australian journalist who spent years on the breakout, thought the Japanese had probably forgotten to swing the ammunition belt round with the gun. But the guards believed something else. Cooper said: "I think it was Hardy that took the bolt out of the gun", so that it could not be fired. The Australian War Memorial says that Hardy's last act was to make the gun unusable.

At dawn, Cooper saw the gun. In front of it, he said, the dead were piled in a half circle, three to four feet high. "Just like a pile of firewood," he said, "with dead bodies."

## 17. Broadway
According to the official history, not one Japanese prisoner got across the road that ran through the middle of the compound. The fire there was so heavy that more than two hundred men took shelter in a deep ditch at its side.

Takahara reached Broadway with the first group. By the time he got there, he said, there were already about thirty bodies lying on the road. A bullet grazed him and hit the man behind him in the heart. He died instantly.

Yamada and Murakami both ended up lying in the side ditch. They stayed there until the sun came up. Then, Murakami remembered, an Australian came and spoke to them in Japanese. "We will not harm you," he said, "but you have to come back. Get out of the ditch."

The shooting went on, Cooper said, from two o'clock until about half past five, until dawn. To him, it passed like the snap of a finger.

## 18. Inside the wire
When it was light, the Australians walked into Camp B. Almost every hut was ash. Inside the burned huts they found the bodies of twelve men who had died in the fire.

The court of inquiry counted how the prisoners had died. Twenty had died by hanging or strangling, done by their own hands or by each other. Nine had stabbed themselves. Two had lain down under a train. Most of the rest had been shot.

Murakami remembered the fire. "We had a big fire and put blankets in to make it big," he said. "We wanted to burn everything."

Just outside the wire, in a ditch, lay the body of the bugler. The record of his grave gives the cause of death as a gunshot wound to the chest and a self-inflicted wound. He was buried two days later, under the name Tadao Minami.

## 19. Into the country
Several hundred prisoners had got clear of the camp, into the dark farmland around Cowra. Camp guards, soldiers from a training camp nearby and the local police went out after them.

The official history says that in this phase the Japanese offered no resistance. A police constable from the village of Woodstock arrested eight of them by himself.

Many did not wait to be found. Some hanged themselves from trees. At about seven in the morning, Cooper said, two men lay down on the railway line between Blayney and Cowra.

## 20. The rock
That evening, a young lieutenant named Harry Doncaster led a party of trainee soldiers to search the hills north of the town. Some of the men had been in the army only a few weeks, so they carried no rifles.

At dusk they came upon a group of escaped prisoners. One of them stood on a large rock, holding a knife. With no better weapon, Doncaster picked up a rock. The trainees, by their own admission at the inquiry, lost their nerve and ran.

Doncaster was killed. When he was found, the bodies of ten Japanese prisoners lay around him. They had died by their own hands.

The people of Cowra heard about the breakout at seven o'clock that Saturday morning, five hours after it began. Hundreds of escaped prisoners were loose in the farmland around the town.

Whether because of Kanazawa's order or not, no civilian was hurt during the breakout, and the newspapers reported that no home was broken into.

## 22. Tea and scones
On a farm outside Cowra that day, May Weir was making scones. Her daughter Margaret later told the Memorial what happened next.

The farm's mechanic, Stan Bradtke, came down the path with three Japanese prisoners in their maroon prison clothes. "What do they want, Stan?" Mrs Weir asked. "They want food, Mrs Weir," he said.

"Well, just take them round to the side verandah," she said, "and I'll be with you in a minute. I'll just finish making these scones."

Margaret was sent to call the military. When she came back, her mother was pouring the prisoners tea. They were wearing old felt hats which the family later suspected they had found in the shearing shed. The three men ate for about ten minutes. Then they got up, and thanked her.

One of those three men would come back to that farm, forty years later. And the family would still have the jug.

## 23. Nine days
The search went on for nine days. In the end, three hundred and thirty-four prisoners were brought back to the camp. Twenty-five of them came back dead, most by their own hands.

The total was terrible. The court of inquiry counted two hundred and thirty-one Japanese dead and one hundred and eight wounded. Three more later died of their wounds, which is why the official history gives two hundred and thirty-four.

Four Australians had been killed: Hardy, Jones, Shepherd and Doncaster. A fifth, Sergeant Thomas Hancock of the Volunteer Defence Corps, was hit by a comrade's rifle that went off by accident during the search, and died nine days later.

It was the largest prison break of the Second World War. And it was the only land battle of the war fought on Australian soil.

## 24. The inquiry
A military court of inquiry sat from August seventh to fifteenth and heard more than sixty witnesses.

On September eighth, Prime Minister John Curtin read its findings to the House of Representatives. He did not name the camp. He said that over nine hundred Japanese prisoners of war in a camp in Australia had made an unprovoked mass attack upon their guards.

The court found that the prisoners had been treated according to the Geneva Convention, and that the attack was characterized by "a suicidal disregard of life". Its conclusion was that the responsibility rested entirely on the prisoners themselves, and that it had been their intention to engage in suicidal combat with their guards.

## 25. Secrecy
The government kept the details quiet. Newspapers were not allowed to print the number of dead. In Australia the event became known simply as the Cowra incident. When Sydney's Daily Telegraph printed more than the censors allowed, a file was opened on their breach of the rules.

The reason was the twenty-one thousand Australians in Japanese camps. The authorities feared that if the story reached Japan in the wrong form, Australian prisoners would pay for it. Cooper said that he once stopped a soldier who wanted to shoot a group of captured Japanese, by reminding him of the Australians in Japanese hands.

The Australian government sent its report through Switzerland, the neutral protecting power, to the Japanese government. Japan's own archives hold the file. Its heading reads: protest to Australia.

## 26. A medal six years late
The secrecy lasted long after the war. It was not until September first, 1950, that the London Gazette announced two posthumous awards of the George Cross, Britain's highest award for gallantry not in the face of the enemy.

The citation reads that Hardy and Jones "stood their ground and continued to work the gun until bashed to death, displaying outstanding gallantry and devotion to duty in their fight against an overwhelming onslaught of fanatical Japanese."

Ben Hardy's widowed mother died just days before the award was announced. His sister Beatrice received the cross in Sydney in 1952. Ralph Jones's brother Walter received his from King George the Sixth, at Buckingham Palace, on behalf of their mother.

Because Cowra was not counted as a war zone, his widow, Linda, was refused a war widow's pension. She could not support their three daughters, and the girls were made wards of the state.

## 27. Going home
The surviving prisoners were split up and sent to the camps at Hay and at Murchison, in Victoria. In February and March 1946, they were shipped home to a defeated Japan.

They did not go home as heroes. Many went home under their false names. A Japanese filmmaker, Yasuhiro Mitsuda, who interviewed survivors, said the men believed they would have become heroes by dying, but were only ordinary men because they had survived. According to a New South Wales museum, half of them never told their families that they had been prisoners at all.

One survivor, Seiichiro Tachibana, had Hansen's disease, leprosy, and had been kept in a tent of his own at Cowra. He knew nothing of the plan. He kept his false name for the rest of his life.

Takahara built a new life. Later he would joke that he was a graduate of Cowra University.

## 28. The name on the grave
The dead were buried at Cowra in August 1944, next to the cemetery where the four Australians lay.

One of the Japanese graves was marked with the name Tadao Minami. In the 1980s, Japanese and Australian researchers established who he really was: Hajime Toyoshima, of the carrier Hiryu, the first Japanese prisoner taken on Australian soil.

His bugle survived. It had been made in London by the instrument makers Boosey and Hawkes, in the 1930s. How it reached a Japanese prisoner camp in New South Wales is still a mystery. Major Timms took it home and hung it in his sitting room in Sydney. After his death, his widow gave it to the Australian War Memorial, where it is today.

## 29. The graves
In 1952, the local branch of the Returned Services League, the old soldiers of Cowra, began to look after the Japanese graves.

In 1964, on the twentieth anniversary of the breakout, the Japanese ambassador opened the Cowra Japanese War Cemetery. It is the only Japanese war cemetery in Australia. The Japanese who died anywhere in Australia during the war were brought there, five hundred and twenty-four in all, including the men of Cowra and the airmen shot down over Darwin.

In 1979, a Japanese garden opened in Cowra. Its designer, Ken Nakajima, deliberately left a grove of gum trees standing at the top of the hill. They stand for the Australian soldiers who were killed.

## 30. The jug
In 1984, on the fortieth anniversary, survivors of the breakout came back to Cowra. One of them was one of the three men who had eaten May Weir's scones.

He went back to the farm. Her son Bruce said he ran around the place like a little dog off a chain. Margaret told him the family still had the jug her mother had used to pour him tea. It was the very same jug.

Takahara came back too, and persuaded twelve Buddhist priests to come with him. Yamada became president of the Cowra association in Japan. "Everybody thought we would die," he said, "but something happened, and we are still alive."

## 31. The last survivor
Teruo Murakami, the man who drew a circle, made his last trip to Cowra in August 2019, for the seventy-fifth anniversary. He was ninety-eight.

He said he had voted yes because he wanted to die. And he said: "I don't want any young person to do the things we have done, because it's such a miserable thing." His message, he said, was simple. "No war. Never, never again."

He died in Japan in September 2023, at the age of one hundred and three. He was the last survivor of the Cowra breakout.

## 33. Echo
Today, almost nothing is left of Number Twelve Prisoner of War Compound. After the war the huts were taken away, and the barbed wire was sold off to local farmers.

On the night of the eightieth anniversary, in August 2024, people gathered at the site of the camp at half past one in the morning, the hour the breakout is said to have begun.

Three kilometres away, in the Japanese War Cemetery, the cemetery's records list a grave under the false name he chose in Darwin, Tadao Minami. Beside it, they give the real one, which took forty years to find. Hajime Toyoshima, the pilot who blew the bugle.
