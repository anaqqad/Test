# German POWs Were Sent to a Minnesota Town. It Answered Them in German.

## 1. Broadway

In June 1944, two army trucks rolled down Broadway, the main street of New Ulm, Minnesota. In the back sat German prisoners of war, young men, smoking and talking among themselves. People stopped on the sidewalk to stare at them, and the prisoners stared back.

The local paper described the scene. A few girls walking along Broadway got a rousing cheer from the trucks. And the first thing the prisoners noticed in the town was a sign for beer, which one of them read out to the others.

In this town, the prisoners would hardly need a translator. New Ulm had been founded by German immigrants, and in 1944 much of the town still spoke German. This is the documented story of the German prisoners the Army sent to a German-speaking town in Minnesota, and of what the town did with them.

## 2. The town

New Ulm was founded in 1854 by a land company of German immigrants from Chicago, and named after the city of Ulm in Germany. On a hill above the town stands a statue of Hermann, the chieftain Germans remember for beating the Roman legions. By 1940, about eight thousand seven hundred people lived there.

The town had paid for being German once before. In July 1917, the summer America entered the First World War, about eight thousand people gathered in New Ulm, a town of six thousand at the time, for a meeting about the new draft. The mayor, Louis Fritsche, and the city attorney, Albert Pfaender, told the young men to obey the law, but said they hoped New Ulm's soldiers would not be sent to fight in Europe.

The state's Commission of Public Safety charged them with promoting seditious meetings. The governor suspended both men in August, and removed them from office in December. Within three years, both men were back in office. Twenty-seven years later, the Army chose this town for a camp of German soldiers.

## 3. The camp

The reason was peas. With so many men in uniform, Minnesota's canneries expected to need more than thirty-one thousand workers in 1944, and they asked for prisoners of war. That summer, about one thousand two hundred and seventy-five German prisoners were sent to work in the state.

The camp outside New Ulm was an old relief camp from the Depression, log cabins in the trees on the Cottonwood River, inside a low barbed-wire fence. The Army rented it for fifty dollars a week. In June, about a hundred and fifty prisoners arrived. Most were young airmen of the Luftwaffe, eighteen to twenty-five years old.

They worked twelve-hour shifts at the cannery in Sleepy Eye, about twelve miles to the west, packing peas and corn. The cannery paid the government for their labor. The prisoners were paid eighty cents a day, in coupons they could spend at the camp store.

## 4. The rules

The Army warned the people of the area not to communicate, consort with, give aid or comfort to the prisoners. The camp's commander, Lieutenant Arthur Roberts, also had the rules explained in the local newspaper, because the town did not understand them.

The rules came from a treaty signed in Geneva in 1929. Its second article said prisoners of war must at all times be humanely treated and protected, particularly against acts of violence, insults and public curiosity.

And its eleventh article said the food ration of prisoners of war shall be equal in quantity and quality to that of troops at base camps. The camp's food came from the Army's own depot at Fort Snelling. The prisoners' spokesman, a twenty-five-year-old named Tietz, asked the town for German books, and the newspaper took donations at its office.

## 5. In German

Within days, the paper was reporting contacts. A paper boy named Herman Schaper and a friend were cooking eggs on a hike near the camp when two prisoners walked up. One told them in German that they were frying the eggs wrong. Herman answered in German. The prisoners wanted to know about the invasion of Normandy.

A Lutheran pastor from nearby Courtland offered to hold services in German. He said he would treat them as he would like his own son treated if he were captured. Women sent baskets of food to the camp. At the cannery, American workers bought the prisoners ice cream and beer on their breaks.

On Sunday afternoons, a band of former Luftwaffe men gave concerts in front of the recreation hall. People from New Ulm came out to listen. One former prisoner remembered that they sang along, in German.

## 6. The farms

The guards could not watch everything. At the cannery they were everywhere on the first day, even on the roof. After a few days they disappeared, and came back only for the bus. Sometimes the prisoners had to go and find them, because they could not go home without their guards.

Then farmers began to hire them for the harvest. A farmer would pick his prisoners up at the camp, keep them on the farm all week, and bring them back for the weekend. They ate meat and potatoes and dessert at the family table, and sometimes they got a beer.

One farmer, Carl Ramberg, remembered the rules years later. He said they weren't supposed to feed the prisoners, but you couldn't expect a man to work on an empty stomach. They weren't supposed to visit with them either, and that, he said, was rather cruel.

## 7. The weekend

One family went further. A prisoner named Helmut Lichtenberg had worked on their farm, and he spent a whole weekend with them. On Sunday afternoon, the farmer and his mother-in-law drove him back to the camp. The guards stopped the car at the gate.

Lichtenberg went into solitary confinement. The Americans went to court. The judge gave them a lecture and fined them three hundred dollars. One account says they had also taken the prisoner to church. A history of Minnesota's camps calls it the only escape the New Ulm camp ever recorded.

## 8. The enemy

Not everyone in New Ulm took baskets to the fence. A woman named Joyce Aufderheide said years later that the prisoners made her angry. Her husband was over there, she said, and she was bitter.

Her husband, Jack, came home from an internment camp in Europe. He found German prisoners working at his grandfather's brickyard, eating well and treated, as he saw it, like kings. A few weeks after he got home, he went to work there, and he got rid of them. He said he resented them and wanted nothing to do with them.

In the spring of 1945, letters from New Ulm's own soldiers changed the town. They had seen the camps the Nazis built at Dachau and Buchenwald, and they wrote home about what they found. One local history puts it simply. The sympathy was gone. The prisoners were the enemy again.

The prisoners stayed until the work was done. That summer, the prisoners in Minnesota were credited with saving nearly two thirds of a record pea crop. In December 1945, the camp on the Cottonwood River closed, and the last prisoners left Minnesota.

## 9. What is left

Fifty years later, a newspaper reader asked whether it was true that many of the prisoners had stayed in New Ulm after the war, because they liked it better. The research director of the county historical society answered. None of them had stayed.

Some did come back to America on their own. Herbert Richter, who had joined the German air force at nineteen and been shot down as a radio operator, settled in Kenosha, Wisconsin, and lived there for thirty-five years. In 1985 he still remembered the Sunday concerts, and the people from town singing along.

## 10. The cabins

The log cabins on the Cottonwood River are still there. The camp is now the group center of Flandrau State Park, one of the few prisoner of war camps in the country still kept up. Visitors can ask at the park office to see them. The lake where the prisoners were allowed to swim is gone. Its dam was taken down in 1995, and the river runs past the cabins again.
