- Our documented version: New Ulm, Minnesota, a town founded by German immigrants where much of the population still spoke German.
  The town's mayor had been removed from office in 1917 for a rally against sending draftees overseas. In June 1944 the Army put a
  camp of young German prisoners outside it. The townspeople answered them in German, fed them, sang with them, a farm family was
  fined for taking one home for a weekend, and the sympathy ended when the camps in Germany were found. Not covered by our past
  episodes (whitewater-camp was a different Minnesota camp, about two escapees and a teacher).

## Main sources
1. **Anita Albrecht Buck, *Behind Barbed Wire: German Prisoner of War Camps in Minnesota*** (North Star Press, 1998), ch. 13
   "Camp No. 7, New Ulm", pp. 73-81, and pp. 14, 18, 21, 24, 35-36, 44 **[read via the Internet Archive's search-inside, item
   behindbarbedwire0000buck]**. Buck quotes the *Brown County Journal* and *New Ulm Daily Journal* of 1944 and interviews from 1985.
   - p73: "The city had been founded a century earlier by immigrants from Germany, and much of the population still spoke German.
     Many families still had relatives in Germany, giving them a sympathetic connection with the prisoners. Older people
     particularly felt sorry for them."
   - p73: first notice in the *Brown County Journal*, 16 May 1944 (Lt. George O. Gilead of Algona inspected the site). Journal, 4 June
     1944: "Excitement reigned along Broadway for a short time this morning as the first detachment of Nazi war prisoners rolled into
     the city to prepare the old Transient Camp on the banks of Cottonwood lake for the 150 prisoners that will be encamped there to
     help with the work at the Sleepy Eye canning plant." "It was a happy crew of prisoners that filled the two army trucks. They
     smoked and conversed among themselves while watching the number of onlookers who were just as curious to get a look at them.
     They were a crew of young men, apparently happy to be out of the war, and the first thing they spotted along the street was a
     'beer' sign which one prisoner apparently read to the rest."
   - p74: "A few girls walking along Broadway caused a rousing cheer from the trucks as they rolled past on their way to camp." The
     convoy arrived about 10 a.m. Bob Steinboch, high school: "I thought they would look different than Americans, but they didn't."
     Contracts with California Packing Company in Sleepy Eye and other canners. Camp: transient relief camp of 1934, then a CCC camp,
     closed January 1942; log-sided cabins in the trees about 100 yards from a small lake made by damming the Cottonwood River;
     barracks with kitchen and mess hall, seven "hauser", recreation hall with fireplace and library.
   - p75: "low, barbed-wire fence"; guards from the 410th Military Police Escort Guard Company; rent fifty dollars a week; prisoners
     "ranged in age from eighteen to twenty-five years"; spokesman Tietz, twenty-five, asked for German-language books, "Donations may
     be left at the Daily Journal offices"; "civilians were warned that contact with them was not acceptable"; New Ulm Daily Journal of
     9 June 1944 reported two contacts.
   - p76: Herman Schaper, a Journal paper carrier, and a friend on a hiking trip near the camp; two prisoners approached; "You're
     frying eggs wrong," one said in German; Herman was fluent; "The prisoners were interested in news of the Normandy invasion."
     Lt. Arthur S. Roberts, commanding officer of Camp No. 7, wrote an explanation of the Geneva Convention of July 1929 printed in
     the local paper ("ample food, comfortable housing, good sanitation, and sufficient recreation"). The Rev. E.J. Markhausen of
     Emanuel Lutheran Church, Courtland, offered services in German: "I will treat them as I would like my son treated if he were
     captured." "Women sent baskets of food to the camp, and young girls flirted with the POWs. Those working in the canneries bought
     them ice cream and beer on their breaks."
   - p77: main body of about 150 arrived 18 June 1944; worked mostly in the cannery; Sleepy Eye cannery (Richard Arbes): twelve-hour
     shifts, ages sixteen to fifty-five; guards at the cannery "disappeared" after a couple of days; "We can't go home without the
     guards."
   - p78: Herbert Richter (from Schweidnitz, Luftwaffe radio operator at nineteen, later lived in Kenosha, Wisconsin, for 35 years;
     interview Memorial Day 1985): Sunday band concerts in front of the recreation hall; "People from New Ulm came to listen and sang
     along in German along with the music." Farm work: farmers picked up the prisoners and returned them every weekend (Francis
     Beranek); "fed meat and potatoes, vegetables, and desserts at the family table and occasionally given beer." Carl Ramberg: "We
     weren't supposed to feed them, but you couldn't expect them to work on an empty stomach. We weren't supposed to visit with them,
     either, which was rather cruel." After the canning season: American Art Stone (New Ulm) and Ochs Brick and Tile (Springfield).
   - p79: guard house burned December 1944 (leaky oil burner), the prisoners built a new one. Larry Ochs: "Without the prisoners, the
     business would have closed down." Joyce Aufderheide (1985): "They made me angry. My husband was over there. I was bitter."
   - p80: Jack Aufderheide "returned to New Ulm from an internment camp in Europe to find his enemies working in the family business";
     within two or three weeks he got rid of them: "I resented them and didn't want anything to do with them." Farmer Daniel Dietz:
     guards had to shoo away young women walking along the river. Spring 1945: 143 prisoners back, spokesman T/Sgt. Tietz. Iris
     Mathiowetz: prisoners "surprised and happy to land in New Ulm, a German-speaking community".
   - p81: "As death camps were uncovered in Germany, New Ulm soldiers saw what the Nazis had done in camps at Dachau and Buchenwalt
     [sic]. They wrote home of what they had seen. Feelings toward the Germans prisoners of war changed. Sympathy was gone. The POWs
     were again the enemy." Richter: menus went from pork chops and steaks to potatoes and herring.
   - p14: a 1994 St. Paul *Pioneer Press* reader believed many prisoners stayed in New Ulm after the war; Darla Gebhard, research
     director of the Brown County Historical Society: "none of the prisoners stayed in New Ulm after the war"; about 150, almost all
     enlisted men, arrived in June 1944. p36: about 1,275 German prisoners allocated to Minnesota in 1944; Owatonna, Fairmont and New
     Ulm kept theirs over the winter. p44: New Ulm 317 prisoners in July 1945.
2. **Edward J. Pluth, "Prisoner of War Employment in Minnesota During World War II," *Minnesota History* 44:8 (Winter 1975),
   290-303** (`docs/pluth-1975-mn-history-pow-employment.pdf`) **[read directly]**. Canners estimated they would need more than
   31,000 workers in 1944 and could employ at least 3,000 prisoners; "about 1275" German prisoners allocated (5 June 1944); branch
   camps at Ortonville, New Ulm, Fairmont, Faribault, Montgomery, St. Charles opened June-July 1944; New Ulm kept its prisoners over
   the winter. Army notice (Lt. Col. Arthur T. Lobdell, Algona): residents not to "communicate, consort with, give aid or comfort" to
   the prisoners, "but this caution was not always an effective deterrent"; "many came to sympathize with their plight". Prisoners
   received 80 cents a day; the California packing plant at Sleepy Eye paid the government 60 cents an hour for hand-packing. In
   1945 prisoners "were credited with saving 65 per cent of the record-breaking pea crop". The last prisoners left Minnesota in late
   December 1945.
3. **Wikipedia, "Flandrau State Park"**, citing Dean B. Simmons, *Swords into Plowshares: Minnesota's POW Camps During World War II*
   (2000), Buck (1998), Niskanen (2010) and the park's interpretive signage **[via fetch]**. About 160 prisoners in June 1944, mostly
   Luftwaffe, 18-25, twelve U.S. Army guards; cannery at Sleepy Eye; small unguarded groups on farms; "Prisoner Helmut Lichtenberg spent
   a weekend with a farm family he had worked for"; the farmer and his mother-in-law drove him back on Sunday afternoon, guards stopped
   them; Lichtenberg in solitary confinement; the Americans fined $300 and lectured by the judge; the camp's only documented escape
   incident (Simmons). Camp closed December 1945. The camp buildings are the park's group center, one of the few POW camps still kept. The park's dam on the Cottonwood River, built by WPA and CCC crews and finished in 1937, was demolished in 1995 (park signage, via the same article).
   **Wikipedia, "New Ulm, Minnesota"** (citing Simmons): "In 1944, a New Ulm family was fined $300 for removing a prisoner from the camp,
   housing him, and taking him to church." Founded 1854 by the German Land Company of Chicago, named after Ulm; 1940 population 8,743.
4. **MNopedia (Paul Nelson), "New Ulm Military Draft Meeting, 1917"** (Minnesota Historical Society) **[via fetch]**. 25 July 1917; a
   crowd estimated at 8,000 in a town of 6,000; Mayor Louis Fritsche and city attorney Albert Pfaender urged compliance with the draft
   law but hoped New Ulm's soldiers would not have to fight in Europe; the Commission of Public Safety charged them with "promoting and
   participating in seditious public meetings"; Governor Burnquist suspended them on 21 August and removed them on 1 December 1917;
   by 1920 both were back in their offices. **Wikipedia, "Louis A. Fritsche"**: mayor 1912-1920; the same charge.
5. **Convention Relative to the Treatment of Prisoners of War, Geneva, 27 July 1929**, in Bevans, *Treaties and Other International
   Agreements of the U.S.*, vol. 2, pp. 938-940 (`docs/Geneva-1929-POW-arts-2-11-Bevans.pdf`, Library of Congress) **[read
   directly]**. Art. 2: "They must at all times be humanely treated and protected, particularly against acts of violence, insults and
   public curiosity." Art. 11: "The food ration of prisoners of war shall be equal in quantity and quality to that of troops at base
   camps."
6. **New Ulm Journal, "Sharing memories of WWII POW camp research"** (14 Nov 2023) **[via fetch]**. Denny Warta, a high-school senior
   who drove Eibner's bakery van to the camp and, because he spoke German, visited with the prisoners: "There was a shortage of men in
   New Ulm." Prisoners paid in chits, 10 cents an hour, up to 80 cents a day.
7. **Camp Algona POW Museum, "Branch Camps: New Ulm"** **[via fetch]**. Branch camp No. 7, CCC camp, 7 June 1944 to December 1945;
   farm labor, canning, brick and tile; 1st Lt. Arthur S. Roberts; 315 men (Sept. 1945).
8. **Wikipedia, "Hermann Heights Monument"**: statue of Hermann (Arminius), remembered for the German victory over the Romans in the
   Teutoburg Forest; built 1888-1897 by the Sons of Hermann; about 102 feet tall.

## Disagreements and how the script handles them
- **How many arrived:** "about 150" (Buck, Journal), "about 160" (Wikipedia). Script: about a hundred and fifty.
- **Opening date:** 7 June 1944 (Algona museum); advance detachment noticed in the Journal of 4 June; main body 18 June (Buck).
  Script: "In June 1944".
- **The weekend:** Flandrau article (Simmons): weekend with a farm family, driven back on Sunday, $300 fine, judge; New Ulm article
  (Simmons): family fined $300 "for removing a prisoner from the camp, housing him, and taking him to church". Script tells the
  weekend and says one account adds church.
- **1917 crowd:** 8,000 (MNopedia) vs 10,000 (Wikipedia). Script: about eight thousand, in a town of six thousand (MNopedia).
- **Fritsche after 1917:** MNopedia "by 1920 both returned to their offices"; Wikipedia says he served as mayor until 1920. Script:
  "Within three years, both men were back in office."
- **Ages:** 18-25 (Journal via Buck, Wikipedia); the Sleepy Eye cook-room supervisor recalled sixteen to fifty-five. Script: mostly
  young airmen, eighteen to twenty-five.
- **Jack Aufderheide:** "returned ... from an internment camp in Europe" (Buck). Script says he came home from a camp in Europe, without
  calling him a prisoner of war.
