## Description
In June 1944, two army trucks full of German prisoners of war rolled down Broadway in New Ulm, Minnesota. The town had been founded by German immigrants, and in 1944 much of it still spoke German. The Army warned the townspeople not to talk to the prisoners, feed them or help them. Within days, a paper boy was chatting with two of them in German.

This is the documented story of Camp New Ulm: a town whose mayor had been removed from office in 1917 for a rally against the draft, a pastor who said he would treat the prisoners the way he would want his own son treated, Sunday concerts where the town sang along in German, a farm family fined $300 for taking a prisoner home for the weekend, and the spring of 1945, when letters from the town's own soldiers about Dachau and Buchenwald ended the sympathy. Told from the 1944 newspapers, a history of Minnesota's camps, and the 1929 Geneva Convention.
