# German POWs Didn't Know Who Was Listening. Many Were Jewish Refugees.

## 1. Cold open

During the Second World War, not far from the Pentagon, German prisoners sat in small rooms and talked. U-boat officers, army officers, scientists. They talked about the war, and about the Americans who had captured them. They thought they were only talking to each other.

In another building, young American soldiers in headphones were writing it all down. Many of them had been born in Germany or Austria. Many were Jewish, and had escaped the Nazis only a few years before. Now they were listening to the men they had fled.

The place did not officially exist. It had no name, only a mailing address: P.O. Box 1142. This is the documented story of America's secret listening post, and of the men who kept its secret for sixty years.

## 2. The box

The address belonged to Fort Hunt, an old coastal artillery fort on the Potomac River in Virginia, on land that had once been part of George Washington's estate at Mount Vernon. In April 1942, the Army began using it to question prisoners.

The site was chosen with care. It was isolated enough to keep a secret, and close enough to the Pentagon to send what it learned there quickly. At its height, there were eighty-seven buildings behind the wire. Neighbors saw unmarked buses with darkened windows driving in and out.

On paper, it was not a prison camp at all. It was a processing center. That mattered. Prisoners could be held for days or weeks before anyone told the Red Cross where they were. Under the Geneva Convention, the Red Cross was supposed to know.

## 3. The listeners

To make it work, the Army needed something rare: soldiers who spoke German the way Germans did. Many of the people it found were refugees. They had fled Nazi Germany and Austria as boys, often because they were Jewish, and they had every reason to want the war won.

The Navy was just as choosy. It interviewed four hundred and fifty candidates for interrogation work, and took thirty-five. Some of the Army's men had trained at Camp Ritchie, in Maryland, the school for military intelligence.

One of them was Peter Weiss, a young sergeant born in Vienna. Decades later he described his job in a single sentence. My job, he said, was to listen, and listen very carefully. Sometimes that listening happened when the prisoners did not know anyone was listening.

## 4. The prisoners

Between 1942 and the summer of 1945, more than three thousand four hundred prisoners passed through P.O. Box 1142. They were not ordinary soldiers. They were picked for what they might know: U-boat commanders and their crews, senior officers, engineers and scientists.

Among them was General Reinhard Gehlen, who had run German intelligence against the Soviet army on the Eastern Front. For the Americans, men like him were becoming more valuable by the month. The war against Germany was being won. The next rival was already in view.

## 5. The rooms

The rooms where the prisoners lived and talked were wired with hidden microphones. At first, even some American officers were uneasy about it. There was an old feeling that gentlemen do not read each other's mail. But the results were hard to argue with.

The interrogations were useful. The recordings were often better. Some of the most important discoveries came not from questioning at all, but from prisoners talking among themselves, sure that they were alone. There were also stool pigeons: men placed among the prisoners to steer the conversation.

Not every prisoner was fooled. Some guessed the rooms were bugged, and talked endlessly about nothing, leaving American clerks to type up hours of meaningless chatter.

## 6. A battle of wits

The veterans of Fort Hunt insisted that they did not beat anyone. Instead, they rewarded prisoners who cooperated, with books, newspapers, cigarettes and liquor. Some prisoners were taken out to steak dinners. Others were drawn into games.

Henry Kolm, a young physicist who later worked at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, said that a game of chess or Ping-Pong got more information out of a German general than torture does today. Arno Mayer, who later became a historian at Princeton, said his job was to keep certain prisoners happy, because the Army hoped they would come to work for the United States.

For the most stubborn men, there was a harder card to play. Interrogators threatened to hand them over to the Soviet Union. To make the threat believable, Russian-speaking American soldiers dressed up as officers of the Red Army and sat in on the questioning. It was a bluff, and for some prisoners it was enough.

## 7. What they learned

By the end of August 1945, the people of P.O. Box 1142 had written more than five thousand reports. Interrogations helped point Allied bombers at Peenemünde, the German rocket base on the Baltic coast.

Interrogations and recordings revealed that the Germans had started loading supplies at railway crossings, because the stations were being bombed. They revealed that the German navy had built dummy concrete structures at its submarine pens, to fool Allied bombers. Both discoveries changed where the bombs fell. Questioning U-boat crews about Germany's acoustic torpedo also helped the Americans develop a defense against it.

## 8. The man on the fence

One prisoner never left. Werner Henke commanded the U-boat U-515. In December 1942, his boat sank the British liner Ceramic, and British radio later accused him of firing on the survivors. Henke believed the British would put him on trial as a war criminal.

He was captured in April 1944 and brought to Fort Hunt. According to his biographer, the interrogators used his fear of the British against him. On June fifteenth, 1944, Henke ran to the fence and started to climb. He kept climbing after the guards ordered him to stop, and a guard shot him dead. No prisoner ever escaped from P.O. Box 1142.

## 9. The other secret

Fort Hunt had a second secret, kept even from its own commanding officer. In another part of the post, a separate team built escape kits for American prisoners held in Germany.

They hid compasses inside uniform buttons, five million of them. They printed maps on silk and on tissue paper. They packed radio parts into baseballs and maps into decks of playing cards, and sent it all to German prison camps in care packages from relief organizations that did not exist. By 1944, eighty to a hundred and twenty packages were going out every day.

## 10. Burn all records

When the war ended, so did P.O. Box 1142. In August 1945, the War Department ordered the escape program's records destroyed. The men who had worked there were sworn to secrecy, and in 1946 the barracks were bulldozed. By 1948, the land was a park again.

Most of the veterans kept the oath. They did not tell their wives, or their children. As Arno Mayer put it, when you swear to secrecy, you don't really know when the oath expires.

## 11. Breaking the silence

Sixty years later, the National Park Service started asking questions about the old fort. It tracked down the men who had served there and recorded their memories. One ranger, Brandon Bies, interviewed more than seventy of them.

In October 2007, about two dozen veterans of P.O. Box 1142 came back to Fort Hunt, most of them in their eighties and nineties. A flag was raised and a marker unveiled, at exactly eleven forty-two in the morning.

They came back during another war, and another argument about how to question prisoners. Several of them had something to say about it. George Frenkel, eighty-seven, said they had extracted information in a battle of wits, and that he was proud he had never compromised his humanity.

## 12. Echo

Today, Fort Hunt is a park. Families have picnics in a pavilion built where the old post hospital stood, the building where the escape kits were once planned. The microphones are long gone. For sixty years, so was the story.
