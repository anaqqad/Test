# Sources: "German POWs Didn't Know Who Was Listening. Many Were Jewish Refugees."

No source, no line. Every fact in `script.md` maps to one of the entries below.

## Why this topic (competitor research, 31 Oct 2026)
YouTube, this week: "German POWs Mocked Their American Guards—Then They Heard Perfect German!" (Mr. WWII) 256K in 13 days;
copies by Battlefield Untold (99K in 7 days) and WW2 PUB (80K in 9 days); "German POWs Thought No One Could Understand Them. The

## Main sources
1. **National Park Service, "POWs and Intel at Fort Hunt in World War II"** (nps.gov/articles/forthuntww2.htm; U.S.
   government work). Used for: a top-secret installation known only as "P.O. Box 1142", mailing address in Alexandria;
   87 structures at its height; interrogation, "listening devices" and "stool pigeons"; MIS and the Navy's ONI; U-boat
   commanders and crews; of 547 Army personnel, 68 trained at Camp Ritchie; some interrogators were recent immigrants from
   Nazi Europe; "non-violent interrogation methods tailored for individual POWs"; 3,451 prisoners processed from 1942 to July
   1945, more than 5,000 reports by the end of Aug. 1945; Peenemünde identified through interrogation; Reinhard Gehlen
   questioned; von Braun's team questioned in fall 1945 at Fort Strong (not Fort Hunt); MIS-X from Feb. 1943, secret even from
   the fort's commandant; silk maps, five million buttons with hidden compasses; care packages through two fictitious relief
   organizations with compasses, maps, money, radios; 80–120 packages a day by 1944; radio parts in baseballs, maps in playing
   cards; servicemen "sworn to secrecy"; E&E records ordered destroyed 20 Aug. 1945; declared surplus Nov. 1946; buildings
   removed; returned to the NPS Jan. 1948; picnic pavilion built on the old hospital site in the 1960s.
2. **Warren Fishbein, review of Robert K. Sutton, *Nazis on the Potomac* (Casemate, 2021), *Studies in Intelligence* 66,
   no. 2 (CIA, June 2022).** Sutton, retired NPS chief historian, built on the NPS veterans' interviews. Used for: location
   chosen for isolation and nearness to the Pentagon; more than 3,000 prisoners incl. high-ranking officers and weapons
   scientists; many interrogators were Jewish refugees who had fled Nazi Germany, "especially motivated"; cooperative
   prisoners rewarded with reading material and liquor; interviewees said they avoided physical coercion; a bogus threat to
   transfer the most resistant prisoners to the Soviet Union, with fake Red Army officers played by Russian-speaking U.S.
   soldiers; audio surveillance at first controversial ("gentlemen do not read each other's mail"); revelations: supplies
   loaded at railway crossings instead of bombed stations, dummy concrete structures at submarine pens.
3. **Petula Dvorak, "Fort Hunt's Quiet Men Break Silence on WWII," *Washington Post*, 6 Oct. 2007** (via HNN and WETA
   *Boundary Stones*): for six decades the veterans kept quiet; about two dozen met for the first time since the 1940s;
   methods included bugging cells, censoring letters, steak dinners, games; Henry Kolm, 90, MIT physicist: "We got more
   information out of a German general with a game of chess or Ping-Pong than they do today, with their torture." George
   Frenkel, 87: "We extracted information in a battle of the wits. I'm proud to say I never compromised my humanity."
4. **"Talk Was Their Weapon — Silence Their Legacy," *Connection Newspapers*, 8 Oct. 2007**: first P.O. Box 1142 reunion,
   flag raised and marker unveiled "at exactly 11:42 a.m."; sworn to secrecy for more than 60 years; Navy interviewed 450
   candidates and chose 35 interrogators; Peter Weiss, 81, Army sergeant born in Vienna: "My job was to listen and listen very
   carefully." / "Sometimes that listening occurred when the prisoners were not aware we were listening."
5. **"Arno Mayer: His job during WW II was to befriend the enemy," HNN (2007)**: keep prisoners happy, newspapers and liquor;
   for generals, the aim was the Red Army's order of battle; "when you swear to secrecy you don't really know when the oath
   expires."
6. **"P.O. Box 1142: The Secrets of Fort Hunt," WETA *Boundary Stones*, 9 Dec. 2015**: on part of George Washington's
   Mount Vernon estate; MIS-Y from April 1942; a "processing center", not a camp; prisoners held days or weeks before being
   reported to the Swiss and the Red Cross; cells bugged; the most important finds came from recorded conversations among
   prisoners; some noticed and kept clerks transcribing "meaningless chatter"; unmarked buses with darkened windows; no
   prisoner escaped; Werner Henke killed in June 1944 trying to escape, reportedly fearing transfer to the British; "Burn all
   records".
7. **Wikipedia, "P. O. Box 1142"** (cites Sutton; NPS): many interrogators were Jewish immigrants who had fled Germany as
   children; more than 3,400 questioned 1942–46; acoustic-torpedo countermeasure; interrogator Rudy Pins on the fake Russian
   officers; NPS ranger Brandon Bies interviewed more than 70 veterans; the Red Cross was not notified (Geneva); in 1946 the
   barracks were bulldozed; memorial at Fort Hunt Park.
8. **Wikipedia, "Werner Henke"** (main source Timothy Mulligan, *Lone Wolf*, 1993): U-515, sank the liner *Ceramic*
   (7 Dec. 1942); a British broadcast accused him of shooting survivors; captured 9 April 1944; shot climbing the fence at
   Fort Hunt on 15 June 1944.

## Disagreements
- **Prisoner count:** 3,451 (NPS, to July 1945) vs "more than 3,400" (Wikipedia, to 1946) vs "more than 3,000" (CIA review).
  Script: "more than three thousand four hundred".
- **Von Braun:** questioned at Fort Strong, Boston (NPS) vs Fort Hunt (Wikipedia). Script does not place him at Fort Hunt.
- **"Broke their silence":** Arno Mayer said he had spoken about it before; script says "most kept quiet".
- **Henke's death:** details tagged "citation needed" on Wikipedia; WETA reports the same; script attributes it.
