# Japan Expected MacArthur to Humiliate Them on the Missouri. Then He Called Two Prisoners Forward.

## 1. The little flags

Tokyo Bay, the second of September, 1945. It is four minutes before nine in the morning, and the sky is grey. A small boat comes alongside the American battleship U S S Missouri. Eleven Japanese men climb out of it and start up the ladder. The first of them climbs slowly, with a cane, because he walks on an artificial leg.

At the top of the ladder there is no room to stand. Sailors fill every turret top and every gun platform. Photographers hang from the superstructure. Admiral Nimitz wrote that evening that every point of vantage was taken, by newsmen, by cameramen, and by any man from the ship who could get a foothold. More than two hundred correspondents are aboard.

One of the eleven is a diplomat named Toshikazu Kase. Years later he wrote down what it felt like to walk out onto that deck. He felt, he wrote, that they were being put in the pillory. A million eyes seemed to beat on them like a storm of arrows. Never, he wrote, had he realized that staring eyes could hurt so much.

They are led to a spot in front of a table covered with a green cloth, and left to stand there. Kase wrote that they waited like penitent boys awaiting the dreaded schoolmaster. While he waited, he looked up at the wall of the ship. Painted on it was a row of little Rising Sun flags, one for every Japanese plane and submarine the Missouri's gunners claimed to have destroyed.

He tried to count them, and tears gathered in his eyes. He thought of the young suicide pilots. He was sure of one thing. He had been brought here to be humiliated, in front of the whole world.

He was wrong about what would happen in the next twenty minutes. And before that day was over, this quiet diplomat would put a question in writing to the Emperor of Japan. This is the documented story of the eighteen days that ended the Second World War, told from the records of both sides: the Navy's radio messages, MacArthur's own staff history, the memoirs of the men who were there, and the recording of the ceremony itself. To understand Kase's question, we have to go back to the day the war stopped.

## 2. What Japan feared

On the fifteenth of August, 1945, the people of Japan heard their Emperor speak on the radio. He told them the war was over. Japan had accepted the Allied terms. For the first time in its recorded history, the country would be occupied by a foreign army.

Nobody knew what that would mean. MacArthur's own staff later collected what the Japanese newspapers had printed in those first days. The newspaper Yomiuri reported that within three days of the broadcast, the people of Tokyo had convinced themselves of five things. American soldiers would loot Japan. Americans would rob Japan of all the food. Women and girls would be violated. All men would be killed. What was left of Tokyo would be devastated.

The government that had to manage this was brand new. The Emperor's uncle, Prince Higashikuni, became prime minister. His job was to hand the country to the Americans without a civil war. Three to four million Japanese soldiers were still under arms.

His first test was a single trip. The Allies had ordered Japan to send envoys to MacArthur's headquarters in Manila, to receive the instructions for the occupation.

## 3. The white bombers

On the nineteenth of August, sixteen Japanese officers led by Lieutenant General Torashiro Kawabe took off for Manila. The Allies had ordered how they must travel. They flew in two twin-engine Betty bombers, painted white all over with green crosses.

Their call signs were Bataan One and Bataan Two. The bombers flew to the island of Ie Shima, where American cameramen filmed them landing. From there the envoys were put on an American transport and flown to Manila.

The Navy's historian later summed up the talks in Manila in one sentence: there were no negotiations. Kawabe's men were handed the orders and the dates. Then they started home.

They did not arrive on time. On the way back, one of the white planes had to be ditched, and the delegation was missing for hours. In Tokyo, Prime Minister Higashikuni sat up all night. He later wrote that he was wondering whether Japan's own air attack units had shot the envoys down.

When Kawabe finally reached the palace, the terms were not as severe as Tokyo had feared. MacArthur's staff history, quoting Higashikuni's memoirs, says the Emperor was quite relieved. But Higashikuni's fear that night was not imagination. At the very airfield where MacArthur intended to land, a Japanese navy captain had refused to surrender.

## 4. A divine nation does not surrender

The airfield was called Atsugi, about twenty-five miles southwest of Tokyo. It was the home of the 302nd Naval Air Group. It had fought right up to the morning of the fifteenth of August, the last morning of the war. It had only ever had one commander, Captain Yasuna Kozono.

After the Emperor's broadcast, Kozono called his officers together. According to a study published by Japan's own Ministry of Defense, he told them the Emperor had been deceived by his advisers, and that Japan did not know how to surrender and could not surrender. Then he sent a radio message to the entire Japanese navy. It said that its leaders had lost the right to command.

He had about a thousand men, around a hundred and thirty aircraft, many of them damaged, and supplies for three months. From the sixteenth to the eighteenth of August, his planes flew over bases and cities and dropped leaflets calling on the country to fight on.

A rear admiral was sent to talk him out of it, and failed. Kase later wrote that men at the air base were even boasting that they would attack the battleship Missouri. MacArthur's plane was supposed to land on this airfield in a matter of days.

The navy did not dare to send troops against its own men. What stopped Captain Kozono in the end was not an order. It was something much smaller.

## 5. Fever

Kozono had malaria. It had come back on the day of the surrender broadcast, and as the rebellion went on, it got worse. By the eighteenth of August he was confined to his quarters. The navy's doctors had to sedate him again and again.

On the evening of the twentieth, two of his senior officers, Commander Hideo Sugawara and Lieutenant Commander Minoru Yoshino, were received by Prince Takamatsu, the Emperor's younger brother and himself a navy officer. The prince told them the surrender was the Emperor's own wish. It was not a plot by his advisers.

Early the next morning, the twenty-first, Kozono was heavily sedated and moved to the naval hospital at Yokosuka. Without him, the revolt broke apart in a single day. About eighty young pilots and aircrew took thirty-four planes and flew them to Army airfields, hoping to keep fighting from there. They were brought back and arrested. The propellers were taken off every plane at Atsugi and the fuel tanks were drained. That afternoon the 302nd Air Group was disbanded.

A week later, the first Americans to land at Atsugi found rows of Japanese planes sitting helplessly, stripped of their propellers. Kozono was court-martialed in October for group insubordination and sentenced to life in prison. He was paroled in 1950, and died ten years later. But while Atsugi was being emptied, the sound of B-29s came back over Japan, and people ran for the shelters out of habit.

## 6. Bombers without bombs

From the twenty-seventh of August, the Superfortresses came back. They flew low over the cities, and people who had lived through the fire raids ran for the air raid shelters. MacArthur's staff wrote that it was force of habit. There were no bombs.

The planes had words painted under each wing in big letters: P W Supplies. They were looking for the prison camps. Under every camp roof, Allied prisoners of war had been starving for three years, some since the fall of Bataan and Corregidor. Now the B-29s dropped them food, medicine and clothing, packed in bundles meant to last three, seven or ten days.

The prisoners painted answers on their roofs, big enough to read from the air. The staff history lists them. S O S. Have hospital cases here. Drop radio please. Hong Kong men thank you. Men from Corregidor, Bataan thank Wasp. And on one roof, after the trucks had come: All left this camp.

Not every drop went well. Some drums broke loose from their parachutes and fell straight through camp roofs. Eight of the bombers were lost. Over the next three weeks the Twentieth Air Force flew nine hundred effective sorties, dropped four thousand four hundred and seventy tons of supplies, and reached a hundred and fifty-eight camps.

But food could be dropped from the sky. Men who could not walk could not be lifted out by parachute. The Japanese government's own count said there were six thousand one hundred and twenty-five Allied prisoners in the Tokyo area, and that four hundred and seventeen of them could not get out of bed. To reach them, somebody had to land.

## 7. The first one hundred and fifty

The first landing was set for the twenty-sixth of August. A typhoon pushed it back two days. In the meantime, an American Navy pilot from the carrier Yorktown landed at Atsugi against orders, and had the Japanese hang a banner for the Army. It said: Welcome to Atsugi from Third Fleet.

At eight twenty-eight on the morning of the twenty-eighth, a C-46 transport touched down at Atsugi. It was followed by fifteen more. On board was the advance party: about a hundred and fifty officers and men under Colonel Charles Tench of MacArthur's staff. The Japanese officer in charge of the airfield, Lieutenant General Seizo Arisue, later wrote in his memoir that the Americans landed downwind, ignoring the wind sock he had put up for them.

Arisue walked up to Colonel Tench and stiffly saluted him. He offered the Americans a meal. Tench politely declined. His men had brought their own rations. Within forty-five minutes, to the amazement of the Japanese, the Signal Corps had radios on the air to Okinawa.

Fewer than two hundred American soldiers with light weapons were standing in the middle of a country where three to four million soldiers were still under arms. General Arisue wrote that he had never had to use his head and his body so hard for so long.

That same morning, the first of two hundred and fifty-eight Allied ships steamed into Tokyo Bay. And that night, out in the bay, an American picket boat picked up two men who should not have been there.

## 8. Go ahead

The voices belonged to two British prisoners of war. They had escaped from a camp. Their story reached Admiral William Halsey, commander of the Third Fleet. The next day a Swiss doctor from the Red Cross confirmed it. In the camps around Tokyo Bay, prisoners were dying.

The Red Cross gave the numbers. At a camp called Omori, on the waterfront, two hundred captured aviators were in extremely bad condition. At the hospital of a camp called Shinagawa, a hundred and fifty men were seriously ill.

Halsey had three hospital ships ready, with doctors, corpsmen, food and clothing. But he had an order from MacArthur, sent on the twenty-fifth of August. It said it was not believed advisable for the Third Fleet to take any action on its own to evacuate prisoners in the Tokyo Bay area.

On the twenty-ninth, Fleet Admiral Chester Nimitz arrived in the bay. He sent MacArthur a radio message, urging him to let Halsey act immediately. Then he added a deadline of his own. If no reply came from MacArthur by one o'clock that afternoon, Halsey was authorized to do whatever humanitarian considerations required. In Halsey's memoir, Nimitz's words to him were shorter. Go ahead. General MacArthur will understand.

## 9. No authority

That afternoon, landing boats went up the estuary toward Omori. The rescue was led by Commodore Rodger Simpson, with the hospital ship Benevolence behind him. Simpson's chief of staff was Commander Harold Stassen, who before the war had been the youngest governor in the history of Minnesota.

At the gate of the camp the Japanese commandant met them. Halsey's memoir records what he said: I have no authority to release these men. Stassen was unarmed, like the rest of his party. He answered: You have no authority, period.

Inside the wire, the prisoners had already seen the boats. The Navy's photographs show them crowding the waterfront, waving the flags of the United States, Britain and the Netherlands. The Associated Press reporter wrote that they were delirious with joy, many of them weeping.

Among them was Major Gregory Boyington, the Marine fighter ace known as Pappy. He told the reporters he had been beaten with a baseball bat. The senior prisoner at Omori was Commander Arthur Maher, the senior survivor of the cruiser Houston, sunk in 1942.

At ten past seven that evening, the first freed prisoners were carried aboard the Benevolence. The Navy gave them a new name: RAMPs, Rescued Allied Military Prisoners. By midnight, according to Halsey, seven hundred and ninety-four men had been brought out. The next morning, Commodore Simpson sent a radio message to the fleet about what his men had found.

## 10. The blackest hellhole

The message survives in MacArthur's staff history, word for word. There has never been a blacker hellhole than the P O W hospital we are now evacuating, one half mile north of mooring. Approximately five hundred have now been processed to Benevolence, including fractures, open wounds, concussions, burns, and in general the worst malnutrition imaginable.

Simpson went on. Bestial beatings were common, especially at Ofuna, inquisitorial den of brutism. MacArthur's staff later called Ofuna the Gestapo center of Japan. Then came the line his officers never forgot. The cheers of P O W as our boat hove into sight brought tears to our eyes.

The Navy photographed some of the men on the Benevolence. One picture shows Private Alfred Sorenson of the U S Army, captured on Corregidor on the sixth of May, 1942, sitting in front of his first full meal in three years. He is looking at it, not eating yet. Another shows a man too weak to walk being carried down to a boat, past a crate marked U S Navy Battle Ration.

Two days later the Japanese government sent a formal protest. A U S Naval landing unit of about one hundred and fifty men, it said, broke into the Shinagawa camp of the Allied war prisoners and forcibly led away the prisoners. The Navy filed the complaint. Nobody gave anyone back.

## 11. The Bataan lands

On the thirtieth of August, the transports started landing at Atsugi one every three minutes. They brought the paratroopers of the 11th Airborne Division. By nightfall four thousand two hundred American soldiers had been flown into the heart of Japan. General Robert Eichelberger of the Eighth Army arrived that morning. This, he said, was the beachhead where he was supposed to land in the invasion of Japan, and he had never expected to get there by plane without a shot being fired.

About two in the afternoon, a four-engine C-54 came in to land. Eichelberger wrote down its name: the Bataan. MacArthur had ordered that there be no Japanese reception party.

MacArthur came out with his corncob pipe in his mouth, and walked over to Eichelberger and the paratroopers. MacArthur's headquarters recorded what he told them: Melbourne to Tokyo was a long road, but this looks like the payoff.

There is a famous story about this landing. MacArthur is said to have told his officers to take off their pistols, because if the Japanese meant to kill them, sidearms would be useless, and nothing would impress them more than a show of absolute fearlessness. The story appears in biographies written years later. It is not in the records of 1945. What the records do show is that he chose to land in a country of millions of armed men with a few thousand paratroopers. Then he got into a car.

## 12. Backs to the road

The road from Atsugi to the New Grand Hotel in Yokohama ran for about fifteen miles. MacArthur's staff wrote that it was lined, the whole way, with thousands of armed Japanese soldiers and policemen. They stood at attention, but faced away from the road.

It was a security measure, and an honor. In Japan, guards turned their backs that way only for the Imperial family. Eichelberger, riding in the convoy, wrote that until that day it had been done only for the Emperor himself.

The convoy was not impressive. The Japanese had found whatever cars they could. The Navy's historian notes that MacArthur rode in an old American-made car that broke down several times. Eichelberger wrote that it took them two hours to go twenty miles. He also wrote that he did not draw an easy breath until MacArthur was inside the hotel.

That night MacArthur's party ate dinner at the New Grand. A photographer who was there wrote down the menu. Hamburgers and grapes. Five hundred paratroopers of the 11th Airborne guarded the building. And the next evening, a thin general leaning on a cane walked into that dining room.

## 13. Out of Manchuria

Lieutenant General Jonathan Wainwright had surrendered the American forces in the Philippines on Corregidor in May 1942, after MacArthur had been ordered out to Australia. For more than three years he was a prisoner, in camps from Luzon to Formosa to Manchuria. With him at the end was the British general who had surrendered Singapore, Lieutenant General Arthur Percival.

Wainwright wrote that in the middle of the night at Mukden, an American officer named Major Lamar brought him a message. General MacArthur had invited General Percival and him to fly to Yokohama, to be with him at the formal surrender of the Japanese nation.

On the thirtieth of August he landed in Manila to be outfitted for the ceremony. An Associated Press reporter described him getting off the plane, pale and leaning on a cane. Wainwright said three words: This is wonderful.

The next afternoon he landed at Atsugi. As he was driven to Yokohama, Japanese generals came to attention and saluted him. He wrote that this came after years of humiliating servility before Japanese privates, of being beaten for failing to bow to a sentry. That evening at the New Grand he met MacArthur for the first time since March 1942. Their reunion is a story of its own, and we told it in an earlier episode. That night, in Tokyo, the Japanese government still could not find anyone willing to sign.

## 14. Nobody volunteers

Kase was blunt about it. Nobody, he wrote, wanted to volunteer for the odious duty. The prime minister, Prince Higashikuni, was the Emperor's uncle, and could not be sent. Prince Konoye, a former prime minister, refused. In the end the task fell to the foreign minister, Mamoru Shigemitsu. He accepted it as a sign of the Emperor's trust.

Shigemitsu had lost a leg to a bomb in Shanghai in 1932. The army had to send someone too. It chose the Chief of the Army General Staff, General Yoshijiro Umezu, a man who had opposed surrender to the very end.

Kase recorded what happened when Umezu was told, and he was careful to say it was only what people reported. Umezu went white with anger, it was said, and declared that if he was forced to do this, he would commit suicide. It took the Emperor's personal persuasion to make him go.

Nine aides went with them, three each from the foreign ministry, the army and the navy. Kase was one of them. The trip was kept secret, Kase wrote, in case extremists tried to stop them by force.

## 15. Without flag or sword

They left Tokyo at about five in the morning on the second of September. The road to Yokohama ran for miles through the ruins left by the fire raids. On the cars, the little flags that normally flew on official cars had been taken off. In Yokohama the officers left their swords at the governor's office. Kase wrote one line about it that he clearly never forgot. Diplomats without flag, and soldiers without sword.

At the quay four American destroyers were waiting. The Japanese were taken aboard the destroyer Lansdowne. The ride out to the Missouri took about an hour.

On the battleship, the Navy had spent days preparing. Sailors had rehearsed the ceremony, standing in for the guests and timing every step. And in a frame above the door of the captain's cabin hung a very old flag. It was the flag Commodore Perry had flown when his warships sailed into Tokyo Bay in 1853. A Navy courier had carried it nine thousand miles from Washington. It was too fragile to fly, so it had been backed with linen, and the stars ended up on the wrong side.

On the deck that morning stood more men who had come out of Japanese camps. The NBC radio announcer read their names on the air. Commander Arthur Maher of the Houston. A Navy torpedo pilot. A Marine officer captured on Corregidor. A machinist's mate from the submarine Grenadier. And, just behind the place where MacArthur would stand, Wainwright and Percival. At eight fifty-six, the Japanese came up the ladder, and Kase began to count the little painted flags.

## 16. The speech nobody expected

At two minutes past nine, General MacArthur walked briskly to the microphones. Kase was surprised by that alone. He wrote that it was their understanding there would be no speech. Halsey, standing nearby, noticed that MacArthur's voice was clear and firm, but that his hands were shaking.

This is his voice, recorded on the Missouri that morning.

[CLIP navy_victory_bulletin.mp4 567.6 584.4] We are gathered here, representatives of the major warring powers, to conclude a solemn agreement whereby peace may be restored.

[CLIP un_signing.mp4 179.7 198.3] The issues, involving divergent ideals and ideologies, have been determined on the battlefields of the world, and hence are not for our discussion or debate.

Then he said something the Japanese had not expected to hear. Nor is it for us here, he said, to meet in a spirit of distrust, malice or hatred.

It is my earnest hope, he went on, and indeed the hope of all mankind, that from this solemn occasion a better world shall emerge out of the blood and carnage of the past. A world founded upon faith and understanding, a world dedicated to the dignity of man and the fulfillment of his most cherished wish, for freedom, tolerance and justice.

Kase listened, and he understood exactly what was being offered. The man at the microphone, he wrote, could exact his pound of flesh if he so chose. He could impose a humiliating penalty if he so desired. And yet he pleaded for freedom, tolerance and justice. For a man who had come aboard expecting the worst humiliation, Kase wrote, this was a complete surprise.

Then MacArthur turned to the Japanese.

[CLIP navy_victory_bulletin.mp4 616.6 635.4] I now invite the representatives of the Emperor of Japan and the Japanese Government and the Japanese Imperial General Headquarters to sign the instrument of surrender at the places indicated.

The crowded deck went silent.

## 17. Two prisoners at the table

Shigemitsu walked to the table first. He took off his silk top hat and his gloves, and he had trouble with the chair because of his leg. He seemed not to know where to sign. MacArthur turned and told his chief of staff, General Richard Sutherland, to go and show him the line. Shigemitsu signed. Then he paused and looked up at Kase, who checked his wristwatch. It was four minutes past nine.

General Umezu came next. He did not sit down at all. Halsey wrote that he signed briskly, standing up. The radio announcer said he was in very much of a rush. It was six minutes past nine, and the Japanese part was done.

Then MacArthur stepped to the table himself. And at this moment he said something that is not in the typed text of his speech. The radio recording of the ceremony caught it. MacArthur said the Supreme Commander would now sign on behalf of all the nations at war with Japan. Then he said: Will General Wainwright and General Percival step forward and accompany me while I sign?

In 1942 these two men had surrendered to Japan, one at Corregidor and one at Singapore. Now they stood at MacArthur's shoulders while he signed Japan's surrender. The announcer said MacArthur's hand was shaking. He used several pens. Halfway through his signature he turned and handed the first pen to Wainwright. The second went to Percival.

The rest of the signing took fourteen minutes. Nimitz signed for the United States, then China, Britain, the Soviet Union, Australia, Canada, France, the Netherlands and New Zealand. The Canadian, Colonel Lawrence Moore Cosgrave, who was blind in one eye from the First World War, signed the Japanese copy on the wrong line, and pushed every signature after him down one place. The New Zealander, whose name ended up in the margin, called himself a humble footnote to the document.

## 18. These proceedings are closed

At twenty-two minutes past nine, the last signature was on the page. MacArthur went back to the microphones.

[CLIP un_signing.mp4 471.9 488.8] Let us pray that peace be now restored to the world, and that God will preserve it always. These proceedings are closed.

Kase wrote that MacArthur faced the Japanese delegation when he said those last words, and said them curtly.

As he said it, the clouds over the bay broke. The NBC announcer described it on the air, and Kase described it in his book, the sun coming through over Tokyo Bay. Then came the sound. The Navy's figure is four hundred and fifty carrier planes from the Third Fleet, flying over the Missouri in massed formation, mast high. A few minutes later the B-29s came over.

Before they left, the diplomats checked their copy of the surrender and found the Canadian's mistake. Kase and his colleague Katsuo Okazaki would not accept a document with signatures on the wrong lines. General Sutherland sat down at the table, crossed out the country names, wrote in the right ones by hand, and initialled every change. Only then did the Japanese accept their copy.

At nine twenty-nine, after thirty-three minutes on board, they were piped over the side, with the honors due to men whose country was now officially at peace with the United States. No one saluted them and no one shook their hands. But nobody insulted them either. After the cease-fire, Nimitz had ordered that the use of insulting epithets about the Japanese did not now become officers of the United States Navy.

On the way back, in the destroyer's gun room, Kase heard MacArthur on the radio, speaking to America. Today the guns are silent, MacArthur said. A great tragedy has ended. A great victory has been won. He spoke of the long, tortuous trail from those grim days of Bataan and Corregidor. And in that gun room, Kase began to write.

## 19. The question

What he was writing was a report for the Emperor. As soon as the delegation reached Tokyo, Shigemitsu took it straight to the palace, where, Kase wrote, the Emperor was anxiously waiting. Kase never published the report word for word. But in his book he told what was in it.

He had described the speech, the deck, and the man at the microphone. And then he had asked a question. Would it have been possible for us, he asked, had we been victorious, to embrace the vanquished with a similar magnanimity? He answered it himself in the next line of his book. Clearly, it would have been different.

When Shigemitsu came back from the palace, he told Kase what had happened. The Emperor had read the report, and had nodded, with a sigh, in agreement. We know this only from Kase, who heard it from Shigemitsu. No one else was in the room.

Kase ended that chapter with one more line. We were defeated, he wrote, by a nobler ideal. He was writing in 1950, under the occupation, about a man he admired. MacArthur's own staff wrote much the same: that his policies took Japan by complete surprise. Neither of them was neutral. But the fears listed in the Tokyo newspapers in August did not come true.

## 20. What became of them

General Umezu was tried by the Tokyo war crimes tribunal and sentenced to life in prison. He died of cancer in prison in January 1949. Shigemitsu was sentenced to seven years, the lightest sentence the tribunal gave. He was paroled in 1950 and became Japan's foreign minister again a few years later. Toshikazu Kase became Japan's first ambassador to the United Nations. He lived to the age of one hundred and one.

Wainwright was flown home, and on the tenth of September President Truman gave him the Medal of Honor in the White House garden. He died in 1953, eight years to the day after the ceremony on the Missouri. Harold Stassen went on to help sign the charter of the United Nations, and ran for president of the United States again and again.

In the three weeks after the surrender, MacArthur's recovery teams freed thirty-two thousand six hundred and twenty-four Allied prisoners of war from camps all over Japan. Some of them had been prisoners since the first weeks of the war.

If your father, grandfather or uncle was one of those prisoners, at Omori, Shinagawa, Ofuna, or any of the camps the B-29s found from the air, or if he stood on the deck of the Missouri that morning, tell us his name in the comments. We read every one.

The Missouri is a museum ship now, at Pearl Harbor. Set into her deck is a brass plaque that marks the spot where the green table stood, on the morning a Japanese diplomat came aboard expecting to be humiliated, and went home with a question for his Emperor.
