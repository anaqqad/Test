# Missouri surrender: fact sheet (raw)

Story window 15 Aug - 2 Sep 1945. Rule: no source, no line. Text in quote marks is verbatim from the copy I read
(OCR slips in archive.org scans corrected only where the page image or context makes the reading certain; those are
marked [sic] or explained). U.S. government texts (NHHC speech and radio texts, *Reports of General MacArthur*, Navy
radio messages) are public domain and quoted at length. Kase (Yale 1950), Halsey (1947) and Eichelberger (1950) may
still be in copyright: only short quotes are given, the rest is paraphrase with a page number. **FLAG** = weakly
sourced or second-hand. Research done 23 Nov 2026.

Note for the script: the channel already published "Japan Expected MacArthur to Court-Martial Wainwright. Then He
Handed Him the Pen." (video/wainwright-pen). The pens and the Wainwright reunion are covered there; keep them short
here and build this episode on the Japanese delegation, Atsugi and the Tokyo Bay prisoner rescue.

This is the prepared text as typed (typos are in the NHHC transcription: "representating", "faitfhul", "formallly",
"ncessary"; corrected here in brackets). Full text, public domain:

> "We are gathered here, representatives of the major warring powers, to conclude a solemn agreement whereby Peace may
> be restored. The issues, involving divergent ideals and ideologies, have been determined on the battle fields of the
> world and hence are not for our discussion or debate. Nor is it for us here to meet, represent[ing] as we do a
> majority of the peoples of the Earth, in a spirit of distrust, malice or hatred. But rather it is for us, both
> victors and vanquished, to rise to that higher dignity which alone befits the sacred purposes we are about to serve,
> committing all of our peoples unreservedly to faith[ful] compliance with the undertakings they are here
> forma[l]ly to assume.
>
> It is my earnest hope and indeed the hope of all mankind that from this solemn occasion a better world shall emerge
> out of the blood and carnage of the past - a world founded upon faith and understanding - a world dedicated to the
> dignity of man and the fulfillment of his most cherished wish - for freedom, tolerance and justice.
>
> The terms and conditions upon which surrender of the Japanese Imperial forces is here to be given and accepted are
> contained in the instrument of surrender now before you.
>
> As Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers I announce it my firm purpose, in the tradition of the countries I
> represent, to proceed in the discharge of my responsibilities with justice and tolerance, while taking all
> n[e]cessary dispositions to insure the terms of surrender are fully, promptly and faithfully complied with.
>
> I now invite the representatives of the Emperor of Japan and the Japanese Government, and the Japanese Imperial
> General Headquarters to sign the instrument of surrender at the places indicated.
>
> The Supreme Command for the Allied Powers will now sign on behalf of all the Nations at war with Japan.
>
> The representative of the United States of American will now sign. [then, one line each:] ... Republic of China ...
> United Kingdom ... Union of Soviet Socialist Republics ... Australia ... Canada ... France ... Netherlands ... New
> Zealand will now sign.
>
> Let us pray that Peace be now restored to the world, and that God will preserve it always. These proceedings are
> closed."

- The typed text has no line calling Wainwright and Percival forward. That was said off-script (see [10], [17], [22]).

- "My Fellow Countrymen: Today the guns are silent. A great tragedy has ended. A great victory has been won. The skies
  no longer rain death - the seas bear only commerce - men everywhere walk upright in the sunlight. The entire world
  lies quietly at Peace. The Holy Mission has been completed."
- "I speak for the thousands of silent lips, forever stilled among the jungles and the beaches and in the deep waters
  of the Pacific which marked the way."
- "As I look back on the long, tortuous trail from those grim days of Bataan and Corregidor, when an entire world lived
  in fea[r]; when Democracy was on the defensive everywhere, when modern civilization trembled in the balance, I thank a
  merciful God that he has given us the faith, the courage and the power from which to mould victory."
- "We must go forward to preserve in Peace what we won in War."
- "We have had our last chance. If we do not devise some greater and more equitable system Armageddon will be at our
  door."
- "We stand in Tokyo today reminiscent of our countryman, Commodore Perry, ninety-two years ago."
- "We are committed by the Potsdam Declaration of Principles to see that the Japanese people are liberated from this
  condition of slavery."
- "If the talents of the race are turned into constructive channels, the country can lift itself from its present
  deplorable state into a position of dignity."
- Ending: "And so, my fellow countrymen, today I report to you that your sons and daughters have served you well and
  faithfully ... They are homeward bound - take care of them."

- 2 Sep: at 0800 Missouri hoisted the flag the press claimed had flown over the Capitol on 7 Dec 1941; Capt. Murray:

- "Following a benediction, the ceremony ended at 0925. There were no salutes or handshakes. As the Japanese were

**[6]** Toshikazu Kase, *Journey to the Missouri*, ed. David Nelson Rowe (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1950),
ch. 1 "A Date with Destiny," pp. 1-14, and ch. "Into the Light," pp. 262-65. Full text (Kansas City Public Library

**Everything in this block comes only from Kase** (one delegate, writing in 1950 under the occupation, openly
admiring MacArthur; he says himself the chapter's style is "somewhat emotional").
- p. 1: Shigemitsu, signing, "paused for a moment looking inquiringly up at me. I consulted my wrist watch: four
  minutes past nine." Shigemitsu wrote "0904" after his name.
- p. 3: "It was a day of humiliation for us, the vanquished, a day of jubilation for them, the victorious."
- p. 4: Overcast, cool. They left Tokyo "at about five o'clock in the morning." Nine aides (three each from the Foreign
  Office, War and Navy ministries) plus Shigemitsu and Umezu. Miles of ruins along the road to Yokohama.
- p. 5: The trip was kept secret "lest extremists attempt to impede us by violence." "Nobody wanted to volunteer for
  the odious duty." Higashikuni (the Emperor's uncle) was unsuitable; Konoye "shunned the ordeal"; the task fell to
  Shigemitsu.
- pp. 5-6: Umezu had opposed surrender to the last; "so it is reported, he grew white with anger" and said he would
  commit harakiri if forced. "It required the Emperor's personal persuasion" (hearsay in Kase: "so it is reported").
- p. 6: Yokohama in under an hour; flags removed from the car hoods; officers left their swords at the prefectural
  governor's office. "Diplomats without flag and soldiers without sword, morose and silent we continued the journey to
  the quay." Four destroyers marked A, B, C, D; they boarded B, the Lansdowne (OCR "Lamdovm"). About an hour out to the
  Missouri, "some eighteen miles off shore." Sailors "lining the rails, a shining multitude in their uniforms of
  immaculate white."
- p. 7: Shigemitsu "limping heavily with his cane. He walks on a wooden leg, having had his left leg blown off in a bomb
  outrage in Shanghai some fifteen years ago." "eleven of us." Table "covered with green cloth."
- p. 7: "As we appeared on the scene, we were, I felt, being subjected to the torture of the pillory. A million eyes
  seemed to beat on us with the million shafts of a rattling storm of arrows barbed with fire." "Never had I realized
  that staring eyes could hurt so much."
- p. 7: they waited "like penitent boys awaiting the dreaded schoolmaster."
- pp. 7-8: He saw "several miniature Rising Suns" painted on the wall (planes and submarines claimed by the ship's
  gunners), tried to count them, and "tears quickly gathered in my eyes." He thought of the young suicide pilots.
- p. 8: MacArthur "walked briskly" to the microphones. "it was our understanding that there would be no speech." The
  paper in MacArthur's hands trembled "in the wind."
- p. 9: "He can exact his pound of flesh if he so chooses. He can impose a humiliating penalty if he so desires. And
  yet he pleads for freedom, tolerance, and justice. For me, who expected the worst humiliation, this was a complete
  surprise."
- p. 9: Shigemitsu signed first, then Umezu; "It was eight minutes past nine when MacArthur put his signature to the
  documents." Then US, China, UK, USSR, Australia, Canada, France, Netherlands, New Zealand.
- p. 10: MacArthur "then faced our delegation and said curtly, 'These proceedings are closed,'" The sun broke through;
  "Four hundred B-29's and 1,500 carrier planes" flew over (OCR "6-29'$ and i ,500"; same numbers as [7] p. 32).
- pp. 10-12: on the destroyer back, in the gun room, he heard MacArthur's broadcast to America on the radio (Kase prints
  its text).
- p. 13: He saw Perry's flag framed on the ship (he names the flagship "Powhattan").
- p. 13: **The report to the Emperor.** On the destroyer he wrote his impressions; "Shigemitsu took this document to
  the throne immediately after our return to the capital where the Emperor was anxiously waiting." In it "I raised the
  question whether it would have been possible for us, had we been victorious, to embrace the vanquished with a similar
  magnanimity. Clearly, it would have been different." "Returning from the audience Shigemitsu told me that the Emperor
  had nodded with a sigh in agreement."
  (Kase does not quote his report word for word; this paraphrase in his own book is the closest text. The Emperor's
  reaction is second-hand twice: Emperor to Shigemitsu to Kase.)
- p. 14: "We were defeated by a nobler ideal."
- p. 264: Kawabe's party flew to Manila on 19 Aug; when it was late returning (forced landing), Tokyo feared a
  dissident pilot had shot it down; Kawabe returned 21 Aug. The local navy air unit (he calls it the "Sagamihara Air
  Corps") kept flying; 2,000 members of the Special Attack Corps "even boasted they would attack the battleship
  Missouri." Prince Takamatsu, sent at the Emperor's wish, persuaded the officers. Kase dates the airfield's handover to
  the evening of 24 Aug and says mishaps were averted "a matter of a bare twelve hours" (see Disagreements).
- p. 265: "It is said" MacArthur told the Emperor at their first meeting no monarch had shown such leadership (hearsay).
- Kase does not mention Wainwright or Percival anywhere in the ceremony chapter.

**[7]** General Headquarters, SCAP (MacArthur's General Staff), *Reports of General MacArthur*, Vol. I Supplement,
*MacArthur in Japan: The Occupation: Military Phase* (Washington: GPO, 1966; CMH Pub 13-4 facsimile 1994). Public
domain. https://archive.org/details/ReportsOfGeneralMacarthurMacarthurInJapan (page numbers checked against
archive.org's page index; p. 100 checked against the page image). Written by MacArthur's own staff: favourable to him.
- pp. 22-23: The envoys' return from Manila was delayed when one plane "had to be 'ditched'"; Prime Minister Higashikuni
  "sat up all night wondering 'whether our invigorated Tokko Tai (air attack units) had shot them down.'" He was "far
  happier to learn that General MacArthur's terms for his nation were not so severe as he had feared." Kawabe reported
  at the palace; "The Emperor was quite relieved" (citing Higashikuni, *My Memoirs*, 1947).
- p. 23: "General MacArthur's humane and considerate occupation policies unquestionably took Japan by complete
  surprise."
- p. 24: Yomiuri reported that within three days of the surrender Tokyo citizens feared: "American soldiers would loot
  Japan. Americans would rob Japan of all the food. Women and girls would be violated. All men would be killed. What was
  left of Tokyo would be devastated." The decision to bring the troops' food from America "produced a profound,
  completely unexpected, and highly favorable impression." People heard Allied planes and "from force of habit ran to
  their air raid shelters. There were no bombs" (the POW supply drops).
- p. 24: initial target date at Atsugi postponed from 26 to 28 Aug by weather.
- p. 26: At Atsugi "Japanese planes sat helplessly stripped of their propellers." A detachment of the Naval Security
  Corps "armed with clubs" guarded the field. First plane, a C-46, touched down "at 0828" on 28 Aug, followed by fifteen
  others. Lt. Gen. Seizo Arisue reported in his unpublished "Memoirs of Atsugi" that the planes landed downwind despite
  the wind sock the Japanese had put up.
- p. 27: Col. Charles P. Tench, GSC, G-3 Section, commanded the advance party; "General Arisue stiffly saluted Colonel
  Tench"; Tench declined the offered food, having brought his own rations. Party of "approximately 150 officers and
  men"; second flight 0935, third 1100; "30 officers and 120 men." The Japanese were amazed that "within forty-five
  minutes" Signal Corps radios were on the air to Okinawa. "less than 200 men with light weapons" in a country with
  "three to four million soldiers" still under arms. Arisue: he had "never had to use my head and body for so long to
  such a great extent." The occupation was "the first by a foreign army in the recorded history of that nation."
- p. 28: 11th Airborne first echelon landed at Atsugi early 30 Aug, aircraft "at three-minute intervals."
- p. 29: Eichelberger, arriving six hours after the first troops: "This is the beachhead where I was supposed to land in
  the invasion of Japan ... I certainly never expected to get here by plane without a shot being fired." "Shortly
  before 1400" MacArthur stepped from the plane and told Eichelberger, Swing and the 11th Airborne veterans: "Melbourne
  to Tokyo was a long road but this looks like the payoff." (citing GHQ Monthly Summary of Operations, Aug 45, p. 12).
  Fn 8: "General MacArthur had ordered that there be no formal Japanese reception party."
- p. 30: "The fifteen miles of roadway between the airfield and the New Grand Hotel were lined with thousands of armed
  Japanese soldiers and policemen. They stood at attention but faced away from the road, an additional security measure
  which was customarily used only for the movements of the Japanese Imperial family." The 188th Para-glider Infantry
  also guarded the whole road. GHQ set up in the Yokohama Customs House.
- p. 31: 4,200 troops and 123 planes moved that day. Dinner at the New Grand: "hamburgers and grapes" (citing Carl
  Mydans' notes). Next day paratroopers saw "women and children ran into hiding, while men and boys saluted and bowed."
- p. 32: "An umbrella of 400 B-29's and more than 1500 fleet carrier planes circled above the bay." Fn 17: MacArthur
  signed "at 0908 and four minutes earlier by the Japanese representatives." Plate 12 caption: Wainwright, "defender of
  Corregidor until its fall in May 1942," stands behind MacArthur.
- p. 92 (plate 30): Wainwright and Percival "after release from PW Camps in Manchuria."
- pp. 94-98: B-29 supply drops by Twentieth Air Force "from 27 August to 20 September 1945"; planes marked "PW
  Supplies" under each wing; packages in three-, seven- and ten-day units, 200 persons per plane. Medical supplies for
  31,000 and quartermaster stores for 50,000 packed by 18 Aug. Japanese "Yellow List" of 73 camps received 27 Aug.
  "900 effective sorties and dropped 4,470 tons of supplies. Successful drops were made to 158 camps." Fn 22: Third Fleet
  report, 30 Aug: "PW's are tremendously appreciative of food drops ... some packages without parachutes have been
  plummeting through roofs"; Japanese reports of casualties from falling drums (Naoetsu, Hanaoka, Maizuru).
  Table (p. 97): 8 aircraft lost, 77 casualties.
- p. 98: Third Fleet had ready "three hospital ships, thirty doctors, ninety corpsmen, and clothing and food for 3,000
  men." The Red Cross reported "150 were seriously ill in Shinagawa camp hospital."
- p. 99 fn 34: MacArthur to Halsey, 25 Aug: "it is not believed advisable for Third Fleet to undertake any unilateral
  action re assistance and evacuation prisoners of war Tokyo Bay area."
- p. 100: Red Cross reported "200 aviation personnel, in extremely bad condition at Omori camp on the waterfront."
  Japanese figure: "6,125 Allied PW's in the Tokyo area, of which 417 were bedridden." On 29 Aug Nimitz authorized
  Halsey to act. "The first prisoners to be evacuated were from Omori, Shinagawa, and Ofuna camps."
- p. 100, radio from Commander Task Group 30.6 (Simpson) to Commander Third Fleet, 30 Aug 1945 (Rad 292310Z), verbatim:
  "There has never been a blacker hellhole than the POW hospital we are now evacuating one-half mile north of mooring.
  Approximately 500 have now (30 August) been processed to Benevolence including fracture, open wounds, concussions,
  burns and in general the worst malnutrition imaginable. Bestial beatings were common especially at Ofuna,
  inquisitorial den of brutism. The cheers of POW as our boat hove into sight brought tears to our eyes. Operations are
  proceeding according to plan. The bath, medical care, chow, interview, and clean bed routine is a merciful machine of
  efficiency. . . . Preliminary list of POW will be sent Commander 3rd Fleet in morning."
- p. 100 fn 35, messages painted on camp roofs: "SOS," "406 Prisoners," "503 Men Here," "Have Hospital Cases Here,"
  "Drop Radio Please," "Thanks Yanks, Aussies," "PW US PW 1734 Men," "Men from Corregidor, Bataan Thank Wasp," "Hong
  Kong Men Thank You," "All Left This Camp."
- p. 100 fn 36, Nimitz to MacArthur, 29 Aug: "urge that you immediately authorize Halsey to take immediate action ...
  COM3rdFLT is hereby authorized, if no reply is received from you by 1300 Tokyo time today, to initiate such local
  action as to POW's as humanitarian considerations require." MacArthur concurred the same day.
- p. 100 fn 37, the Japanese complaint (radio of 1 Sep): "On the evening of 29 August 1945, a U. S. Naval Landing unit of
  about 150 men under the command of a Rear Admiral, broke into the Shinagawa camp of the Allied War Prisoners, Tokyo,
  and forcibly led away the prisoners."
- p. 100: "more than 1,000 emaciated and starving Allied war prisoners were taken aboard the USS Ancon" at the camp near
  Yokosuka. p. 101: among them "survivors of Wake and Bataan"; "At least 80 percent of them were suffering from
  malnutrition"; Ofuna "had been the Gestapo center of Japan."
- p. 101 (plate 32): photo of Omori barracks, 30 Aug 1945, with Leon S. Johnston (Atlanta) and Harry R. Sanders (Terre
  Haute), "interned for over three years."
- p. 101: Recovery teams "released, processed, and arranged transportation for 32,624 prisoners of war, all of them
  liberated within a period of three weeks."

## Disagreements

1. **Shigemitsu's leg.** Right leg: Wikipedia [16] and NHHC H-Gram [4]. Left leg: Kase [6] p. 7 and Halsey [8] p. 282.
   Script: "he had lost a leg to a bomb in Shanghai in 1932" (no side). Kase says "wooden leg"; Halsey's story that
   Hirohito gave him the leg is hearsay.
2. **Perry's flag.** NHHC [4]: Perry's flagship USS Susquehanna, July 1853, now at the Naval Academy Museum. Kase [6]
   p. 13 and Wikipedia [19]: Powhatan. Halsey [8]: "flown in 1854." Murray [14]: "1853-54." Best sourced: NHHC (the
   Navy owns the flag). Script: "the flag Commodore Perry flew in Tokyo Bay in 1853."
3. **The flag at the masthead.** Kase [6] p. 6 repeats the story that it had flown over the White House on Pearl Harbor
   day and at Casablanca, Rome and Berlin. Capt. Murray [14] and H-Gram [4]: a plain GI-issue flag. Use Murray; Kase's
   version can be shown as "what the Japanese were told."
4. **Flyover.** 400 B-29s and 1,500 carrier planes: Kase [6] p. 10 and *Reports of General MacArthur* [7] p. 32 (as an
   "umbrella" over the bay). 450 carrier planes then B-29s: NHHC [5]; 450 carrier planes and 600 B-29s: H-Gram [4];
   450 from TF 38: Halsey [8]. "Over 800 ... including 462 B-29s": Wikipedia [19]. Safest: "hundreds of carrier planes
   and B-29s"; the Navy's own figure for the carrier planes is 450.
5. **Pens.** Five (Wainwright [10], Citino [17], Lengel [18]) or six (H-Gram [4], Wikipedia [14]); Wainwright counts
   five black plus Mrs. MacArthur's red one, so six in all. Who got what differs ([17] vs [14]); see wainwright-pen file.
6. **When Wainwright and Percival were called.** Wainwright [10]: MacArthur motioned to them as he came through the
   lineup, to stand behind him as he went to the microphone; later, halfway through his signature, he asked Wainwright to
   step forward. Citino [17]: MacArthur asked them to "step forward and accompany me while I sign." The typed speech [1]
   has neither line. **Settled by the NBC recording [22]:** after the Japanese signed, MacArthur said the Supreme
   Commander would now sign, then "Will General Wainwright and General Percival step forward and accompany me while I
   sign?" (Wainwright named first; Citino has Percival first). They may also have stood behind him from the start, as
   Wainwright wrote; the call came at the signing (between 9:06 and 9:08).
7. **MacArthur's words at Atsugi.** "Melbourne to Tokyo was a long road but this looks like the payoff" (GHQ summary
   via [7] p. 29). "It has been a long, hard road, but this looks like the payoff" (AP [11]). "Bob, this is the payoff"
   (Eichelberger [9] p. 262). "Bob, from Melbourne to Tokyo is a long way, but this seems to be the end of the road"
   (MacArthur 1965 [20]). Best: the 1945 wording [7]/[11].
8. **Time MacArthur landed.** "Shortly before 1400" [7]; "About two in the afternoon" [9]; "2 p.m." [11]. Agree.
9. **Distance Atsugi - Yokohama.** 15 miles to the New Grand [7]; "twenty miles" [9] and NHHC [5]; Wainwright "about
   ten miles out of Yokohama" [10]. Drive took "two hours" [9].
10. **Who stalled at the table.** Halsey [8]: Shigemitsu was "stalling" with hat, gloves, cane, pen. H-Gram [4]: he was
    "confused about where to sign" and Sutherland showed him. NHHC caption [5]: Kase "assisting" him. Kase [6] says only
    that Shigemitsu looked up at him and he checked his watch. Script: he fumbled; Sutherland pointed; Kase checked the
    time. Do not call it deliberate stalling.
11. **Umezu and suicide.** H-Gram [4] states it took the Emperor's intervention to stop his suicide; Kase [6] pp. 5-6
    has it as "so it is reported" (he threatened harakiri; the Emperor persuaded him). Use Kase's hedge.
12. **The Atsugi rebellion.** NIDS [12]: 16-23 Aug; Kozono sedated and removed early 21 Aug; about 80 men took 34
    planes; over by 22 Aug. Wikipedia [13]: "33 pilots left." Kase [6] p. 264: airfield handed over evening of 24 Aug,
    2,000 Special Attack men threatened the Missouri, Takamatsu persuaded "the officers," "a bare twelve hours" to spare
    (dramatic and probably compressed; NIDS is the specialist study). Eichelberger [9] says there was "bitter fighting"
    with casualties when propellers were removed: not in NIDS (**FLAG**, don't use). Kozono's death: 1960 [13] vs
    "November 1965, aged 58" [12] (1960 fits his age). Officers' sentences: five years, pardoned April 1947 [12] vs
    four to eight years, amnestied Nov 1946 [13]. Prefer [12].
13. **The Atsugi banner.** "Welcome to Atsugi from Third Fleet" (H-Gram [4], dated 27 Aug); "Welcome to the U.S. Army from
    the Third Fleet" (Halsey [8], "I am told"). Pilot unnamed in both.
14. **How many prisoners the first night.** Halsey [8]: first aboard Benevolence at 1910 on 29 Aug, 794 by midnight.
    Simpson's radio [7]: "Approximately 500" processed to Benevolence by 30 Aug. AP [11]: 500 freed incl. Boyington.
    H-Gram [4]: 1,500 from Omori "by the next day" (probably all Tokyo Bay camps). Totals: 19,000 in eastern Honshu in 14
    days (Halsey [8]); 32,624 POWs in Japan within three weeks [7]. Japanese count: 6,125 in the Tokyo area, 417
    bedridden [7].
15. **"Aomori" vs "Omori."** NHHC photo captions [5] say "Aomori prison camp, near Yokohama"; Aomori is a city in
    northern Honshu. The H-Gram [4], [7] and AP [11] say Omori. The captions are wrong; say Omori.
16. **Who broke into Shinagawa.** The Japanese radio [7] fn 37 says a unit "under the command of a Rear Admiral";
    the Navy's own accounts [4][5][8] name Commodore Simpson. A Japanese complaint, not a U.S. record of rank.
17. **Kase's words on the staring eyes.** Yale 1950 [6]: "Never had I realized that staring eyes could hurt so much."
    Lengel [18]: "Never have I realized that the glance of glaring eyes could hurt so much" (probably another edition or
    his report). Use [6].
18. **Where the Missouri lay.** "some eighteen miles off shore" (Kase [6] p. 6); "4.5 nautical miles northeast" of Perry's
    anchorage (H-Gram [4]). Not in conflict; don't give a distance from shore.
19. **Courtesies to the Japanese.** Halsey [8] ordered no coffee or cigarettes on the destroyer, then "had been directed
    to revoke it" (by whom, he doesn't say). At the end the Japanese were piped off "with the honors due those now
    officially at peace" [5], while the H-Gram [4] says "no salutes or handshakes." Both can be true: piped, not saluted.
20. **Kawabe's planes.** H-Gram [4]: "Bataan 1" and "Bataan 2," white with green crosses. [7] p. 22: one plane was
    "ditched" on the way back; Kase [6] p. 264: "a forced landing." Agree in substance.

## Named people

  Emperor [6]. Left the ministry 1948, published *Journey to the Missouri* 1950, Japan's first ambassador to the UN
  (1955), ambassador to Yugoslavia, retired 1960; interviewed for *The World at

  our sovereign" [16].

  lightest sentence; paroled 1950; Foreign Minister again 1954-56; died Jan 1957 [16]. Kase's footnote confirms the
  seven-year sentence of 12 Nov 1948 [6] p. 5 fn 1.
- **Gen. Yoshijiro Umezu** (1882-1949), Chief of the Army General Staff, signed at 9:06 [5]. Had opposed surrender;
