# Story outline: the Missouri surrender (~30 min, 19 acts)

Source numbers refer to `facts_raw.md`. Every beat below has a source; where a beat rests on one witness, it says so.
The pens and the Wainwright reunion were the core of our earlier episode (wainwright-pen): here they get one beat each,
and the episode is carried by Kase, Atsugi and the Tokyo Bay prisoner rescue.

Spine: one Japanese diplomat, Toshikazu Kase, arrives on the Missouri sure he will be humiliated; by nightfall he asks the
Emperor whether Japan, had it won, would have shown the same mercy. Around him, the 18 days that decided whether the
surrender would hold: a mutiny at the airfield, prisoners dying in camps by the bay, and two starved generals flown in
from Manchuria.

## Title options (WWII Box format)

1. **Japan Expected MacArthur to Humiliate Them on the Missouri. Then He Called Two Prisoners Forward.** (working title)
   Supported: Kase "expected the worst humiliation" [6] p. 9 and "the torture of the pillory" p. 7; MacArthur's spoken
   "Will General Wainwright and General Percival step forward and accompany me while I sign?" on the NBC recording [22]
   and in Citino [17]. Caution: no source says the Japanese reacted to the two generals (Kase never mentions them). The
   script must not claim the call was aimed at the Japanese or that they understood it; it can say the two men who had
   surrendered in 1942 now stood at the table where Japan surrendered.
2. **Japan Expected MacArthur to Humiliate Them. Then He Spoke of Freedom, Tolerance and Justice.** The most tightly
   sourced: the speech [1] and Kase's reaction [6] pp. 8-9.
3. **Japan Expected the Americans to Loot and Kill. Then They Came for the Prisoners First.** The Yomiuri list of fears
   [7] p. 24; the Navy's rescue started 24 hours early, before MacArthur arrived [4], and the Japanese protested that
   the sailors "broke into" Shinagawa [7] p. 100.

Recommendation: keep title 1 (searchable names, POW angle, the owner's working title) and pair it with a thumbnail of
Wainwright behind MacArthur at the table (photo USA C-4627 [4]).

## Strongest one-sentence hook

At 8:56 on the morning of 2 September 1945, a Japanese diplomat climbing onto the deck of the USS Missouri saw a row of
little Rising Sun flags painted on the ship, one for each Japanese plane her gunners had shot down, and began to cry,
certain he had been brought there to be humiliated. [5][6] pp. 7-8

## Acts

Times are rough targets (about 1:35 per act). Subscribe line and button at act 10 (midpoint).

**1. Cold open: the little flags (0:00-1:40). Kase.**
8:56, 2 Sep. Eleven Japanese climb the Missouri's ladder; Shigemitsu first, limping on his artificial leg [4][5][6] p. 7.
Sailors on every turret; Nimitz: every point of vantage taken [3]. Kase: "A million eyes..." and the pillory [6] p. 7.
He sees the painted Rising Suns, tries to count them, tears [6] pp. 7-8. They wait "like penitent boys awaiting the
dreaded schoolmaster" [6] p. 7.
Open loop: before the day was over this man would put a question to the Emperor of Japan that no one in Tokyo wanted to
hear. To understand it we go back 18 days.

**2. What Japan feared (1:40-3:15). Prince Higashikuni.**
15 Aug, the broadcast. Within three days Tokyo believed the Americans would loot, take the food, violate women and girls,
kill all men [7] p. 24. The new prime minister, Higashikuni [6] p. 263, [7] p. 22. Nobody had ever occupied Japan [7]
p. 27.
Loop: the first test was a plane carrying Japan's envoys, and it did not come home on time.

**3. The white bombers (3:15-4:50). Lt. Gen. Torashiro Kawabe.**
19 Aug: two Betty bombers painted white with green crosses, call signs "Bataan 1" and "Bataan 2," fly Kawabe's 16 men
to Ie Shima, then by C-54 to Manila; "There were no negotiations" [4]; film NPC-15471. On the way home one plane is
ditched; Higashikuni sits up all night wondering if Japan's own attack units had shot them down [7] p. 22, [6] p. 264.
The Emperor "was quite relieved" [7] p. 22.
Loop: Higashikuni's fear was not paranoia. At the very airfield where MacArthur was to land, a navy captain had declared
war on the surrender.

**4. "A divine nation does not surrender" (4:50-6:25). Capt. Yasuna Kozono.**
The 302nd Air Group at Atsugi, which fought until the morning of 15 Aug [12]. Kozono tells his officers the Emperor was
deceived; radios the whole navy that its leaders have lost the right to command; about 1,000 men and 130 aircraft [12].
16-18 Aug: leaflets dropped over cities and bases [12][13]. Kase: some boasted they would attack the Missouri [6] p. 264.
Loop: the navy didn't dare send troops. What stopped him was something else.

**5. Fever (6:25-8:00). Kozono; Cdr. Hideo Sugawara; Prince Takamatsu.**
Malaria returns; delirium; sedated; on the 21st carried off heavily sedated to Yokosuka naval hospital [12]. 20 Aug: the
Emperor's brother tells Sugawara and Yoshino the surrender is the Emperor's own wish [12]. 21 Aug: about 80 men fly 34
planes to Army fields; brought back and arrested; propellers off, fuel drained [12]. One week later the Americans find
"planes sat helplessly stripped of their propellers" [7] p. 26. Kozono: life sentence, paroled 1950, died 1960 [12][13].
Loop: while Atsugi was being emptied, B-29s came back over Japan, and people ran for the shelters out of habit.

**6. Bombers without bombs (8:00-9:35). Dr. Marcel Junod (Red Cross).**
Twentieth Air Force supply drops from 27 Aug; "PW Supplies" painted under the wings; three-, seven-, ten-day packs
[7] pp. 94-96. "There were no bombs" [7] p. 24. Junod presses Tokyo to raise rations [7] p. 97 fn 27. Messages painted
on roofs: "Men from Corregidor, Bataan Thank Wasp," "Hong Kong Men Thank You," "All Left This Camp" [7] p. 100 fn 35.
Some drums fell without parachutes through roofs [7] p. 95 fn 22. Totals: 900 sorties, 4,470 tons, 158 camps; eight
planes lost [7] pp. 97-98.
Loop: food could be dropped. Men could not be lifted out by parachute, and Tokyo said 417 of them could not get out of
bed.

**7. The first 150 (9:35-11:10). Col. Charles P. Tench.**
28 Aug, 0828: a C-46 lands at Atsugi downwind, ignoring the wind sock (Arisue's memoir) [7] p. 26. Lt. Gen. Arisue
"stiffly saluted"; Tench declines the food, he brought his own [7] p. 27. A banner from a Navy pilot who landed the day
before against orders [4]. Radio on the air within 45 minutes; fewer than 200 men in a country with three to four
million soldiers [7] p. 27. Same day the fleet enters Tokyo Bay, 258 ships [4].
Loop: out in the bay, a picket boat heard someone shouting from the beach.

**8. "Go ahead" (11:10-12:45). Adm. William F. Halsey; Fleet Adm. Chester Nimitz.**
Two escaped British prisoners picked up in Sagami Bay; a Swiss Red Cross doctor confirms their story [8] pp. 277-78.
Red Cross: 200 airmen in very bad shape at Omori; 150 seriously ill at Shinagawa [7] pp. 98-100. MacArthur had said on
25 Aug that unilateral Third Fleet action was "not believed advisable" [7] p. 99 fn 34. 29 Aug, Nimitz: act if no reply
by 1300 [7] p. 100 fn 36; to Halsey, "Go ahead" [8] p. 278.
Loop: that afternoon boats went up the estuary to the camp the Japanese called Omori.

**9. "You have no authority, period!" (12:45-14:20). Cdr. Harold Stassen; Commodore Rodger Simpson.**
The commandant of Omori: "I have no authority to release these men." Stassen, unarmed: "You have no authority, period!"
[8] p. 278. Prisoners waving U.S., British and Dutch flags (photo 80-G-490444) [5]; "delirious with joy, many of them
weeping" [11]. Boyington says he was beaten with a baseball bat [11]. First RAMPs aboard Benevolence at 1910; 794 by
midnight [8] p. 279. Senior POW: Cdr. Arthur Maher of USS Houston [4].
Loop: next morning Simpson radioed the fleet a sentence no one could forget.

**10. The blackest hellhole (14:20-16:00). Simpson; Pvt. Alfred Sorenson. [subscribe ask here]**
The TG 30.6 radio, read in full: "There has never been a blacker hellhole..." [7] p. 100. Sorenson, captured on
Corregidor, with his first full meal (photo 80-G-490452) [5]. Ancon: more than 1,000 from the camp near Yokosuka [7]
p. 100. Then the Japanese protest: a Navy unit "broke into the Shinagawa camp ... and forcibly led away the prisoners"
[7] p. 100 fn 37.
Loop: the next afternoon the man Japan feared most stepped out of a plane at Atsugi.

**11. The Bataan lands (16:00-17:35). Gen. Robert Eichelberger.**
30 Aug: C-54s every three minutes [9] p. 262; 4,200 troops by nightfall [7] p. 31. About 2 p.m. MacArthur's C-54
Bataan; corncob pipe [9] p. 262. "Melbourne to Tokyo was a long road but this looks like the payoff" [7] p. 29, [11]
(other versions: Disagreement 7). He had ordered no Japanese reception party [7] p. 29 fn 8. The sidearms story and
Churchill's remark only as "is said to" (**FLAG** [21]).
Loop: then fifteen miles of road, lined with armed Japanese soldiers.

**12. Backs to the road (17:35-19:10). Eichelberger; Lt. Gen. Seizo Arisue.**
Soldiers and police at attention, facing away, an honour kept for the Imperial family [7] p. 30; "almost all of them
stood with their backs toward us" [9] p. 263. Two hours for twenty miles; an old car that broke down [9] p. 263, [4].
Eichelberger "did not draw an easy breath" until the New Grand Hotel [9]; dinner: hamburgers and grapes [7] p. 31.
Animated road scene here.
Loop: the next evening a thin general on a cane walked into that dining room.

**13. Out of Manchuria (19:10-20:45). Lt. Gen. Jonathan Wainwright; Lt. Gen. Arthur Percival.**
At Mukden the OSS officer Lamar brings MacArthur's invitation [10] (15 Nov). Manila, 30 Aug: "pale and leaning on a
cane": "This is wonderful" [11]; film 111-ADC-5188. Atsugi 31 Aug: Japanese generals snap to attention and salute him,
"after years of humiliating servility before Jap privates" [10] (16 Nov). One line on the reunion (see our earlier
episode) [10][23].
Loop: in Tokyo, nobody wanted the job of signing.

**14. Nobody volunteers (20:45-22:20). Gen. Yoshijiro Umezu; Mamoru Shigemitsu.**
"Nobody wanted to volunteer for the odious duty" [6] p. 5. Konoye refuses; Shigemitsu accepts as a mark of the
Emperor's trust [6] p. 5. Umezu, it was reported, went white and threatened harakiri until the Emperor persuaded him
[6] pp. 5-6 (H-Gram's stronger version [4], Disagreement 11). Names kept from the press for fear of extremists [6] p. 6,
[14].
Loop: they left Tokyo before dawn, in cars with the flags taken off.

**15. Without flag or sword (22:20-23:55). Kase; Lt. John Bremyer; Capt. Stuart Murray.**
5 a.m. through miles of ruins [6] p. 4; swords left at the governor's office; "Diplomats without flag and soldiers
without sword" [6] p. 6. Destroyer Lansdowne [4][6]. On the Missouri: Perry's 31-star flag flown 9,000 miles by
Bremyer, hung backwards because of its linen backing [4]; Murray: the ensign was "just a plain GI-issue flag" [4][14];
sailors rehearsing with broomsticks [4]. On deck, freed prisoners: Maher, Condit, Harris, Shaw, plus Wainwright and
Percival [22][8] p. 283.
Loop: back to the moment of the little flags; and then something the Japanese had been told would not happen.

**16. The speech nobody expected (23:55-25:30). Kase; MacArthur.**
9:02 [5]. Kase: "it was our understanding that there would be no speech" [6] p. 8; the paper trembling [6] p. 8,
Halsey: hands shook [8] p. 282. Play MacArthur's own voice [22] over the key lines [1]: "Nor is it for us here to meet
... in a spirit of distrust, malice or hatred," "freedom, tolerance and justice." Kase: "For me, who expected the worst
humiliation, this was a complete surprise" [6] p. 9.
Loop: then MacArthur sat down to sign, and called two names.

**17. Two prisoners at the table (25:30-27:05). Wainwright, Percival, Shigemitsu, Col. Lawrence Moore Cosgrave.**
Shigemitsu fumbles with hat, gloves, pen; MacArthur sends Sutherland [22][4]; 9:04, Kase checks his watch [6] p. 1;
Umezu signs standing, fast [8] p. 283, [22]. MacArthur's voice: "Will General Wainwright and General Percival step
forward and accompany me while I sign?" [22]; first pen to Wainwright, second to Percival [22][10]. "Start 'em now!"
to Halsey [8] p. 283. Cosgrave, blind in one eye, signs on France's line [15]; Isitt "an humble footnote" [8].
Loop: MacArthur stood up and said four words to the Japanese.

**18. "These proceedings are closed" (27:05-28:40). MacArthur; Kase.**
"Let us pray that peace be now restored to the world..." and "These proceedings are closed" in his voice [22], said
"curtly" to the delegation [6] p. 10. The sun breaks through ([22], [6] p. 10). 450 carrier planes, then the B-29s
[5][8]. 9:29: the Japanese piped over the side "with the honors due those now officially at peace" [5]. In the
destroyer's gun room Kase hears MacArthur on the radio: "Today the guns are silent ... those grim days of Bataan and
Corregidor" [2][6] pp. 10-12.
Loop: on that destroyer Kase wrote his report for the Emperor.

**19. The question (28:40-30:00). Kase; Emperor Hirohito; Shigemitsu.**
Shigemitsu carries Kase's report straight to the palace, "where the Emperor was anxiously waiting." Kase's question:
could Japan, had it won, have embraced the vanquished "with a similar magnanimity? Clearly, it would have been
different." The Emperor "nodded with a sigh in agreement" [6] p. 13 (say plainly: this is Kase's account, told to him by
Shigemitsu). Coda: Shigemitsu, seven years, paroled 1950; Umezu died in prison 1949; Kase, Japan's first UN ambassador
[16]; 32,624 prisoners freed within three weeks [7] p. 101. End on the question to viewers (comment prompt).

   7-9, with MacArthur's real voice [22].

   Simpson's "blacker hellhole" radio [7] p. 100.
3. The road from Atsugi lined with armed soldiers standing with their backs turned [7] p. 30, [9] p. 263.
4. MacArthur's recorded "Will General Wainwright and General Percival step forward and accompany me while I sign?" [22].
5. The report to the Emperor and the magnanimity question [6] p. 13.
