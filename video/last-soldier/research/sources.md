# Sources: "A German POW Hid in America for 40 Years. Then His Wife Packed Her Bags."

No source, no line. Every fact in `script.md` maps to one of the entries below.

## Main sources
1. **David Holley and Carol McGraw, "Hitler's Last Soldier in U.S. Surrenders in San Pedro: POW Fled N.M. Camp 40 Yrs. Ago,"
   *Los Angeles Times*, 11 Sept. 1985.** Used for:
   - "Hitler's last soldier in America … surrendered in San Pedro this morning"; tears; kept his past secret from his U.S.-born
     wife of 21 years "until early last year, when she became so frightened by his unwillingness to discuss his past that she
     threatened to leave him." Quote: "Her bags were packed and a taxi was waiting." Gaertner, 64. Turned himself in at her urging.
   - The last remaining escaped German POW in the U.S.; INS regional commissioner Harold Ezell: a deportable alien, but married
     to a U.S. citizen for more than 20 years, so his prospects of legal residence and citizenship were excellent; press
     conference in the commissioner's office on Terminal Island.
   - Joined the German army in 1940 at 19; North Africa under Rommel; captured; Ft. Deming, N.M., where he was camp translator.
   - Escaped 21 Sept. 1945 "by creeping under fences and wires, evading sentries and hopping an open Southern Pacific boxcar
     which brought me here to San Pedro, Calif.--my first destination as a free man."
   - Native of Schweidnitz, Silesia, occupied by the Soviet army, today Poland; escaped to avoid repatriation. Quote: "What for
     many was a moment of celebration, was for me a time of horror as I visualized the brutality which awaited me at the hands
     of the Russians … I concluded that only by escaping from the camp could I remain free and safe in the United States."
   - His lawyers' biography: a year "fleeing from real and imaginary pursuers from one West Coast town to the next, working
     frequently as a dishwasher, lumberjack or migrant worker."
   - Perfected his English, Social Security card as Dennis Whiles; Norden near Lake Tahoe; ski instructor in winter,
     construction and sales in summer; met Jean Clarke at a YMCA dance in Palo Alto; married 1964; two children from her
     previous marriage; tennis pro; lived in Boulder, "construction estimator and architectural consultant."
   - Book *Hitler's Last Soldier in America* (Stein & Day, Sept. 1985); the surrender was timed to its release, the publisher
     acknowledged.
2. **Wikipedia, "Georg Gärtner"** (cites Krammer and Gaertner, *Hitler's Last Soldier in America*, 1985; Blumenthal, *New York
   Times*, 11 Sept. 1985; Udell, *Fort Collins Coloradoan*, 2018). Used for: born 18 Dec. 1920, Schweidnitz; captured in Tunis
   1943; calculated the freight schedule; first alias Peter Petersen, a "Norwegian immigrant"; farm work around Fresno; invented
   an orphanage backstory; ski instructor at Sugar Bowl; tennis club at Aptos near Santa Cruz; Hawaii, construction estimator on
   military projects; could not get a passport, which strained the marriage; Army manhunt until 1963; FBI wanted posters from
   1947; his autobiography says he joined the ski rescue of the snowbound *City of San Francisco* in Jan. 1952 and *Life*
   photographed the group; believed his family dead, but his parents had fled to West Germany and were alive in the 1960s,
   keeping his photo on the kitchen table; "surrendered" to Bryant Gumbel on the *Today* show; died 30 Jan. 2013, Longmont,
   Colorado, aged 92.
3. **War History Online, "Afrika Korps' Last Soldier in America"** (popular history, follows the memoir): told no one of his
   plan; escaped while other prisoners watched a movie; never prosecuted or deported; became a U.S. citizen in 2009.
4. **Wikipedia, "German prisoners of war in the United States"**: about 425,000 German POWs in about 700 camps; prisoners
   "expected to go home immediately after the end of the war in 1945," most worked in the U.S. until 1946 and some then spent
   up to three more years as laborers in France and Britain (Carlson 1997); 2,222 escaped, most recaptured within a day (Holley,
   *LA Times*, 1985); seven could not be accounted for at repatriation; the FBI ended its search for Gaertner in 1963.
5. **Wikipedia, "City of San Francisco (train)"**: trapped 13 Jan. 1952 near Yuba Pass in the Sierra Nevada, about 12 feet of
   snow, 196 passengers and 30 crew; freed 19 Jan.; Gaertner among the rescuers.
6. **J. R. Skates, *Mississippi History Now* (MDAH, 2001)**: about 275,000 German and Italian soldiers captured in Tunisia,
   May 1943.

7. **U.S. Department of State, *Foreign Relations of the United States, 1947*, vol. III, doc. 401** (telegram, 13 March 1947):
   the U.S. memorandum of 3 Dec. 1946 asked that the German prisoners of war the U.S. had transferred to France be repatriated
   by 1 Oct. 1947. https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1947v03/d401
8. **Arnold Krammer**, *Nazi Prisoners of War in America* (Stein & Day, 1979), the standard work on German POWs in the U.S.
   (American Historical Association obituary, *Perspectives on History*, April 2019).
9. **Wikipedia, "City of San Francisco (train)"** also: six days to free the train; hundreds of workers and volunteers cleared
   U.S. 40; evacuees walked to vehicles (Nyack Lodge).

## Disagreements
- **Escape date:** 21 Sept. 1945 (Gaertner himself, LA Times), 22 (Wikipedia), 24 (War History Online). Script: September 1945,
  "on the night of September twenty-first" in his own account.
- **Where he died:** Longmont (Wikipedia) vs Loveland (War History Online). Script: "in Colorado".
- **The surrender:** to the INS at San Pedro (LA Times) and, for the cameras, to Bryant Gumbel on *Today* (Wikipedia). Script
  tells both.
- **1952 rescue:** from his memoir (via Wikipedia); told as "he wrote that…".
