# A German POW Hid in America for 40 Years. Then His Wife Packed Her Bags.

<!-- One paragraph = one scene. Lines starting with "##" are act markers and are not narrated. Write numbers as spoken words; years as digits are converted by tools/tts_*.py. -->

## 1. Cold open

Early in 1984, a woman named Jean packed her bags and called a taxi. She had been married for twenty years to a man who would not talk about his past, and it had started to frighten her.

Her bags were packed and a taxi was waiting, her husband later said. So he told her. His name was not Dennis Whiles. It was Georg Gärtner, and he was a soldier of the German army who had escaped from an American prisoner-of-war camp in 1945. The ski instructor, the tennis pro, the man she had married, had been on the run for almost forty years.

About a year and a half later, he walked into an immigration office in California and gave himself up. He was sixty-four years old, and he was the last escaped German prisoner of war still at large in the United States. This is his documented story, and the story of why he ran.

## 2. A soldier from Silesia

Georg Gärtner was born in December 1920 in Schweidnitz, a town in Silesia, in the east of Germany. In 1940, at nineteen, he joined the army. He was sent to North Africa, to serve under Rommel.

In May 1943, the German and Italian armies in Tunisia surrendered. About two hundred and seventy-five thousand soldiers were taken prisoner. Gärtner was one of them, captured in Tunis.

Like hundreds of thousands of others, he was shipped across the Atlantic. By the end of the war, about four hundred and twenty-five thousand German prisoners were held in America, in around seven hundred camps. Gärtner was sent to Camp Deming, in the desert of southern New Mexico. There, he became the camp's translator.

## 3. The wrong kind of peace

In May 1945, Germany surrendered. For most of the prisoners, the end of the war meant one thing: going home. They expected to be sent back right away.

It did not happen quickly. Most worked on in America until 1946, and some were then shipped to Britain and France, to work as laborers for up to three more years. In 1947, the American government was still asking France to send home, by that October, the German prisoners it had handed over. For Gärtner, the problem was not when he would go home. It was where home now was.

Schweidnitz had been taken by the Soviet army. It would soon become part of Poland. Gärtner later said that what for many was a moment of celebration was, for him, a time of horror. He imagined what was waiting for him at the hands of the Russians. He decided that the only way to stay free and safe was to stay in America.

## 4. The boxcar

He studied the railroad near the camp, and worked out when the freight trains came through. He told no one what he was planning.

On the night of September twenty-first, 1945, by his own account, he crept under the fences and wires, slipped past the sentries, and climbed into an open Southern Pacific boxcar. The train carried him west, all the way to San Pedro, the port of Los Angeles. His first stop, he said, as a free man.

He was one of more than two thousand German prisoners who escaped from American camps. Almost all of them were caught, most within a day. When the last prisoners were finally sent home, seven could not be accounted for. Gärtner was one of the men nobody could find.

## 5. A new man

For the next year, by his lawyers' account, he moved from one West Coast town to the next, running from pursuers, real and imaginary. He washed dishes, cut timber, and picked crops alongside migrant workers. At first he called himself Peter Petersen, a Norwegian immigrant.

Then he settled on another name: Dennis Whiles. He perfected his English, got a Social Security card in that name, and invented a story about growing up in an orphanage.

He moved to Norden, in the mountains near Lake Tahoe. In winter he taught skiing. In summer he worked in construction and sales. Meanwhile, the FBI put his face on wanted posters, and the Army kept looking for him until 1963.

In January 1952, a passenger train called the City of San Francisco was trapped by a blizzard in the Sierra Nevada, under about twelve feet of snow, with more than two hundred people on board. It took six days to free the train. Hundreds of workers and volunteers dug through the drifts, and the passengers walked out to waiting vehicles. Gärtner later wrote that he joined the ski rescue. Life magazine photographed the rescuers. Nobody recognized the wanted man among them.

## 6. Jean

At a YMCA dance in Palo Alto, Dennis Whiles met a woman named Jean Clarke. They married in 1964, and he adopted her two children from an earlier marriage.

He worked as a tennis pro and a ski instructor. With Jean, he ran a tennis club near Santa Cruz. They moved to Hawaii, where he worked as a construction estimator on military building projects. But there was always something he could not do. He could not get a passport, and it strained the marriage.

He believed his family in Germany had died. They had not. His parents had fled west, to West Germany, and were still alive in the 1960s. They kept his photograph on the kitchen table.

## 7. The bags

By 1985, the couple were living in Boulder, Colorado, where he worked as a construction estimator. And his wife still knew almost nothing about him.

Early in 1984, Jean became so frightened by his refusal to talk about his past that she threatened to leave him. The bags were packed. The taxi was waiting. Dennis Whiles told her everything.

She did not leave. Instead, she urged him to turn himself in. He began working with the historian Arnold Krammer on a book about his life. Krammer was the scholar who had written the standard history of German prisoners in America. Now one of those prisoners was sitting across the table from him. They called the book Hitler's Last Soldier in America.

## 8. The surrender

In September 1985, Gärtner walked into the office of the Immigration and Naturalization Service on Terminal Island, in San Pedro, the same port where his boxcar had arrived forty years earlier. In front of reporters, with Jean beside him, he told his story, and there were tears on his cheeks.

The timing was not an accident. His surrender was set to coincide with the release of the book, and the publisher said so. On national television, he surrendered once more, this time to the host of the Today show, Bryant Gumbel.

Then came the question of what to do with him. The regional commissioner of the immigration service said he was technically a deportable alien. But he had been married to an American citizen for more than twenty years, and his chances of being allowed to stay were excellent.

## 9. Aftermath

He was never sent away. He was not prosecuted for the escape. He went on living in Colorado, and in 2009, he became a citizen of the United States, the country he had run into as a prisoner sixty-four years earlier.

Georg Gärtner died in January 2013, in Colorado, at ninety-two. For forty years, the files had called him an escaped prisoner of war. In the end, he was simply the last one.

## 10. Echo

In 1945, almost every German prisoner in America was waiting for the same thing: a train and a ship that would take him home. One man looked at the map, saw what home had become, and jumped on a train going the other way. He spent forty years as someone else. It took a packed suitcase and a waiting taxi to bring Georg Gärtner back.
