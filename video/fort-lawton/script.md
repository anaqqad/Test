# An Italian POW Was Hanged in Seattle. The Army Tried 43 Black Soldiers.

## 1. The cable

At about six in the morning on the fifteenth of August, 1944, two military policemen on patrol at Fort Lawton, an Army post in Seattle above the water of Puget Sound, came to the obstacle course at the foot of a bluff. A man was hanging from one of its cables.

He was an Italian prisoner of war. His name was Guglielmo Olivotto. His gravestone says he was born in October 1911. He was thirty-two years old. A few hours earlier, the part of the fort above him had been the scene of a riot.

Within three months, the Army put forty-three Black American soldiers on trial for that night. It was the largest and longest Army court-martial of the Second World War. The prosecutor would later become one of the most famous lawyers in America.

This is the documented story of the night at Fort Lawton, of the report the defense was never allowed to read, and of the sixty-three years it took the Army to admit that the trial had been unfair.

## 2. The fort

Fort Lawton sat on a bluff in the northwest corner of Seattle. In the war it became a port of embarkation, the last stop before the Pacific. A National Register report written in 1978 put it plainly. At the height of World War II, over one million servicemen were processed thru Fort Lawton for the combat zones of the Pacific and the Far East.

The Italians were there because their country had changed sides. In October 1943, Italy declared war on Germany, and the Army asked its Italian prisoners in America to volunteer for service units. Of about fifty-one thousand Italian prisoners in the country, more than forty-five thousand joined.

They were still prisoners, but they worked for the Army. Each unit had between forty and two hundred and fifty men, under an Italian officer. They were paid twenty-four dollars a month, and only a third of it in cash.

At Fort Lawton, the twenty-eighth Italian Quartermaster Service Unit had about two hundred and six men. On some evenings, they went into town.

## 3. The port companies

Next to them lived about six hundred and twelve Black American soldiers, in three port companies. The Army was segregated. At Fort Lawton, the Black soldiers were housed in their own remote part of the fort, right beside the barracks of the Italian prisoners.

On the night of the fourteenth of August, the port companies were in the final preparation for shipment overseas, the next morning.

The Black soldiers served in a segregated Army. The men next door were enemy prisoners, with passes into town. Newspapers at the time blamed resentment of those privileges.

## 4. The riot

At about eleven that night, four Black soldiers were walking back to their barracks. They met a group of Italians. The account the Army later accepted says one of the Americans swore at them and shouted, hey, Italian. An Italian turned around. An American walked toward him with what appeared to be a knife. The Italian hit him with his fist and knocked him to the ground.

Accounts differ on who started it, but every account agrees on the punch that put a Black soldier on the ground.

What came next was not a fistfight. Between one hundred and two hundred men went into the Italian area. The Army's own summary says they carried stones, knives, shovels, clubs, and at least one ax.

Some of the military police were slow to step in. They took the injured Black soldier to the hospital farthest from the fight. The riot went on for about an hour before the military police broke it up. Twenty-four men were taken to the hospital.

## 5. The body

At six the next morning, the two military policemen found Guglielmo Olivotto on the obstacle course, about four hundred feet down a steep path from the scene of the riot.

The doctor who examined the body found no bruises on it. The time of death was put at about midnight. Nobody photographed the body while it was still hanging. Nobody took fingerprints. There were two footprints under the body. The provost marshal put a piece of cardboard over them, and no cast was ever made. Later the rope itself was lost.

## 6. The charges

By the fall, forty-three Black soldiers from the port companies were charged with rioting, which carried a maximum of life in prison. Three of them were also charged with the murder of Guglielmo Olivotto. That carried a maximum of death.

At the same time, the Army sent a general from the Inspector General's office to find out what had gone wrong at the fort. Brigadier General Elliot Cooke took sworn testimony from one hundred and sixty-four witnesses. His report was finished on the twenty-eighth of October, 1944. He called the fort's investigation reprehensible.

The report had consequences, but not for the trial. The fort's commanding officer was relieved, and two white military policemen who had failed to step in were court-martialed. The report itself was stamped confidential.

## 7. The trial

The trial began on the sixteenth of November, 1944. The prosecutor was a lawyer from Texas, Lieutenant Colonel Leon Jaworski.

The defense was a Seattle lawyer in uniform, Major William Beeks, with one assistant. In the words a congressman later wrote into a bill, the defendants were tried in a group, and all forty-three shared two Army defense counsel, who were given just ten days to prepare the defense.

Jaworski had the Cooke report all through the trial. In open court, Beeks asked to see it. Jaworski refused, and the officers on the panel did not step in. The prosecution was still allowed to use statements from it.

The central witness for the prosecution was an Italian prisoner. Army intelligence had already looked at him. The congressman's bill says it plainly: Army intelligence had previously identified him as probably pro-Nazi, a fact not made available to the defense.

There was no murder weapon. No witness and no evidence tied any defendant to the death of Guglielmo Olivotto.

## 8. The verdict

On the eighteenth of December, 1944, the court gave its verdicts. Twenty-eight men were convicted of rioting. Two of them were also convicted of manslaughter. Thirteen were acquitted.

The sentences ran from six months to twenty-five years. All but one of the men who were convicted got a dishonorable discharge. On the nineteenth of April, 1945, an Army review board looked at one of the biggest trial records of the war, and dismissed every appeal without comment.

The men went to prison. By the end of the decade, the last of them was out, carrying a dishonorable discharge for the rest of his life.

## 9. Leon Jaworski

After the war, the prosecutor tried German war criminals, and then became one of the best known lawyers in the country. On the first of November, 1973, Leon Jaworski became the special prosecutor in the Watergate scandal. He took the President's tapes all the way to the Supreme Court. Fort Lawton was not part of the story people told about him.

## 10. The grave

Guglielmo Olivotto was buried in the small post cemetery at Fort Lawton, in an area away from the American graves.

In 1986, a Seattle television reporter named Jack Hamann found his headstone. Hamann made a documentary about the riot. At the time, he believed the Army's version.

Years later, Hamann and his wife Leslie went back to the case. At the National Archives in Maryland, Leslie found the Cooke report, declassified, in a miscellaneous section of the war records.

In 2005, the Hamanns published a book about the case, On American Soil. That summer, Seattle congressman Jim McDermott put a bill before Congress, and its findings told the story the trial had not.

## 11. The ruling

The families of the convicted men had to apply one by one. In October 2007, the Army Board for Correction of Military Records gave its answer. Its three members voted unanimously.

The board wrote that it is unimaginable that two attorneys could fully and freely represent each accused. It called the prosecutor's use of the report the most egregious error in the trial. Its conclusion was that the trial had been fundamentally unfair and improper.

The board did not say who killed Guglielmo Olivotto, and it did not rule on guilt. It set aside the convictions, made the discharges honorable, and ordered the Army to pay what the men had lost.

## 12. The check

Samuel Snow, of Leesburg, Florida, was one of the twenty-eight. After prison, he took a job as a janitor. In November 2007, his back pay arrived.

On the floor of the United States Senate, Senator Bill Nelson of Florida asked the question for him. Mr. President, do you know how much that was? It was seven hundred and twenty-five dollars, 1944 dollars.

There was no interest. Congress added it by law in October 2008. By then, it was too late for Samuel Snow.

## 13. The apology

On Saturday, the twenty-sixth of July, 2008, the Army held a ceremony at Fort Lawton, which was now a city park. An assistant secretary of the Army, Ronald James, stood before the families. He told them the Army was genuinely sorry, and that he was genuinely sorry.

A few days before, a congressman from Washington had told the House what that meant. Only two of the original twenty-eight soldiers are alive today, meaning twenty-six Army veterans passed away before an extreme personal injustice was rectified.

Samuel Snow had come to Seattle for it. He was eighty-four. At the dinner the night before, he felt ill, and he was taken to the hospital. His son went to the ceremony and accepted the honorable discharge for him, and brought it back to him.

According to Senator Nelson, he clutched it in his hands. A few hours later, he died.

## 14. The question

Today, nobody stands convicted of the death of Guglielmo Olivotto. The two manslaughter convictions were set aside with the rest. Jack Hamann points to a white military policeman who was present at every key moment in the forty-eight hours around his death. That is his theory. No court has ever ruled on it.

## 15. What is left

Most of Fort Lawton is Discovery Park now, the biggest park in Seattle, with meadows above the water. Since 2024, two signs there tell the story of that night.

The cemetery stayed with the Army. In it, away from the American graves, there is still a white stone column with an Italian name on it, a long way from Italy, on American soil.
