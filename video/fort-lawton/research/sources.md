# Fort Lawton riot (Seattle, 14–15 August 1944): research sources

## Why this topic
Competitor research, 7 Nov 2026 (YouTube search by views, POW niche):
- Italian-POW formats are pulling views now: "Why Italian POWs Didn't Want to Go Home After the War" (145K, 5 months),
  "Italian POWs Called American POW Camps Almost Paradise" (117K); food and kindness formats dominate (Tennessee "small meal"
  697K, Wisconsin white rice 829K). All tell the same story: America was kind.
- The documented counter-story with an Italian POW at its centre: Fort Lawton, 1944. On YouTube it is almost untouched
  (old clips under 2K views, one new upload with 26 views). It has a famous name (Leon Jaworski, later Watergate) and a
  2007 ruling that the trial was unfair, with public-domain documents to show on screen (Congress, the Senate record).
- Hook: two MPs find an Italian prisoner hanging from an obstacle-course cable; 43 Black soldiers go on trial.
- Not covered by past episodes.

Compiled 2026-11-07. Quotes marked **[read directly]** were copied from the downloaded primary document (text
extracted with pdftotext / antiword). Quotes marked **[via fetch]** came through a web-page fetch and should be
re-checked against the live page before they go on screen. Anything not confirmed is marked **UNVERIFIED**.

Important corrections to the brief:
- The defence counsel was **Maj. William (T.) Beeks**, not "Beeler" (sources 2, 8, 9, 16, 23).
- The apology ceremony was **not in October 2007**. October 2007 was the ABCMR ruling. The Army's apology
  ceremony in Seattle was on **Saturday 26 July 2008** at Discovery Park (sources 8, 14, 16). An earlier
  family ceremony for Booker Townsell, with an Assistant Secretary of the Army present, took place in
  Milwaukee on 19 January 2008 (sources 3, 20).
- Samuel Snow attended the dinner on Friday 25 July 2008 but fell ill and **missed** the ceremony. His son
  accepted the discharge for him, and Snow died hours later (sources 5, 14, 15, 17).

### 1. ABCMR Record of Proceedings, Docket AR20070009496 (Board date 18 October 2007)
- URL (DoD Boards Reading Room mirror of Army BCMR decisions):
  https://boards.law.af.mil/ARMY/BCMR/CY2007/20070009496C071029.doc
- Local copy: `docs/ABCMR-AR20070009496-2007-10-29-Fort-Lawton.doc` (Word .doc, 118,272 bytes, 3 pages).
- The applicant's name is redacted. It is one of the Fort Lawton petitions (inducted 2 May 1944, sentenced
  to 1 year, discharged 17 Oct 1945, ABCMR denied 1949, upgraded to general discharge 1975). Board members:
  John N. Slone (chair), John T. Meixell, David W. Tucker. Vote: all three "GRANT FULL RELIEF".
- Verbatim quotes **[read directly]**:
  - "On 14 August 1944, three port companies, composed of about 612 "Negro" troops, were stationed in the
    northwest section of Fort Lawton, WA." / "...were engaged in the final preparation for shipment overseas
    on the following morning."
  - "The 28th Italian Quartermaster Service Unit, consisting of about 206 troops (prisoners of war who
    volunteered to serve with the U. S. Army after Italy became a non-belligerent), was stationed immediately
    to the west of the port companies."
  - Cause (Army/IG version): "At about 11:00 p.m. on 14 August 1944, four "Negro" Soldiers were on their way
    to their respective barracks. They were met by members of the Italian Service Unit. As the two groups
    passed, at least one of the American Soldiers swore at the Italians, and one of them shouted, "Hey,
    Italian." One of the Italians stopped and turned around. An American walked toward the Italian with what
    appeared to be a knife. The Italian struck the American with his fist, knocking him to the ground."
  - "The number was variously estimated at between 100 and 200. The Americans were armed with stones, knives,
    shovels, clubs of all sizes and description, and at least one ax."
  - "MPs eventually broke up the riot about an hour after it started."
  - "The body of one Italian was found at about 6:00 a.m. the following morning by two MPs, including one of
    the MPs (Private L___) who took the injured "Negro" Soldier to the hospital the previous night. The body
    was found hanging from a cable which was part of the obstacle course."
  - "The cable was about 398 feet by a path down a steep incline over rough terrain from the area of the
    riot. The time of death was estimated as midnight but could have been as early as 11:00 p.m., 14 August
    or as late as 1:00 a.m., 15 August."
  - Autopsy physician "testified that there were no bruises or any contusions on the body such as would come
    from a blow on that body."
  - IG on the MPs: "They also chose to take the injured Soldier to the hospital most distant from the scene of
    the disturbance..."
  - IG on the evidence: "no photographs were taken of the body while it was still hanging, and no effort was
    made to secure fingerprints. The installation Provost Marshal placed a piece of cardboard over two foot
    prints found where the body was hanging, but no casts were made of those prints and they were later
    obliterated."
  - IG: investigating officers "cannot help but believe that the white MP's had, for some undetermined
    reason, agreed amongst themselves not to identify any of the rioters."
  - Why hidden: "The IG report was classified "Confidential" and was not given to the defense prior to or
    during the trial. However, the prosecutor was allowed to use statements at trial..."
  - Defence time: "The lead defense counsel had 9 days from service of the charges on the applicant and on 42
    other accused to prepare for trial. He advised the court at that time that he interviewed 132 witnesses
    and had not interviewed some of his clients since the referral of charges. On the first day of trial, the
    defense requested a 4-day continuance, which was granted by the panel president."
  - "On 18 December 1944, the applicant ... was convicted by a general court-martial, in a joint trial with
    42 other Soldiers and contrary to his pleas, of committing a riot."
  - "Three other Soldiers were convicted of manslaughter (the charge had been premeditated murder) and
    committing a riot. Thirteen Soldiers were acquitted." (see Disagreements: most sources say two.)
  - "The Manual for Courts-Martial, U. S. Army, in effect at the time, did not require confidential reports to
    be provided to the defense."
  - Conclusions: "Despite limitations (13 days to prepare for the defense of 43 defendants; no access to the IG
    report), the Record of Trial showed that the defense team mounted a spirited defense."
  - "In the setting of this case, it is unimaginable that two attorneys could fully and freely represent each
    accused."
  - "The right to prepare for trial is fundamental. To deny this right is to deny a fair trial."
  - "Perhaps the most egregious error occurring in this trial involved the trial counsel's access to and use of
    portions of the IG investigation, a right denied defense counsel despite his many requests."
  - "...such findings would not be proper since the factors described above, overall, rendered the trial
    fundamentally unfair and improper."
  - Remedy: "setting aside his 18 December 1944 conviction and restoring to him all rights, privileges, and
    property lost as a result of that conviction"; honourable discharge dated "2 March 1946"; "paying to him
    (or his estate) all back pay and allowances due as a result of the above corrections."
- Note: the decision does not name Jaworski or Cooke; it calls them "trial counsel" and "the IG report".

### 2. H.R. 3174, 109th Congress (introduced 30 June 2005 by Rep. Jim McDermott)

  - p.2: "(2) Forty-three defendants, all of them African American, were charged with rioting, carrying a

  - p.2: "(4) On August 14, 1944, a member of the 650th Port Company (an African American unit at the fort) had

  - p.3: "...24 men were taken to the hospital for treatment."
  - p.3: "(5) On August 15, 1944, two military policemen on patrol spotted the lifeless body of Italian prisoner

  - p.3: "(6) On October 28, 1944, Brigadier General Elliot D. Cooke of the Army's Inspector General Office

    highlighted the fact that the Army's investigation was deeply flawed and, in the words of the General
    himself, was ``reprehensible''."
  - p.3: "(7) As a direct result of General Cooke's report, the Fort Lawton commanding officer was relieved of
    his command, the fort provost marshal was reassigned, and two white military policemen who failed to
    intervene at the outset of the disturbance were court-martialed."
  - p.4: "(8) The defendants were tried in a group, and all 43 shared two Army defense counsel, who were given
    just 10 days to prepare the defense. The court-martial lasted 24 trial days."
  - p.4: "(9) The Cooke Report and its voluminous transcripts were in the possession of the Army prosecutor,
    Lieutenant Colonel Leon Jaworski, throughout the trial. When defense attorney Major William Beeks requested
    access to the report in open court, Lieutenant Colonel Jaworski refused, and the members of the
    court-martial panel refused to intervene."
  - p.4: "(10) The central prosecution witness was an Italian prisoner of war whom United States Army
    intelligence had previously identified as ``probably pro-Nazi,'' a fact not made available to the defense."
  - p.4–5: "(12) The prosecution was unable to produce a murder weapon at trial, nor did it offer any witness or
    physical evidence linking any of the defendants to the death of the Italian prisoner of war."
  - p.5: "(13) Twenty-eight defendants were found guilty of rioting and two were also found guilty of
    manslaughter. All were sentenced to a period of confinement, for periods ranging from six months to 25
    years, with the average period of confinement to which they were sentenced being 7.5 years, and all but one
    of those convicted received a dishonorable discharge."
  - p.5: "(14) On April 19, 1945, a three-member panel of the United States Army Board of Review, faced with one
    of the most voluminous trial transcripts of the war, summarily dismissed all appeals without comment."

### 10. Wikipedia, "Fort Lawton"
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_Lawton
- **[via fetch]**: Fort Lawton was the second-largest port of embarkation to the Pacific; "more than 1 million
  troops passing through"; "over 1,000 German POWs"; ~5,000 Italian POWs "passed through en route to Hawaii".
  The Army declared 534 acres surplus in 1971; the land went to the city in 1972 and was dedicated as
  **Discovery Park in 1973**. The fort officially closed on 14 Sep 2011, and the rest went to the city in 2012
  "except the military cemetery". NRHP listed 15 Aug 1978 (ref. 78002752). (The article has a
  "needs more citations" tag.)

### 11. Wikipedia, "Leon Jaworski"
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Jaworski
- **[via fetch]**: born 19 Sep 1905, Waco, Texas; died 9 Dec 1982. Served in the Judge Advocate General's
  office and rose to colonel. He later prosecuted war crimes in Germany. "On November 1, 1973, Jaworski became

## Key facts (checklist)

- [x] Riot: night of **14 Aug 1944**, first clash **about 11:00 p.m.**, riot broken up about an hour later (1, 9, 8).
- [x] Units: three Black port companies (about **612 men**, shipping out the next morning) and the **28th Italian
      Quartermaster Service Unit (about 206 men)**, ex-POWs who volunteered after Italy's armistice (1, 4).
- [x] Black soldiers who entered the Italian area: "between 100 and 200" (1), "approximately 200" (2), "several
      dozen" (8), "more than 50" (1944 Seattle Times headline cited by 8).
- [x] Olivotto: Private, Italian POW, **age 33** (21; single source, Hamann via NARA). Found **15 Aug ~6:00 a.m.**
      by two MPs, one of them Pvt. Clyde Lomax, hanging from an obstacle-course cable about 398 feet below the
      riot area, at the foot of Magnolia Bluff (1, 2, 8, 9). Hometown: **UNVERIFIED**.
- [x] Charges: **43** Black soldiers charged with rioting (maximum life); **3** of them also with premeditated
      murder (maximum death) (2, 18).
- [x] Defence: **Maj. William T. Beeks** (later federal judge) plus Howard Noyd; two lawyers for 43 men; **9–10
      days** to prepare (1, 2, 9, 16, 19).
- [x] Prosecutor: **Lt. Col. Leon Jaworski** (2, 18), Watergate special prosecutor from **1 Nov 1973** (11).
- [x] Trial: began **16 Nov 1944** (9; 1944 Seattle Times headline "3 Deny Guilt at Ft. Lawton Trial", 16 Nov
      1944, cited by 8); **24 trial days**, about five weeks (2, 9); convictions dated **18 Dec 1944** (1).
- [x] Largest and longest Army court-martial of WWII (2, 3).
- [x] Verdicts: **28 convicted** of rioting, **2** of those also of manslaughter; **13 acquitted**; **2** had
      charges dropped (2, 8, 18). Sentences **6 months to 25 years**, average 7.5 years; all but one
      dishonourably discharged (2, 18).
- [x] Appeal: Army Board of Review "summarily dismissed all appeals without comment" on **19 Apr 1945** (2).
- [x] IG/Cooke report: Brig. Gen. Elliot D. Cooke, filed **28 Oct 1944**, testimony of **164 witnesses**,
      called the Army's investigation "reprehensible" (2). It found MPs failed to warn the guard, no photos or
      fingerprints, footprints not cast, the site cleaned up (1). The commanding officer (Col. Harry Branson)
      was relieved and the provost marshal reassigned; two white MPs were court-martialed (2, 9).
- [x] Why the defence never saw it: classified "Confidential". Jaworski refused Beeks's request in open court
      and the panel did not intervene, yet Jaworski used IG statements to impeach defence witnesses (1, 2).
- [x] Central prosecution witness: an Italian POW whom Army intelligence called "probably pro-Nazi", not
      disclosed (2).
- [x] Hamann: KING-TV journalist. Found the declassified report at National Archives II with Leslie Hamann
      ("Miscellaneous" section). Book *On American Soil*, Algonquin 2005 (9, 21, 26).
- [x] Congress: H.R. 3174, introduced **30 June 2005** (2). McDermott and Hunter referred the cases to the ABCMR (9, 12).
- [x] Ruling: board met **18 Oct 2007** (1); released **Friday 26 Oct 2007** (13, 22). "fundamentally unfair and
      improper" (1). The press quotes "egregious error" (9, 16, 22; the decision's own words are "the most
      egregious error", source 1).
- [x] Remedy: conviction set aside, honourable discharge, "all back pay and allowances" (1).
- [x] Back pay: Snow's check was **$725** (5, 9, 17), received 29 Nov 2007 (9). No interest.
- [x] Interest fix: H.R. 5130 / S. 2548 (23 Jan 2008) (6); enacted as **Pub. L. 110-417 §592, 14 Oct 2008**,
      retroactive to set-asides on or after 1 Oct 2007 (7).
- [x] Apology: **Sat 26 July 2008**, Discovery Park, near the former Fort Lawton chapel; Asst. Sec. of the Army
      **Ronald James**: "The Army is genuinely sorry. I am genuinely sorry." (8, 14, 16).
- [x] Survivors in 2007–08: **2 of 28** (Samuel Snow, Roy L. Montgomery) (4, 5, 12).
- [x] Snow: of Leesburg, Fla.; born 23 Sep 1923 (17); missed the ceremony; died **Sun 27 July 2008** in Seattle
      (5, 8, 15).
- [x] Olivotto's grave: Fort Lawton Post Cemetery, "in an area away from the American graves" (8); Hamann found
      the headstone in 1986 (9); photographed 2009 (Photos list). The cemetery stayed with the Army in the 2012
      transfer (10).
- [x] Fort Lawton today: most of it is **Discovery Park** (dedicated 1973; 1971–72 transfer); the fort closed
      in 2011 (10, 25).
- [ ] Olivotto's hometown and family in Italy: **UNVERIFIED** (not found).
- [ ] Original Army press release (Oct 2007): **not found**.
- [ ] 1944 newspaper page scans: **not obtained** (bot checks).

## Disagreements

0. **Olivotto's age.** Hamann (21) says 33. His gravestone in the Fort Lawton cemetery (photo, Joe Mabel, CC BY-SA 3.0,
   checked by eye on `assets/stills/olivotto_grave.jpg`) reads "SOLD. ITAL. / OLIVOTTO GUGLIELMO / 2? OTTOBRE 1911 / 14 AGOSTO 1944":
   born October 1911, so 32 at his death. The stone also dates the death to 14 August (the IG put it at about midnight).
   Script follows the stone.

1. **How the fight started.**
   - ABCMR / IG report (1): a Black soldier swore ("Hey, Italian"), one walked toward an Italian "with what
     appeared to be a knife", and the Italian knocked him down with his fist.
   - Wikipedia / Hamann (9): an intoxicated Black soldier exchanged words and "rushed forward"; an Italian
     knocked him out with one punch.
   - H.R. 3174 (2): "a brief fistfight". FODP (16): "a brief drunken fistfight".
   - Snow, as paraphrased in 2002 (23): "An Italian prisoner threw the first punch."
   - HistoryLink (8): an MP "claimed that one of the soldiers blew a whistle and trouble then started". 1944
     newspapers blamed Black soldiers' resentment of the POWs' privileges (8, 22).
   - Best: the ABCMR text (1). It summarizes the sworn IG record and is the most detailed. All versions agree
     that an Italian's punch knocked a Black soldier down.
2. **Days the defence had to prepare.** 10 days (2, 9, 16); 9 days from service of charges (1, 19); "13 days"
   in the ABCMR's own conclusions (1; probably 9 days plus the 4-day continuance); "two weeks" (8). Use: "about
   ten days" or "nine days, plus a four-day delay" (1).
3. **Number charged.** 43 (1, 2, 3, 4, 18) vs 44 (8). HistoryLink's own breakdown (2 dropped + 13 acquitted
   + 28 convicted) adds up to 43, so its "44" looks like a slip. Use **43**.
4. **Manslaughter convictions.** Two (2, 4, 9, 18, 22) vs three (ABCMR 1) vs "three ... first-degree murder"
   (St. Petersburg Times inside 5, clearly wrong). Use **two**: Hamann's primary research and the congressional
   findings agree, and no one was convicted of murder.
5. **Sentences.** 6 months–25 years (2, 9, 12, 18) vs "one to 15 years" (23). Use **6 months to 25 years**.
6. **Ruling date.** Board met 18 Oct 2007 (1); file suffix C071029 suggests it was finalized 29 Oct; publicly
   released Fri 26 Oct 2007 (13, AP); 26 Oct in 9, 22; "October 22, 2007" in 16. Use "October 2007". If you
   need a day: "announced 26 October 2007".
7. **Snow's death date and age.** Died Sun 27 July 2008 (5, 8, 15) vs 26 July (17; Wikipedia 9 "that same
   evening"). Use **27 July 2008**: the US Senate record says "Sunday". Age: 84 (16, 17; born Sept 1923) vs 83
   (15, St. Pete Times). Age at conviction: "19" (P-I and St. Pete in 5) vs 21 by the 1923 birth date (17).
   Avoid stating his age at trial, or say "barely twenty".
8. **Number of Italians.** The 28th ISU had about 206 men (1). "5,000 Italian prisoners billeted at Fort
   Lawton" (23) refers to the total that passed through the post during the war (10). Don't conflate them.
9. **Injured.** 24 hospitalized (2) vs 32 hospitalized (8) vs "four white officers and 20 Italians" (23).
   Prefer 2 (24), or say "more than twenty".
10. **Who found the report and when.** Leslie Hamann, "Miscellaneous" section (21); "until 2002" (St. Pete in
    5); "2001–2005" research (9). Use "early 2000s, at the National Archives in College Park".
11. **Apology ceremony date.** The brief said Oct 2007. The correct date is **26 July 2008** (4, 8, 14, 16).
    A separate 19 Jan 2008 Townsell ceremony took place in Milwaukee (3, 20).
12. **Riot date in NPR 2007 (19):** "August 15th 1944". Wrong; the riot was the night of 14 Aug.
13. **NRHP nomination (25)** mentions only a "German prisoner of war compound". It omits the Italian Service
    Unit, so don't use it for the Italians.
