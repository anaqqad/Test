# The True Story of the Los Baños Raid — the 2,147 Prisoners Japan Was Starving

## 1. Quarter to seven

It is a quarter to seven in the morning, on the twenty-third of February, 1945. On a field inside a prison camp in the Philippines, the off-duty Japanese guards are lining up for their morning exercises. They wear loincloths and sandals. Their rifles are locked in a room next door.

In the camp hospital, an American Navy nurse is watching them. Her name is Dorothy Still. She has been a prisoner for almost three years, and she is starving. She later wrote down what she said that morning. We could do calisthenics too, if we were as well fed as they are.

A patient beside her notices something else. Columns of smoke are rising beyond the fence. Then comes the sound of engines, low and heavy. Nine American transport planes come in over the trees, only a few hundred feet up. And the sky beside the camp fills with parachutes.

The guards have been caught at the one moment of the day when they have no weapons in their hands. That is not luck. Someone told the Americans the exact minute. He had crawled out under the barbed wire five nights earlier.

## 2. Two thousand names

This is the true story of the raid at Los Baños. Inside the camp were two thousand one hundred and forty-seven people on a typed roll: Americans, Britons, Canadians, Australians, Dutch, Poles and Norwegians. Missionaries and nuns, mothers and children, eleven Navy nurses, and a baby three days old. The camp was twenty-five miles behind the Japanese lines.

The version you may have heard ends at nine in the morning, with the cheering. The documented story goes on. The man who really ran the camp got away, and for decades the books gave the wrong date for his death. The plaque at the camp today tells a different story about who freed it. And the town around the camp paid a price the celebrations left out.

Some of it comes from Army reports and a war crimes trial. Some comes from the memory of a single internee. When a scene rests on one person's memory, we will tell you whose.

## 3. A college by a lake

The story starts in Manila, in January 1942. Japanese troops had taken the city a few weeks after Pearl Harbor, and thousands of American and Allied civilians were locked inside the University of Santo Tomas.

By 1943, Santo Tomas was overcrowded. In May, the Japanese sent eight hundred able-bodied men south to build a second camp. The site was the College of Agriculture of the University of the Philippines, at Los Baños, about forty miles from Manila. It sat between a wide lake, Laguna de Bay, and the green slopes of an old volcano, Mount Makiling.

One of the eight hundred was Ben Edwards, a young mechanic from Pan American Airways. Remember his name. In 1944, women and children were trucked down too, and by early 1945 there were more than two thousand people behind the wire.

## 4. Hell's Half-Acre

About three hundred Catholic priests, brothers and nuns lived together in one section, which everyone called Vatican City. The rest of the camp had a name for their side too. Hell's Half-Acre.

Among them were eleven women of the Navy Nurse Corps, under Chief Nurse Laura Cobb, who ran the hospital. And there was Frank Buckles, a veteran of the First World War. He would lose fifty pounds in this camp, and live to be a hundred and ten, the last American veteran of that war.

According to the military historian Gordon Rottman, the first two Japanese commandants were lenient. Then, in July 1944, the camp got a new commandant, and a new supply officer.

## 5. Konishi

The commandant was Major Iwanaka. The internees remembered him as an old man who spent his days in a kimono and sandals, potting geraniums, painting watercolors and writing haiku. Some of them thought he was senile. He was never known to overrule his supply officer. And so, in practice, the camp belonged to that officer.

His name was Sadaaki Konishi, a warrant officer. He was twenty-eight years old, five foot four, a farmer from Fukuoka before the war. And he controlled the camp's food. Years later, an internee named Robert Wheeler described him in four words. A sadist, and terribly mean.

Internees told the historian Bruce Henderson that Konishi once let starving men carry hundred-pound sacks of rice into the camp, for more than an hour. Then he ordered them to carry every sack back. What is documented is what happened to their food.

The camp doctor, Dana Nance, kept count. In October 1944, each internee was getting eight hundred and eighty-one calories a day, almost two thirds less than the month before. On the first of November, Nance wrote to the commandant, in capital letters.

The camp urgently needs more food. It did not get more food. After the war, Army investigators found that the ration fell from about four hundred grams of food a day per person in September 1944, to a hundred and fifty grams or less by February 1945. That is about five ounces of food a day.

## 6. You'll be eating dirt

At Thanksgiving 1944, Red Cross packages for the internees were held back. They remembered Konishi telling their committee: Before I'm done, you'll be eating dirt. Someone put up a sign on a patch of lawn. It said: Keep off the grass. We eat it.

They did eat it. They ate weeds, and they ate the camp's cats and dogs. In late November, Dr. Nance went to Konishi. If you fail to remedy this situation, he told him, it can only mean that you are deliberately adopting a starvation policy. Konishi's answer, as Nance remembered it, was four words. Nothing can be done.

By the start of 1945, people were dying every week, and the camp had to double its gravediggers. A girl named Sally Bateman, about thirteen, said many years later that some people died with food under their beds. They had been saving it, in case worse days were coming. Others collected recipes, for the day they would get out.

## 7. Freedom Week

Then, in January 1945, the Americans were coming back to the Philippines. On the sixth of January, the guards burned their records. The next morning, they handed the camp over to the internees' committee, and marched away.

For one week, Los Baños was free. The internees raised the American and British flags. American Navy fighter planes flew over the camp. Then, on the fourteenth of January, the guards came back. The rations were cut again. And the commandant gave an order. Anyone caught outside the fence would be shot on sight.

Hungry men kept slipping out anyway, to buy food in the villages nearby. On the twentieth of January, an internee named Pat Hell was found shot about thirty feet outside the fence. He was still holding a scrawny dead chicken.

Eight days later, at about a quarter to seven in the morning, guards caught another internee, George Louis, crawling back under the wire. At Konishi's trial, a witness testified that Konishi handed his own pistol to a soldier, who shot Louis in the head. Konishi denied it. In May 1945, an Army photographer took a picture of the grave. The caption calls him an American civilian whom the Japanese killed.

Around that time, the guards began digging long trenches near the barracks. Some internees thought they were graves. Nobody knows for certain. But a rumor spread that the Japanese meant to kill everyone before the Americans arrived.

## 8. The guerrillas

The internees were not as alone as they thought. Outside the wire, the hills and villages around Los Baños were full of Filipino guerrillas. There were the Hunters ROTC, a resistance group founded by military cadets and students in Manila in 1942. There were President Quezon's Own Guerrillas, Marking's Guerrillas, the Huks, and a Chinese Filipino squadron. For months, their scouts had been watching the camp.

One of the leaders of the Hunters was Gustavo Ingles. Arrested in 1943, he was tortured at Fort Santiago and sentenced to thirteen years. In June 1944, the Hunters raided the prison where he was held, and broke him out with fifty others.

On the twelfth of February 1945, a young internee named Freddy Zervoulakos, the son of a Greek father and a Filipino mother, slipped out of the camp and met Ingles. The guerrillas wanted to get the internees out. But they needed the camp's leaders to agree.

## 9. The committee says no

The answer was no. The committee's leader, George Gray, told Ingles that under international law, they were a civilian internment camp. And he asked the question that nobody could answer. Did the guerrillas have any way to move more than two thousand people, many of them sick and too weak to walk, out of enemy territory and across to the American lines?

They did not. Only the American Army had the planes, the boats and the guns, and it was twenty-five miles away, fighting for Manila. Somebody would have to get through to the Americans, and tell them everything about the camp.

## 10. Three men and a lantern

On the night of the eighteenth of February, three men decided to go. One was Freddy Zervoulakos. The second was Ben Edwards, the Pan Am mechanic. The third was Pete Miles, an engineer, a rough-and-tumble Texan in his early thirties, who had once been sent to Sumatra by the Saint Louis Zoo to catch animals.

Ben Edwards described the escape years later, and Henderson tells it from his account. At nine at night, the three men walked out of their barracks carrying a lantern. Miles limped and groaned between the other two, pretending to be ill. They walked toward the hospital, then crawled under the wire behind it. A manned guard post stood about forty feet away.

Edwards kicked an empty tin can. The noise, he remembered, was deafening. Nobody came. The three men reached a guerrilla camp in the hills. And the next morning, Filipino boatmen took Pete Miles across the lake in a banca, a narrow outrigger canoe, toward the American lines.

## 11. Quarter to seven

On the nineteenth of February, Miles was brought to the headquarters of the 11th Airborne Division at Parañaque, south of Manila. The division's intelligence officer, Lieutenant Colonel Henry Muller, had been trying to plan a rescue from a rough map. Miles corrected it. He marked the quarters of the internees, the guard houses, the sentries and the pillboxes. He even estimated how many people would have to be carried out on stretchers.

Then Muller asked him about the guards' routine. Miles mentioned their exercises. Muller later told Henderson how the conversation went. Every morning? Yes, and always at the same time. What time? They start exercising at a quarter to seven.

Muller moved the attack from eight in the morning to seven, when the guards would be fifteen minutes into their exercises, with their rifles locked away. The division's history said of Pete Miles: The assistance which this brave man rendered cannot be too highly praised.

## 12. The plan

The division's commander, Major General Joseph Swing, did not have a spare regiment. So the plan his staff drew up was small, fast and complicated, and everything depended on one minute.

It had four parts. The reconnaissance platoon, about thirty men, would cross the lake at night and creep up to the fence with the guerrillas. One company of paratroopers, B Company of the 511th Parachute Infantry, would jump onto a field next to the camp. The rest of their battalion would cross the lake in fifty-four amphibious tractors, called amtracs. And a column with artillery and tank destroyers would attack down the highway, to keep the Japanese busy.

The guerrillas, mostly men of the Hunters ROTC, would wait in the grass with the American scouts and kill the sentries the moment the attack began. Other guerrilla units would block the roads.

Swing's staff estimated that between eight thousand and fifteen thousand Japanese soldiers were within four hours' march of the camp. Henderson writes that the planners expected casualties as high as thirty percent.

## 13. Scheduled for massacre

On the twenty-first of February, a guerrilla commander, Colonel Romeo Espino, radioed the Americans that the Japanese had Los Baños scheduled for massacre. No proof has ever been found that a massacre was planned. But nobody could take that chance.

That night, in a storm, the reconnaissance platoon crossed the lake in bancas. On the far shore, at a schoolhouse in the village of Nanhaya, they met the guerrillas, and they met Ben Edwards and Freddy Zervoulakos. Edwards drew the camp on the blackboard. Both men would go back in with the attack.

On the night of the twenty-second, the teams set out. There was no moon. For about seven hours they waded through flooded rice paddies, until they were lying in the grass near the fence. Some of them, according to the division history, were as close as three feet from the sentry they had been assigned to kill.

At Nichols Field, the paratroopers slept under the wings of their planes. And at about midnight, a report reached General Swing. Night fighter pilots said they had seen hundreds of trucks with their lights on, driving into the camp.

If it was true, the paratroopers would be jumping onto a reinforced garrison. Swing decided to ignore the report. He did not pass it on to the men. At one in the morning, he drove down to the beach to watch the amtracs leave.

## 14. Seven o'clock

At a quarter past five, the amtracs moved out onto the dark lake. Soon after six, nine transport planes took off. And at a quarter to seven, the guards walked out onto the exercise field.

A few minutes before seven, a sentry saw something and fired. The scouts and the guerrillas did not wait. They opened fire a little early. A Filipino officer of the Hunters ordered a guard cut down with a bolo, a long machete. At two minutes to seven, smoke went up on the drop zone and on the beach, to guide the planes and the boats.

At seven o'clock, the first parachute snapped open over the field. It belonged to the company commander, Lieutenant Ringler. They jumped from four or five hundred feet, low enough that each man was in the air for less than half a minute. In less than a minute, they were all on the ground. Not one of them was hurt.

At the same moment, the first amtracs climbed out of the lake onto the beach, and the first American artillery shell landed on a hill to the north, where the diversion had begun. On the exercise field, the guards ran for the room where their rifles were locked. Many of them never got there.

Inside the barracks, nobody knew what was happening. Sally Bateman's mother told her to get under the bed. Sally remembered what she saw from there. I looked out and saw paratrooper boots. It was the most beautiful sight you can ever imagine.

A young internee named Jerry Sams, down to ninety pounds, climbed onto a roof and looked over at the exercise field. It was covered with bodies. In the women's barracks, an internee named Margie Whitaker asked a soldier if the Marines had landed. He said: Sorry, sister. Army paratroopers.

## 15. Five minutes

Within about fifteen minutes, the paratroopers had reached the camp through gaps the scouts had cut in the fence, and the pillboxes were destroyed. Then the amtracs arrived, crashing through the wire and the bushes. The fighting in the camp was over. Now the hardest part began.

But the internees did not want to leave what little they owned, and many were too weak to hurry. The soldiers had no time. So they set the barracks on fire, to get people moving. An internee named Carol Talbot remembered the order. Lady, you've got five minutes to get into an amtrac.

Among the people carried out was the baby, Lois McCoy, born three days before. A soldier carried her in the lining of his helmet. The sick and the old were lifted onto the amtracs on stretchers. By about a quarter to nine, the camp was empty, and the column was moving toward the beach.

The battalion commander, Major Henry Burgess, chose to go back across the lake, in two trips. The first lift carried about fifteen hundred people. The others waited on the beach, under mortar fire, for the amtracs to come back. And at five o'clock, General Swing reported that two thousand one hundred and forty-seven people were safe behind the American lines, with one internee slightly wounded.

The price was paid outside the camp. In the diversion to the north, in the hills at Lecheria, the captain commanding the tank destroyers was shot through the forehead. Two other American soldiers were killed in that fight. Two Filipino guerrillas of the Hunters, Atanacio Castillo and Anselmo Soler, were killed in the raid.

How many Japanese died is less clear. Reports in 1945 said the guards had been killed to the last man, about two hundred and forty of them. Later historians put the number killed in the camp at seventy or eighty. And not everyone in the garrison was on the exercise field that morning. Two men in particular were not: the old commandant, and his supply officer.

General Douglas MacArthur announced the rescue in these words. Nothing could be more satisfying to a soldier's heart than this rescue. God was certainly with us today.

## 16. The same morning

The next day, the twenty-fourth of February, the raid was on the front pages of American newspapers. One paper in Ohio put it in the same headline as another battle. American troops, it said, had freed two thousand one hundred and forty-six more prisoners, and broken the stalemate on Iwo Jima.

On the same morning as the raid, far to the north, Marines on Iwo Jima had raised an American flag on top of Mount Suribachi. That afternoon, a second, larger flag went up, and a photographer named Joe Rosenthal took a picture of it. The photograph was published on Sunday, the twenty-fifth of February.

It became the most famous image of the war. The raid at Los Baños was not hidden by censorship. It made the front pages for a day. Then the photograph from Iwo Jima took over, and the rescue faded from memory.

## 17. The town

For the people of Los Baños, the story did not end on the twenty-third. In the villages around the town, the killing had already started. Army war crimes investigators recorded that on the twenty-first of February, two days before the raid, Japanese soldiers killed sixty-five people from ten families in the village of Tadlak. On the twenty-second, they killed forty-five people from five families in Maahas.

The Japanese commander in the region was Colonel Masatoshi Fujishige, and his war was against the guerrillas. A captured order, printed in the 11th Airborne's history in 1948, reads: Kill all American soldiers brutally. Kill all Filipinos who oppose our Emperor. And after the Americans left with the internees, Japanese soldiers came back to Los Baños.

In the early hours of the twenty-seventh of February, a Japanese unit reached the college grounds, where the camp had stood. Its captain later said he told his men that everyone there was to be killed, because they believed it was a guerrilla area. More than sixty people had taken shelter in the Catholic church on the campus. The war crimes report says that those who refused to leave were burned inside.

The next day, Konishi was there. A Los Baños man, Fernando Bernardo, was ordered with six others to bury the dead. Among the bodies, he testified, was a little boy of two or three, still alive, one hand tied to his dead mother. I heard him call Mama, Bernardo said, and tell her he was hungry. Konishi told the men to turn their backs. When they turned around again, the boy had a bayonet wound.

On the sixth of March, in the village of Anos, soldiers came at night for a Chinese family named Ang. A boy, Ang Kim Ling, was stabbed four times. His six-year-old sister was stabbed sixteen times. At the trial he said: I heard my sister whining, and I crawled out from under the dead bodies and dug her out. His grandparents, his parents, his aunts and uncles were dead. In May, an Army photographer recorded that he and his sister were the only survivors of their family.

How many people were killed around Los Baños is not known. The town's acting mayor estimated six to seven hundred. Other estimates run to fifteen hundred, and more.

Was it revenge for the raid? Some historians say it was. Others point out that the killing began before the raid, and that Fujishige said afterwards that the raid changed nothing in his plans. Ben Edwards, who escaped to bring the Americans in, said he did not believe the atrocities around the college were the direct result of the liberation. What is certain is that after the raid, the killing got worse, and it reached the camp itself.

Guerrilla leaders later blamed the Americans for leaving the area undefended. Gordon Rottman's judgment was that security after the raid was inadequately addressed by all parties.

## 18. The ravine

And Konishi? On the morning of the raid, he said later, he was still in bed when he heard that paratroopers had landed. He ran with the commandant, Iwanaka, and a few other men into a ravine. They hid there until about midnight. Four days later, he was back in Los Baños, with the soldiers.

In September 1945, after Japan's surrender, Konishi was captured on Luzon. In November, investigators took his sworn statement. He said the ration had been set by headquarters, and he had only followed the order. Then they asked him whether the ration had caused starvation. He answered: Yes, actually it caused deaths.

His trial opened in Manila in November 1946. Konishi was sick with tuberculosis, and wore a surgical mask in court. The witnesses came from the camp and from the town: the gravedigger Bernardo, the boy Ang Kim Ling, and a former internee who said he saw Konishi hand over his pistol the morning George Louis was shot.

On the fifteenth of January 1947, the commission found him guilty of the killing of George Louis, the killing of the little boy tied to his mother, the attack on the Ang children, and of letting his men kill sixty-one civilians in March. It also found him guilty of aiding a policy of starvation in the camp, which caused the deaths of four named Americans and many others. Then the president of the commission read the sentence. The commission sentences you to death by hanging.

The commandant, Major Iwanaka, does not appear again in any record we could find. Colonel Fujishige was tried as a war criminal, and sentenced to death.

## 19. The wrong date

For more than thirty years, the books agreed that Konishi was hanged on the seventeenth of June, 1947. The source was a letter written in 1983 by an Army chaplain, who said he had baptized Konishi before his execution that day. The veterans repeated the date. So did the histories. So does the Wikipedia article on the raid today.

It was not true. Bruce Henderson went through the Army's records. In August 1947, General MacArthur had stayed the execution, after a threatened appeal to the Supreme Court. Konishi was moved to Sugamo Prison in Tokyo, and he waited.

In April 1949, the stay was lifted. At thirty-three minutes past midnight, on the thirtieth of April 1949, the trap was sprung at Sugamo. He was pronounced dead fourteen minutes later. The man convicted of starving Los Baños had outlived the date in the history books by almost two years.

## 20. Two plaques

Today, the camp grounds belong to the University of the Philippines Los Baños again. In front of Baker Hall, where the internees once lived, there are markers. They do not tell the same story.

The American record is a War Department citation of August 1945. It honors the division's reconnaissance platoon and B Company of the 511th, for a thrust twenty-five miles behind enemy lines that freed two thousand one hundred and forty-seven people. The division's history of 1948 does not name a single Filipino.

A marker put up by the Philippine government in 2005 tells it the other way around. It says the camp was raided by Filipino guerrillas, organized by Gustavo Ingles, and that the first attack was led by the Hunters ROTC, with the assistance of B Company of the 511th. An older plaque, from 1962, written in Tagalog, says the Hunters freed the camp, with help from other guerrilla groups and some American airborne soldiers.

Each version leaves something out. The Americans brought the plan, the planes and the boats. The Filipinos brought the intelligence, the guides, and many of the men who went through the wire first. An American liaison officer said the mission could not have succeeded without the guerrillas. Colonel Jay Vanderpool put it this way in 1984. There was enough glory for everyone involved that day.

## 21. Afterward

Lieutenant George Skau, who led the reconnaissance platoon, was killed six months later, when his plane crashed on Okinawa. Pete Miles stayed in Manila, and died in 1962. Freddy Zervoulakos served ten years in the United States Navy. Ben Edwards went back to the Philippines, farmed near Los Baños, and went hunting with his old guerrilla friends. He died in 2010, at ninety-one.

Henry Muller, the officer who moved the attack to seven o'clock, retired as a general. And Lois McCoy, the baby carried out in a helmet, grew up to become a nurse.

In 1998, a former amtrac gunner named Dwight Clark found her phone number. For fifty years he had worried that hot shell casings from his machine gun had fallen on the baby. He asked her: I heard you cry. Do you have a scar on your face?

Every year, around the twenty-third of February, the raid is still remembered at Baker Hall. Students at the university's high school study on scholarships named for the liberation. And the field where the guards did their exercises, every morning at a quarter to seven, is part of a university again.
