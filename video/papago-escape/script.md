# German POWs Dug a Tunnel to Float to Mexico. The River Was Dry.

## 1. Cold open

On the night of December twenty-third, 1944, twenty-five German prisoners of war crawled out of a camp in the Arizona desert through a tunnel they had dug by hand. Three of them took a boat with them, in pieces. They meant to paddle it to Mexico.

The boat had been built in secret, from scrap lumber and canvas. The route came from a map. A canal to the Salt River, the Salt to the Gila, the Gila to the Colorado, and the Colorado to the sea. On paper, it was water all the way.

On the ground, it was something else. This is the documented story of the largest escape of enemy prisoners from an American camp in the Second World War, and of the desert that caught them.

## 2. The camp

Camp Papago Park sat on the eastern edge of Phoenix, among red sandstone buttes. It opened in 1943, and by early 1944 it held only Germans, most of them sailors of the German navy. At its peak, it held about three thousand one hundred prisoners, watched by three hundred and seventy-one Americans.

Many of them were U-boat men, and the most stubborn were sent here. Their senior officer was Captain Jürgen Wattenberg. He had been navigation officer of the warship Graf Spee. At forty-one, he took his own U-boat to sea and sank fourteen ships, until three British destroyers sank him off Trinidad in September 1942. The camp's commander would call him the number one super-Nazi of the camp.

The camp had already seen what the hard-liners would do. On March twelfth, 1944, a sailor named Werner Drechsler arrived. He had been informing on other prisoners for American naval intelligence, and his old crewmates recognized him. Within hours, he was dead, found hanging in a shower room. Seven prisoners were later hanged for his murder.

For everyone else, life at Papago was easy. Officers did not have to work. The prisoners called the camp the land of milk and honey. But the officers had time, and they had a fence to look at. One of them, Lieutenant Wolfgang Clarus, said they saw three ways past it. Go through it, fly over it, or dig under it.

## 3. The blind spot

The camp's provost marshal, Captain Cecil Parshall, already knew where the weakness was. Part of the officers' compound could not be seen from any guard tower. "I knew they would discover that blind spot," he said later. In September 1944, four U-boat captains, planning over games of bridge, started a tunnel there.

The entrance was in a bathhouse, the building closest to the eastern fence. They loosened a board in the wall and hid the opening behind a large coal box. The Americans even lent them shovels and rakes, for gardening.

They dug at night, in crews of three, in shifts of ninety minutes. One man dug, one hauled the dirt back in a bucket, and one kept watch. The tunnel was less than three feet across. To pass under a drainage ditch and a road, it dropped fourteen feet down. On a good night, it moved three feet forward.

## 4. The faustball court

The hardest problem was the dirt. They flushed it down the toilets. They hid it in the attics. They carried it out in their pockets and let it fall from their trouser legs. Then one of the captains, Jürgen Quaet-Faslem, had a better idea. He asked the Americans for a sports field.

The game was faustball, a German sport much like volleyball. The guards were delighted. It was the first time these prisoners had cooperated with anything. Every day, fresh earth was raked across the new court, and the Americans took it as German tidiness. In late November, an inspector stood almost on top of the entrance and declared the ground as hard as a rock.

On December twentieth, the tunnel was finished. It ran one hundred and seventy-eight feet, from the bathhouse, under the fence, to the bank of the Crosscut Canal. The exit was hidden beneath a wooden box of dirt and grass.

## 5. The boatmen

They had been saving food for weeks. Bread was toasted, crushed into crumbs and wrapped in waxed paper from cereal boxes, to be mixed with water or milk on the road. Photographs were turned into forged papers, with stamps carved from leather and rubber.

Three officers had a different plan from everyone else. Captain Wilhelm Günther and Lieutenants Wolfgang Clarus and Friedrich Utzolino built a folding boat from scavenged wood. They got canvas and tar by saying they needed to repair the roofs. No piece could be longer than eighteen inches, so the whole boat would fit through the tunnel. They became known as the three mad boatmen.

## 6. The night

The date was Saturday, December twenty-third. In the next compound, prisoners threw a loud party to cover any noise. At nine o'clock, the first men went down the shaft. By two thirty in the morning, all twenty-five were out, twelve officers and thirteen enlisted men. They waded through the icy water of the canal in the rain, and split up into small groups. Most of them headed south. Mexico was about one hundred and thirty miles away.

They spent Christmas Eve on the run. One group rested in a stable, eating bread crumbs and canned milk, while a Mexican family nearby sang carols. In another group, a man with a harmonica played Silent Night.

Back at the camp, nobody noticed for most of a day. The German officers stalled the Sunday count by demanding that an American officer take it. By about seven that evening, Parshall knew men were gone, and called the FBI. Hundreds of soldiers, FBI agents and Papago trackers joined the search, with a reward of twenty-five dollars a man. The Phoenix Gazette called it the greatest manhunt in Arizona history.

## 7. The desert

The desert did most of the work. The nights were cold, it kept raining, and the men were hungry and lost. A twenty-two-year-old U-boat sailor named Herbert Fuchs walked into the Phoenix sheriff's office and gave himself up. Two more surrendered in Tempe. One stepped on a cactus and limped to a farmhouse with his partner, where the mother of the family later remembered them as charming and handsome guests.

The people of Phoenix were angrier about what the prisoners carried than about the escape itself. Some carried rationed goods. One resident complained that citizens could not get a single slice of bacon for weeks on end. Nobody was attacked, and nothing was sabotaged.

The best of them came close. On New Year's Day, a Papago tracker found two of them asleep less than thirty miles from the border. On January sixth, Indian scouts caught Captain Quaet-Faslem and Captain Friedrich Guggenberger within ten miles of Mexico. Three years earlier, Guggenberger's U-boat had sunk the British aircraft carrier Ark Royal.

## 8. The river

The boatmen never got their boat wet. The Salt River, the first blue line on their map, had almost no water in it. The rain shrank their canvas. When they reached the Gila, it was too shallow and muddy to float their boat. They left the boat behind and walked.

On January eighth, near the town of Gila Bend, one of them washed his underwear and hung it on a mesquite tree to dry. Cowboys saw it and called the authorities. Clarus put it simply. We should have known that the Gila wasn't much of a river.

## 9. The last man

Wattenberg never went south. With two sailors, Walter Kozur and Johann Kremer, he hid in a cave in the mountains north of the city. Every few days, Kremer traded places with a prisoner on an outside work detail. Kremer would walk back into the camp to collect food and news, and the other man would spend the night in the cave.

The trick worked for weeks. Then, late in January, a surprise inspection found Kremer inside the camp. The next night, three soldiers were waiting at an abandoned car where the supplies were hidden, and Kozur walked straight into them.

On January twenty-seventh, Wattenberg cleaned himself up and walked into Phoenix with seventy-five cents in his pocket. He spent most of it on a meal, slept in a chair in a hotel lobby, and walked the streets at night. Then he asked a street cleaner for directions. The man did not like his accent and called the police. By nine the next morning, Wattenberg was back at Papago Park, thirty-five days after he left.

## 10. Aftermath

Some of the escapees expected to be shot. Instead, each man got bread and water, one day for every day he had been gone. No American officer or guard was court-martialed. The FBI looked into how loosely Arizona's camps were run.

Clarus later said the tunnel had kept their spirits up, even as Germany was being crushed. In May 1945, Germany surrendered. After the war, Guggenberger joined the new West German navy, rose to admiral, and served as a deputy chief of staff at NATO.

Wattenberg came back to Arizona as an old man, for reunions with his former guards. At one of them, in 1985, he said it was strange that men had to kill each other before they became friends. He died in Hamburg in 1995, at ninety-four.

## 11. Echo

A plaque on the bank of the Crosscut Canal marks the spot where the tunnel came up. Twenty-five men dug one hundred and seventy-eight feet under the desert to reach a river. They planned for the guards, the fence and the dirt. What they could not see from inside the wire was the river itself.
