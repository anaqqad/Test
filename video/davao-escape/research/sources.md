# Sources: the Davao escape (4 April 1943) and the story Washington held back

## Why this topic
Competitor research on 17 Nov 2026 (YouTube search sorted by views, this week and this month). Sealed Orders' "The True Story of
the Escape from Davao: the 10 Americans Who Told the World About Bataan" had 22,523 views in 4 days, the strongest new POW video
outside the crowded "German POWs were shocked by America" format (which we covered with the New Ulm and camp-films episodes).
Other trending POW titles: "A German POW Shouted an Insult. The American Guard Answered in Fluent Low German" (62,824 in 2 days),
"German POWs Were Sent to Work on an Alabama Farm. The Farmer Spoke Bavarian" (192,633 in 12 days). We make the documented version
of the Davao escape and lean on the half of the story competitors skip: the gag order, the Gripsholm, Dyess's death in Burbank
before his story could run, and the release of 28 January 1944.
Hook: Sunday, 4 April 1943, ten prisoners walk out of the gate with the work details while a guard ticks them off on a chalkboard;
one is told loudly that his toothbrush is sticking out of his bag.

## 1. John D. Lukacs, *Escape from Davao: The Forgotten Story of the Most Daring Prison Break of the Pacific War* (Simon & Schuster/NAL, 2010; pb 2011)
Main source. Read via Internet Archive search-inside (escapefromdavaof0000luka). Page numbers of that edition.
- p460 (jacket): "On April 4, 1943, ten American prisoners of war and two Filipino convicts executed a daring escape from one of
  Japan's most notorious prison camps... Theirs was the only successful group escape from a Japanese POW camp during the Pacific war."
- p201: chapter heading "SUNDAY, APRIL 4, 1943 Davao Penal Colony".
- p202: Mellnik slid "a razor and a worn toothbrush into his musette bag"; McCoy hid his maps and charts under his clothes; "his most
  prized possession, a half-roll of toilet paper that he had kept since Corregidor."
- p205: "Hey, Steve!" Carpenter's voice cried out across the compound. "Your toothbrush is sticking out of the back of your musette
  bag. Are you planning to escape?"
- p207: they waited in the brush for Ben de la Cruz and Victor Jumarong. "Ten nervous minutes passed. Then twenty. The dreary sky
  began to spit a heavy downpour." Jumarong arrived alone at 0900.
- p211: "around 1800, during evening tenko... the POWs were lined up and counted. But this time, they were recounted. Then counted
  again... ten American POWs and two Filipinos were unaccounted for."
- p180: Victorio Jumarong, "the illiterate Ilocano", had been sent to Dapecol for murder; p182: Benigno (Ben) de la Cruz, 25.
- p166: plan: swamp, sixty miles of jungle, then Cateel on the east coast and a boat to Australia, "1,300 miles distant".
- p227: four days out, on the railroad toward Lungaog, "a fifteen-year-old Filipino boy, their first encounter with another human
  since leaving Dapecol four days ago." p230: the whole barrio of Lungaog turned out; boiled corn, chicken, a guerrilla played
  "La Paloma" on a mandolin.
- p235: Shofner's diary, April 10: "listening to phonograph play the first American music I heard in a long time."
- p84: the Death March, "the roughly sixty-five-mile journey on foot". p89: "nearly 700 Americans and perhaps as many as 10,000
  Filipinos were believed to have died throughout the three-week-long nightmare that they would call, simply, 'the Hike.'"
- p95: Camp O'Donnell: "nearly 2,000 of the 9,000 Americans that had crossed O'Donnell's infernal threshold in April would be dead by June."
- p121: a Japanese edict dated July 10, 1942, "grouped the prisoners in 'shooting squads' of ten men", threatening the others if one
  escaped. p212: whether the "ten for one" rule was in force at Dapecol: "A consensus was never reached".
- p135: Major Maeda, commandant of Dapecol: "he had been sent a batch of walking corpses" (Mellnik).
- p263, p296-297: USS Trout took off the first group on July 9, 1943; entered Fremantle Harbor July 20.
- p18: Dyess returned to the States "on Monday, August 9, his twenty-seventh birthday"; "he arrived anonymously in Washington".
- p266: Dyess's deposition "would form the foundation of what the War Department would later call 'the greatest story of the war in
  the Pacific.'" (Note p387: Fort Worth Star-Telegram, January 29, 1944.)
- p301-302: sent to Ashford General Hospital, "formerly the luxurious Greenbrier Resort", West Virginia; "a gag order was enforced.
  If Dyess spoke of his experiences to anyone but authorized personnel, he risked his commission and possible criminal proceedings."
  Deposition: "it is not only advisable, but absolutely necessary that all civilized people of the world know the conditions of the
  Japanese prison camps and the atrocities against American prisoners of war."
- p304: Roosevelt's memorandum: "I agree with your opinion that any publication of Japanese atrocities at this time might complicate
  the present and future missions of the GRIPSHOLM and increase the mistreatment of prisoners now in Japanese hands. I request,
  therefore, that you take effective measures to prevent the publication or circulation of any stories emanating from escaped
  prisoners until I have authorized a release." p307: "Roosevelt's memorandum".
- p305: Gen. Alexander Surles (War Dept. public relations) to the Tribune, Sept 9: "Nothing is going to happen on this thing for at
  least six weeks."
- p307: "The Gripsholm was a merchant ship of neutral Swedish registry that was carrying medical supplies and food for Allied POWs in
  the Pacific. The U.S. government was worried that publicizing Japanese atrocities might jeopardize the delivery of those supplies."
  Lukacs adds there were "other reasons" (p282 via Hyer: concern over the government's lack of action in the Pacific).
- p310: McCoy and Mellnik's silence "had reportedly also been secured under the threat of the loss of their commissions and careers."
- p303: the Chicago Tribune offered $21,000; Leavelle: the paper "could tell it to 12 to 14 million people a day."
- p312: Leavelle took the story down at Ashford; Dyess teased him: "You must have a strong 'T' key on this typewriter."
- p318: six days at home in Albany, Texas, escorts "omnipresent". p319: Dallas Morning News headline: "One-Man Scourge of Japanese
  Turns Talents on Texas Steak".
- p324, p327-328: Wednesday, December 22, 1943, Burbank. The P-38, on fire after take-off from Grand Central Air Terminal; a car came
  out of a side street onto Olive Avenue in his landing path; Dyess lifted the plane over it, "noticed that there was a small vacant
  lot at the southeast corner of Myers and Olive. Instinctively, he banked his plane for it"; the left wing struck the steeple of
  Saint Finbar's and the plane "cartwheeled into the vacant lot."
- p330: Dec 27, Albany, "cold, rainy, wind-whipped day"; several hundred filled Matthews Presbyterian Church; recommended for the
  Medal of Honor. p372: "Ed Dyess instead received the Soldier's Medal"; Dec 1, 1956, Abilene AFB renamed Dyess AFB. Grashio: "If Ed
  was watching in the Great Beyond he probably would have laughed, for it was a bomber base while he was a fighter pilot."
- p343: "the official Army-Navy statement regarding atrocities... was finally released... at exactly 12 a.m., Eastern War Time, on
  Friday, January 28, 1944." New York Times: "5,200 AMERICANS, MANY MORE FILIPINOS DIE OF STARVATION, TORTURE AFTER BATAAN."
- p344: "That Sunday, the first installment of the Dyess story ran on the front page of the Chicago Tribune, as well as in 100
  associated newspapers. There would be twenty-four total installments".
- p347: Life, February 7, "Death Was a Part of Our Life" by McCoy, Mellnik and Lt. Welbourn Kelley, "photographs of all ten
  escapees", "The fifteen-page exposé".
- p356: "From January 28 forward, the nature of the war would be drilled into the minds of all Americans."
- p367-368: the last Dapecol prisoners, on the Shinyo Maru, torpedoed by the submarine Paddle on September 7, 1944, off Mindanao;
  "fewer than 100 would survive". The Paddle's commander "had no idea" the ship carried American prisoners.
- p363: Grashio retired in 1965 as a colonel; in the 1980s befriended Saburo Sakai; died 1999. p370: Mellnik retired a brigadier
  general in 1963, died 1994.
- p378: at the penal farm "The only tangible evidence that American prisoners of war had once been incarcerated there is a small,
  weather-beaten bronze memorial plaque affixed to a petrified kapok tree stump".

## 2. Melvyn H. McCoy and S. M. Mellnik, as told to Lt. Welbourn Kelley, *Ten Escape from Tojo* (Farrar & Rinehart, 1944)
Text at mansell.com/pow_resources/camplists/philippines/ten_escape.html.
- "With eight others, we were the first to escape from a Japanese prisoner-of-war camp in the Philippines."
- "only 1100 of our 2000 prisoners were working. The other 900 were too sick to work."
- Ch. 5: "Soon we were in swamp, with water up to our knees and in sharp-edged coogan grass that grew over our heads."
  "we awoke to find ourselves half-floating in our beds." "we waded through water as deep as our armpits."
- "They thought he was a Jap, they said; one of them had taken careful aim on Dyess and had pulled the trigger." (the guerrillas)
- "We believe the facts as presented give a fair picture of the enemy we face in the Pacific."

## 3. Dwight Jon Zimmerman, "POW Escape from Davao," parts 1 and 2, Defense Media Network (24 and 27 April 2013)
- Part 1: "As a guard checked them off on a chalkboard, the men marched through the gate". "In the 10 years of Dapecol's existence,
  no prisoner had escaped." Mellnik: "We must make the horrors of O'Donnell, Cabanatuan, and Davao a matter of record."
- Part 2: "Four days later, they arrived at the village of Lungaog and met friendly Filipino guerrillas"; the ~300-mile journey across
  Mindanao; MacArthur: "The Japanese will pay for that humiliation and suffering."; "(Leo Boelens remained to help the guerrillas)";
  Margaret Truman at the christening of the USS Missouri, January 29, 1944: "May this great ship be an avenger to the barbarians who
  wantonly slaughtered the heroes of Bataan."

## 4. Jason Dawsey, "Exposing Atrocity: The Davao Dozen and the Bataan Death March," The National WWII Museum (31 Jan 2022)
- Seven of the group left Mindanao by submarine for Australia; three stayed with the guerrillas; "Second Lieutenant Leo Boelens was
  killed by the Japanese in January 1944." The War Department first imposed a gag order; Life published on February 7, 1944.

## 5. Wikipedia, "William E. Dyess" (and its sources: The Dyess Story, 1944; USAAF accident report, Dec 1943; USAF fact sheet)
- Born August 9, 1916, Albany, Texas. Captured April 9, 1942; Death March; O'Donnell, Cabanatuan; arrived Davao November 7, 1942.
- Evacuated by USS Trout, July 1943. Ashford General Hospital, September 1943; Chicago Tribune writer Charles Leavelle.
- Dec 22, 1943, P-38G 42-13441, engine fire on take-off from Grand Central Airport; "Rather than bail out over a populated area, he
  tried to land in a vacant lot". Published as *The Dyess Story* (1944), a bestseller.

## 6. Michael Harold Hyer, "What the World (and George and Ruby) Now Knew," in *Saints at War in the Philippines* (BYU Religious Studies Center)
- Quotes Lukacs 319-325: war bond sales soared during the $14 billion drive; the official reason for withholding was "medical
  supplies for Allied prisoners in the Pacific" (Lukacs 283; Daws, Prisoners of the Japanese, 274-75).

## 7. Philippines Historical Committee marker, Davao Penal Colony (1956), photographed 2025 (Wikimedia Commons, JasmineGoforth, CC BY 4.0)
- "Established 21 January 1932 ... used as concentration camp for American prisoners of war; colony was reopened 2 August 1946."

## Disagreements
- **Who was in the ten.** Lists agree: Dyess, McCoy, Mellnik, Shofner, Hawkins, Dobervich, Grashio, Boelens, Spielman, Marshall
  (Ten Escape from Tojo foreword; Lukacs). Wikipedia's Dyess page names only some. Script names the ten as above.
- **Was Dyess's crash a choice to spare houses?** Wikipedia (USAF fact sheet) says he chose not to bail out over a populated area;
  Lukacs describes him lifting over a car and banking "instinctively" for a vacant lot. Script: he stayed with the burning plane and
  put it down in a vacant lot, missing the houses and a car; it does not claim his motive.
- **Why the story was held.** Official reason: the Gripsholm's relief cargo and fear of reprisals (Roosevelt memo). Lukacs says
  there were "other reasons", possibly embarrassment over the Europe-first strategy. Script gives the official reason and says
  "some historians think there was more to it."
- **"Ten for one" at Davao.** Lukacs: no consensus whether the shooting-squad rule was in force at Dapecol. Script says prisoners
  had been told of it at Cabanatuan and nobody knew whether it applied.
