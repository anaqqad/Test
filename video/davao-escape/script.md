# Ten POWs Escaped to Tell America About Bataan. Washington Hid It.

## 1. The gate

On Sunday morning, the fourth of April 1943, the prisoners of the Davao Penal Colony, on the Philippine island of Mindanao, lined up for the day's work. One by one the work details marched out through the gate, while a Japanese guard checked them off on a chalkboard. Among them walked ten Americans whose bags were packed a little too full. Across the compound, a friend shouted at one of them. Hey, Steve, your toothbrush is sticking out of your bag. Are you planning to escape?

He was. That evening, at the six o'clock roll call, the guards counted the prisoners. Then they counted them again, and again. Ten Americans and two Filipino convicts were gone. In the ten years the penal colony had existed, no prisoner had ever escaped from it.

This is the documented story of the only successful group escape from a Japanese prison camp in the Pacific war: ten men who walked into a swamp to tell America about the Bataan Death March, and the months their own government then made them keep quiet.

## 2. The march

Most of them had been prisoners for a year. On the ninth of April 1942, the starving American and Filipino army on the Bataan peninsula surrendered to the Japanese. Its men were marched north for roughly sixty-five miles in the tropical heat. Nearly seven hundred Americans, and perhaps as many as ten thousand Filipinos, are believed to have died on the way. The prisoners called it, simply, the Hike.

At the end of it was Camp O'Donnell. Of about nine thousand Americans who walked through its gate in April, nearly two thousand were dead by June. Then came Cabanatuan. In November 1942, about two thousand prisoners were shipped south to Mindanao, to a prison farm cut out of the jungle near Davao. The Japanese commandant looked them over and said he had asked for workers and been sent a batch of walking corpses.

Ed Dyess was one of them. He was a fighter pilot from Albany, Texas, who had led a squadron of P-forties on Bataan until there were almost no planes left, and then fought on the ground as an infantry officer. He was captured on the day of the surrender and walked the whole march.

## 3. The ten

The others came from every service. Melvyn McCoy was a Navy officer who had left Annapolis with one of the best mathematics records the academy had seen. He would navigate. Stephen Mellnik was an Army major from Corregidor. Austin Shofner, Jack Hawkins and Mike Dobervich were Marines. Sam Grashio was a pilot and Leo Boelens a mechanic, both Army airmen. Paul Marshall and Bob Spielman were sergeants.

Two Filipinos completed the party. Benigno de la Cruz and Victorio Jumarong were convicts at the colony, both sent there for killings. Jumarong had once helped clear a trail through the jungle to a village called Lungaog. Without that trail, the plan was hopeless.

The plan was dangerous for more than the ten. At Cabanatuan, the Japanese had grouped the prisoners into shooting squads of ten. If one man escaped, the others could be shot. Nobody at Davao knew for certain whether the rule still applied. The ten went anyway. Mellnik put the reason simply. The horrors of O'Donnell, Cabanatuan and Davao had to become a matter of record.

## 4. The swamp

For weeks they had smuggled food and supplies out to the fields and buried them. On the morning of the escape they slipped away from their work details into a coconut grove and dug up their bundles, soaked by a week of rain. Then they waited for the two Filipinos. Ten minutes passed, then twenty, and the sky opened. At nine o'clock, Jumarong walked out of the rain alone, with a question from his friend. Ben wanted to know if they were ready.

Then came the swamp. The water rose to their knees, and sharp-edged grass grew higher than their heads. The first night they camped where the water was only ankle deep, and woke up half floating in their beds. In places they waded in water as deep as their armpits, while McCoy kept them on a compass bearing to the northeast.

On the fourth day, walking along an old railroad, they met a fifteen-year-old boy, the first human being they had seen since the escape. He ran, then stopped when the Filipinos called out to him. His village, he said, was close. It was Lungaog.

The whole village turned out. The Americans ate boiled corn and chicken, and a guerrilla played La Paloma on a mandolin. Days later, at a sugar plantation, Austin Shofner wrote in his diary that he was listening to a phonograph play the first American music he had heard in a long time. Not every meeting went so well. One guerrilla mistook Dyess for a Japanese soldier, took careful aim and pulled the trigger. Dyess survived it.

## 5. The submarine

Guerrilla bands passed them from one to the next across some three hundred miles of Mindanao. On the ninth of July 1943, the submarine Trout took Dyess, McCoy and Mellnik off the island. Most of the others followed later by submarine. Three stayed on to fight with the guerrillas, and one of them, Leo Boelens, was killed by the Japanese in January 1944.

In Australia they told their story to General Douglas MacArthur. The Japanese, he said, will pay for that humiliation and suffering. Dyess's account was typed up and flown to Washington. The War Department would later call it the greatest story of the war in the Pacific.

And then, nothing happened.

## 6. The gag order

Ed Dyess landed in the United States on the ninth of August 1943, his twenty-seventh birthday. Nobody met him. There were no reporters and no photographers. Officially, he was not even in the country. He gave a long statement to the War Department and ended it with a plea. It was absolutely necessary, he said, that all civilized people of the world know the conditions of the Japanese prison camps.

Instead, he was sent to Ashford General Hospital in West Virginia, a luxury resort before the war, and ordered to keep quiet. If he told his story to anyone without permission, he risked his commission and possible criminal charges. McCoy and Mellnik were silenced the same way.

The order came from the top. President Roosevelt agreed that publishing Japanese atrocities could endanger the Gripsholm, a neutral Swedish ship carrying food and medicine for Allied prisoners, and could make their treatment worse. He asked that no story from escaped prisoners be published until he authorized it. Some historians think there was more to it. A story about the men left behind on Bataan raised awkward questions about how little Washington had sent to the Pacific.

The Chicago Tribune wanted the story anyway. It offered Dyess twenty-one thousand dollars and sent a reporter, Charles Leavelle, to his hospital room, where Dyess dictated his story from his bed. The finished chapters went to the War Department, and the War Department sat on them. Nothing was going to happen, an Army general told the paper, for at least six weeks.

Dyess was allowed home to Albany, Texas, for six days, with Army escorts at his side the whole time. The newspapers could not print what he had seen, so they wrote about what he ate. One headline read: One-Man Scourge of Japanese Turns Talents on Texas Steak.

## 7. Burbank

Then he went back to flying. On Wednesday, the twenty-second of December 1943, Dyess took off in a P-thirty-eight fighter from Grand Central Air Terminal, near Burbank, California. Just after takeoff, one engine caught fire. Below him were houses.

He stayed with the burning plane. He brought it down toward a street, lifted it over a car that pulled out in front of him, and banked for a small vacant lot. A wing clipped the steeple of a church, and the plane cartwheeled into the lot. Ed Dyess was killed. He was twenty-seven.

Five days later, in a cold rain, several hundred people filled a church in Albany for his memorial. A general told them he would recommend Dyess for the Medal of Honor. The story he had come home to tell had still not been printed.

## 8. January twenty-eighth

A month later, the government changed its mind. At midnight on Friday, the twenty-eighth of January 1944, the Army and Navy released a joint statement on Japanese atrocities against American prisoners. The New York Times headline ran across the front page: five thousand two hundred Americans, and many more Filipinos, dead of starvation and torture after Bataan.

That Sunday, the first part of the Dyess story ran on the front page of the Chicago Tribune and in about a hundred other newspapers. There would be twenty-four installments, one a day for most of a month. On the seventh of February, Life magazine printed McCoy and Mellnik's account across fifteen pages, with photographs of all ten escapees.

The country was furious. At the launching of the battleship Missouri, Margaret Truman, the senator's daughter who christened her, said she hoped the ship would be an avenger for the heroes of Bataan. War bond sales soared. From that day on, one historian writes, the nature of the war in the Pacific was drilled into the minds of all Americans.

## 9. Left behind

The men left behind at Davao were not saved by the story. In 1944, the last American prisoners from the colony were loaded onto a Japanese freighter, the Shinyo Maru. On the seventh of September, an American submarine, the Paddle, torpedoed it off Mindanao. Its commander had no idea the ship was carrying American prisoners. Fewer than a hundred of them survived.

Of the ten, Sam Grashio stayed in the Air Force and retired a colonel in 1965. Years later he made friends with Saburo Sakai, the Japanese ace who had nearly shot him down on the first day of the war. Steve Mellnik retired a brigadier general. And in 1956, the air base at Abilene, Texas, was renamed Dyess Air Force Base. Grashio wrote that Ed would have laughed. It was a bomber base, and he had been a fighter pilot.

## 11. The plaque

The Davao Penal Colony is still a prison today. Its historical marker, put up in 1956, gives the Americans half a line: used as concentration camp for American prisoners of war. When the historian John Lukacs went looking for traces of them, he found only a small, weather-beaten bronze plaque, fixed to the stump of an old kapok tree. The ten walked out of that place so the world would know what had happened there. It took ten months, a submarine and one man's death before it did.
