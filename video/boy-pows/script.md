# A 15-Year-Old German POW Was Handed a Shovel in Maine. He Thought It Was His Grave.

## 1. Cold open
In the woods of northern Maine, during the Second World War, an American guard handed a German prisoner a shovel and told him to dig. The prisoner was fifteen years old.

He started to dig, and then he started to cry. When the guard asked him why, the boy said he believed he was digging his own grave. He was sure that when the hole was deep enough, they would shoot him.

It was a latrine pit. The guards comforted him, and the boy went back to the camp alive.

This is the documented story of the boys who were sent to America as German prisoners of war: what they had been told to expect, and what they found, told from their own words and the people who guarded them.

## 2. The boys
By 1944, Germany was running out of men. As the war turned against it, the army drafted younger boys and older men. At the camp in Houlton, Maine, the prisoners were fifteen years of age and older.

One of them was Gerhard Kleindt. He had been drafted at seventeen. Before he was captured in Normandy, he had been told that the Americans did not take prisoners.

One night his convoy was attacked, and his vehicle overturned in the gunfight. He was certain he would be shot immediately. Instead, American soldiers pulled him to safety. They took him in for questioning, and they fed him milk and cornflakes. The Americans, he said later, saved his life.

Another boy was Hank Welzel. He was twelve when he was in the Hitler Youth, and sixteen when the German army drafted him. He was trained as a medic and sent to fight the Americans and the British in Italy.

Welzel had a secret. He had been born in the United States. His grandfather had always told him to be proud that he was an American, but he never dared to tell the other German soldiers. You had to consider yourself a German, he said, if you wanted to stay alive.

## 3. Captured
On October the tenth, 1944, just short of his eighteenth birthday, Welzel was captured on a hill north of Florence. Now he faced a new question. Should he tell the Americans where he had been born?

He did not. He never told anybody, he said. It was his highest secret.

He was shipped across the Atlantic to the country where he had been born, and he spent the rest of the war in a prisoner of war camp in Alabama, keeping that secret.

## 4. Houlton
Kleindt was sent to Maine. Camp Houlton sat in Aroostook County, potato country. About three thousand five hundred men were held there between 1944 and 1946.

One of the American guards, Milton Bailey, remembered how they looked when they arrived. They were really just boys, he said, ragged, in torn clothing, with not much meat on their bones.

Maine needed them. So many local men were overseas that the farmers had no one to bring in the harvest. The prisoners picked potatoes and cut wood for the paper mills. One of them, Rudi Richter, remembered that they helped pick potatoes and got paid a few cents per barrel.

They were paid in credit, about a dollar a day, to spend at the camp store on chocolate, tobacco and paint. The University of Maine offered them classes. Kleindt cooked for the guards, then worked on a potato farm, and took part in the camp's education program.

## 5. The kitchen
The boys had been raised in a hard military world, where even small mistakes were punished. One day a young prisoner working in a farmer's kitchen knocked over a colander of apples. He broke down in tears and covered his head, waiting to be hit.

Nobody hit him. The farm family could not speak German, and he could not speak much English, but he understood. He went back to the barracks smiling, carrying a box of apples.

At Christmas, a farmer named Fenton Shaw decided that the prisoners on his farm should celebrate the way they did at home. His family brought Christmas stockings for every prisoner, filled with homemade treats and fruit.

That night, the prisoners gathered outside their barracks in the winter night and sang Stille Nacht, Silent Night, to the people who had brought them.

Not everyone in Houlton was happy to have them. Some of the prisoners were dedicated Nazis. But a museum curator in Maine would later say that a lot of them were reluctant soldiers. Many were young and frightened.

## 6. Going home
When the war ended, the boys did not go straight home. They went back to a ruined country.

Rudi Richter could not recognise his own hometown. So much of it had been destroyed that he was lost. He stopped an old man in the street and asked him the way to his family's house.

Sixty years later, telling the story, Richter wept. The old man had not been a stranger. It was his own father.

Hank Welzel worked in France after the war. Then he went to the American embassy in Paris and told his secret. In 1949, at twenty-three, he arrived in the United States with nothing but his German accent and a suitcase of dirty laundry.

A year later, the United States Army drafted him. He went to Korea as an American medic, and he came home with a Purple Heart and a Bronze Star. He had fought in two wars, under two flags. He had nightmares for forty-five years, and he and his wife kept his German service a secret until their children were in high school.

## 7. The return
In 2003, the town of Houlton invited its old prisoners back. Four of them came from Germany, now old men. Veterans gave them a motorcycle escort, and the town made them honorary citizens.

Not everyone was comfortable with it. But one of the four said the experience had changed his life, and the way he saw people and judged them.

Rudi Richter did something more. During the war, a potato farmer's wife named Catherine Bell had treated her prisoners with dignity, though her own brother had been lost in the war, his body never recovered. Sixty years later, Richter went with her to the edge of the Baltic Sea, the final resting place of the brother she had lost.

Gerhard Kleindt looked back on it all in a single sentence. The best years of my life, he said, were spent in a prison camp behind barbed wire. It was the consequence of a senseless war that Germany had started, for which we were shamelessly misused.

## 9. Today
The prisoners at Houlton left something behind. The camp had an art program, and the men painted. One unknown prisoner painted a portrait of a local couple's daughter, an eight-year-old girl named Lois, smiling.

Their paintings still hang in Houlton today. They were painted by boys who had once believed that America would shoot them.
