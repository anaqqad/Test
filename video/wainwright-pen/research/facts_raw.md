# Wainwright and MacArthur: fact sheet (raw)

Rule: no source, no line. Quotes from U.S. government records (Morton/CMH, radiograms, the Medal of Honor citation, Truman papers) are verbatim. Wainwright's 1945 serial and 1946 memoir and MacArthur's *Reminiscences* are still in copyright, so they are paraphrased here, with a pointer to the exact passage. The memoir is borrow-only on archive.org. Its passages were checked with archive.org full-text search. Page numbers come from the book's own index or from Morton's footnotes. **FLAG** marks anything weakly sourced.

## Sources

**[1]** Wainwright, "Wainwright's Own Story," installment 1. King Features serial in the *Los Angeles Examiner*, 7 Oct 1945, pp. 1 and 8. Scan licensed CC-BY: https://archive.org/details/wainwrights-own-story
- On 10 Mar 1942 he took a launch from Mariveles to Corregidor. Sutherland called him "Skinny" and told him MacArthur would leave by PT boat "tomorrow evening".
- MacArthur asked him to tell his troops that he was leaving at Roosevelt's repeated order and "over my repeated protests." He promised to come back as soon as he could with as much as he could. On parting he gave Wainwright a box of cigars and two jars of shaving cream.
- MacArthur promised him a third star if he was still on Bataan when MacArthur returned. Wainwright answered that he would be there if he was alive. Wainwright writes that MacArthur is the only person who calls him Jonathan.
- A photo caption in the same issue dates the MacArthur embrace in Yokohama to 31 Aug 1945, with International News credit. Another caption shows him handing Mrs. Wainwright the Missouri pen.

Facts by topic:
- **Fear of disgrace (Karenko, autumn 1942).** A Domei correspondent asked whether he would be court-martialed "for surrendering." He replied that he had acted on his commander-in-chief's authority. Afterwards he wrote out his reasons in his diary: the Japanese would take the island within a day or two, no help was coming, and he feared a massacre in Malinta Tunnel of about 1,000 sick and wounded and 125 nurses. In the same diary entry he wrote that some would have called him a fool or a criminal if he had sacrificed everyone.
- **Rescue, 19 Aug 1945.** The Sian camp commander told him an American doctor was coming: Maj. Lamar with Cpl. Leith, both OSS. They were the first free Americans he had seen since 6 May 1942. Lamar said the team had parachuted onto Mukden airfield at noon on 16 Aug.
- **Waiting at Sian.** Lamar went back to Mukden and Leith stayed as interpreter. They waited from 21 to 24 Aug.
- **Leaving Sian, 24 Aug.** At noon a Soviet column arrived. Its commander offered to take them to Mukden if they found their own transport within three hours.
- **Mukden to Chungking.** They reached Mukden at 1 a.m. on 27 Aug and took off before 10 a.m. They landed at Sian, China, at 4:30 p.m. On the 28th they saw the American flag raised, the first for them in 39 months, and reached Chungking by 1:30 p.m.
- **Award at Chungking.** Wedemeyer presented him the **Distinguished Service Cross**. MacArthur had awarded it on 30 Jan 1942.
- **Reunion, 31 Aug.** He walked into the New Grand Hotel dining room on the cane MacArthur had left him. Both found it hard to speak. MacArthur said only that he was glad to see him, and Wainwright said the same back. He then told reporters he had waited more than three years for this day.
- **USS *Missouri*, 2 Sep.** MacArthur took **five** fountain pens from his pocket. Halfway through his signature he stopped and gave the pen to Wainwright, then wrote a bit more and gave the next one to Percival. He set three aside for U.S. institutions and finished with Mrs. MacArthur's small red pen. On the destroyer back, MacArthur offered him a command in the occupation army.
- **Baguio, 3 Sep.** Maj. Gen. Edmond H. Leavey, Styer's chief of staff, "took the surrender." Wainwright sat at the Allied center of the table with Styer. Leavey handed him one of his two signing pens. Yamashita wept as he was led away. Wainwright asked Styer to give Yamashita the courtesy due his rank.
- **Fourth star.** In Honolulu, Lt. Gen. Robert C. Richardson pinned eight silver stars on him (full general).
- **Homecoming.** Hamilton Field on 8 Sep. Washington National Airport at 12:30 p.m. on 10 Sep, where Marshall stood beside Mrs. Wainwright. More than 100,000 people gathered on the Monument Grounds, and his speech ended "Remember Bataan! Remember Corregidor!"
- **Captivity route** (map caption, 17 Nov):
  - Takao: 14 Aug 1942
  - Karenko: from 15 Aug 1942
  - Tamazato: April to 23 Jun 1943
  - Muksaq: 24 Jun 1943 to 5 Oct 1944
  - Then by way of Japan and Korea to Manchuria

Facts:
- **MacArthur's exit.** The PT boats cleared the minefields at 2115. A B-17 took off from Del Monte and landed at Darwin at 0900 on 17 Mar. His first statement in Australia was "I came through and I shall return" (*NYT*, 21 Mar 1942).
- **Farewell.** MacArthur cautioned, "You're an old cavalryman, Jonathan." Wainwright pledged: "I'll be on Bataan … if I'm alive." Morton adds: "It was a promise that he would be unable to keep." (Morton cites the memoir, pp. 2–4.)
- **6 May 1942, surrender of Corregidor.** Wainwright decided at 1000 to surrender, "to sacrifice one day of freedom in exchange for several thousand lives." Beebe read the surrender broadcast at 1030 and it was repeated at 1100 and 1145. The white flag went up at noon.
- **Message to Roosevelt** (rad., 6 May 42): "With broken heart and head bowed in sadness but not in shame … Please say to the nation that my troops and I have accomplished all that is humanly possible…"
- **Homma's demand at Cabcaben, 1700.** Homma refused any surrender that did not include "all American and Philippine troops in the Islands." Homma: "I wish only to negotiate with my equal." Wainwright gave in because the disarmed men on Corregidor "faced certain death." He later wrote that he thought of "close to 11,000 men and the wounded and nurses."
- **Surrender signed.** He signed with Col. Sato at San Jose at midnight. Morton calls it "in the bitterest moment of his life."
- **Physical contrast at Cabcaben.** Homma weighed "close to two hundred pounds." Wainwright, "over six feet tall," weighed "only about 160 pounds." A Japanese correspondent called him "thin as a crane" (Morton fn 23: source of this quotation unknown).
- **The KZRH broadcast, 7 May 1942.** He was taken to Manila and, "shortly before midnight," broadcast the surrender order at Radio Station KZRH "in a voice husky with suppressed emotion." He had objected "strenuously."
- **MacArthur's response** (rad. to Sharp, 9 May): "Orders emanating from General Wainwright have no validity." To Marshall the same day: "I believe Wainwright has temporarily become unbalanced."
- **Morton's verdict.** MacArthur "could not have realized" that Wainwright's fear for the 11,000 men drove his decision.

- The medal was presented in the White House Rose Garden on his return to Washington.

**[7]** C. Douglas Sterner, "Douglas MacArthur & Jonathan Wainwright," HomeOfHeroes: https://www.homeofheroes.com/?p=55866
- Marshall proposed the Medal of Honor on 30 Jul 1942. The 30 July date is corroborated by Murphy, *Heroes of WWII* (1990), and O'Reilly, *Killing the Rising Sun* (2016), via archive.org full-text search.
- MacArthur's radiogram, a U.S. government record, as quoted on the page:

  > "The citation proposed does not represent the truth. As a relative matter award of the Medal of Honor to General Wainwright would be a grave injustice to a number of general officers of practically equally responsible positions who not only distinguished themselves by fully as great personal gallantry thereby earning the DSC but exhibited powers of leadership and inspiration to a degree greatly superior to that of General Wainwright… It would be a grave mistake which later on might well lead to embarrassing repercussions to make this award."

- Marshall withdrew the recommendation. He revived it on 5 Sep 1945 and "this time there were no objections."

## Disagreements

1. **MacArthur's departure date.**
   - 11 Mar 1942: [7]. Also implied by [1], where on 10 Mar Sutherland says "tomorrow evening."
   - 12 Mar: Morton's HyperWar text [4].
   - The citation [5] dates Wainwright's command from 12 Mar.
2. **What MacArthur called him.**
   - "Jim": [10], [11].
   - Wainwright [1] says MacArthur is the only person who calls him Jonathan.
3. **The reunion wording.**
   - The "old corps" line is MacArthur's 1964 account [10].
   - Wainwright's 1945 account [2] has only "glad to see you." In his version the job offer, a command in the occupation army, comes on 2 Sep aboard the destroyer.
   - Truman (1964) called the reception cold. Casey [11] rebutted him.
4. **The pens.**
   - Five pens. MacArthur kept one; West Point, Annapolis, Percival and Wainwright got the others [8].
   - Five black pens plus Mrs. MacArthur's red one. Wainwright got the first, at mid-signature, then Percival, and three went to institutions [2].
   - A red Duofold went to his wife; West Point, Wainwright and Percival each got one [9].
   - Wainwright also received a second pen, from Leavey, at Baguio [2].
5. **Baguio.**
   - Yamashita surrendered "to Wainwright": Wikipedia's photo caption and a 1945 Gisborne Herald report.
   - Leavey took the surrender and Wainwright witnessed: Wainwright [2], and *Reports of General MacArthur* p. 464 (cited by Wikipedia's "Battle of Baguio" article).
6. **The rescue.**
   - Team leader: James T. Hennessy (Special Warfare journal; National WWII Museum podcast), or Robert F. Hennessy [12]. Wainwright spells it "Hennessey."
   - Leith: Sgt. [12] or Cpl. [2].
   - Mukden arrival: 1 a.m. 27 Aug [2], 1:30 a.m. 27 Aug [12], or 26 Aug [13].
   - Sian distance from Mukden: 100 miles [12][13] or 150 miles [14].
   - Chungking award: DSM [12] or DSC [2]. Prefer [2].
7. **The 1942 objection.**
   - Schultz (1981) quotes the radiogram as "does not repeat not represent the truth."
   - Pacific Wrecks dates MacArthur's wire to 31 Jul (**FLAG**: seen only as a search snippet).
8. **Captivity dates.**
   - Wikipedia [16] says he stayed at Tarlac "until the following summer." The 1945 map [2] puts him on Formosa by 15 Aug 1942.
   - Karenko to Tamazato: the map's OCR reads both April 2 and April 7, 1943.
9. **The surrender at Cabcaben.**
   - Uno says Wainwright offered to surrender all forces at Cabcaben.
   - Wainwright's own account differs.
   - Morton follows Uno (fn 27–28).
10. **Weight.**
    - About 160 lb at the surrender [4].
    - 132 lb on 3 Oct 1942 [3].
    - **FLAG**: no reliable weight at liberation found. Use "gaunt" or "emaciated" [7][8].
11. **Crowds.**
    - New York: 4,000,000 [3]. **FLAG**: this is his own estimate; check *NYT*, 14 Sep 1945.
    - Washington: "more than 100,000" [2].

## Arc (all beats sourced above)

1. Cold open, 2 Sep 1945: halfway through his name, MacArthur stops and hands the pen to the man he had once said should not get the Medal of Honor [2][7].
2. March 1942: the cigars and the promise of a third star [1][4].
3. May 1942: "broken heart … but not in shame," Homma's demand, and the midnight broadcast [4].
4. MacArthur's "unbalanced" radiogram, then his July 1942 veto of the medal [4][7].
5. Karenko: a Japanese reporter asks about a court-martial, and Wainwright writes his defense into his diary. His weight falls to 132 lb [2][3].
6. 19 Aug 1945: the OSS doctor arrives, and Wainwright asks what America thinks of him [2][12][14].
7. 31 Aug: two versions of the reunion [2][10][11].
8. The Missouri pens, then the Baguio pen [2][8].
9. 10 Sep: Truman's "walk in the garden," and the medal he never expected [3][5].
10. Coda: he dies on 2 Sep 1953, eight years to the day after the Missouri ceremony [16].
