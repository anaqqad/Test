# Wainwright Expected MacArthur to Disgrace Him. Then He Handed Him the Pen.

## 1. The pen

The second of September 1945, aboard the battleship Missouri in Tokyo Bay. The Japanese delegation has signed. General Douglas MacArthur steps to the table and calls two names. Will General Percival and General Wainwright step forward and accompany me while I sign? A thin officer with white hair walks out of the crowd and stands behind him.

MacArthur sits down and begins to write his name. Halfway through, he stops, turns around, and hands the pen to the thin man behind him. Three years earlier, MacArthur had written to Washington that giving this same man the Medal of Honor would be a grave injustice. And for three years, in prison camps from the Philippines to Manchuria, this man had believed that America blamed him for the worst surrender in its history.

This is the documented story of General Jonathan Wainwright: the man MacArthur left behind on Corregidor, the surrender he never forgave himself for, and the day he found out what his country really thought of him.

## 2. The promise

March 1942. The Philippines are cut off. On the island fortress of Corregidor, at the mouth of Manila Bay, MacArthur has orders from President Roosevelt to leave for Australia. Before he goes, he calls in Wainwright, the lanky old cavalryman everyone calls Skinny.

MacArthur tells him to say to the troops that he is leaving on the president's repeated orders, and against his own repeated protests. He promises to come back as soon as he can, with as much as he can. He hands Wainwright a box of cigars and two jars of shaving cream. And he promises him a third star, if he is still on Bataan when MacArthur returns. Wainwright answers that he will be there, if he is alive.

That night, MacArthur's PT boats slip through the minefields in the dark. A week later, in Australia, he tells reporters: I came through, and I shall return. Behind him, Wainwright is left in command of a starving army on Bataan and Corregidor, with no help on the way.

## 3. The surrender

On the ninth of April, Bataan falls. Corregidor holds on for four more weeks under Japanese artillery. On the morning of the sixth of May, Wainwright makes the decision of his life. He will sacrifice, as the Army's official history puts it, one day of freedom in exchange for several thousand lives.

He sends a last message to Roosevelt. With broken heart and head bowed in sadness but not in shame, he writes, my troops and I have accomplished all that is humanly possible. At noon, the white flag goes up over Corregidor.

But that is not enough for the Japanese. That afternoon, at Cabcaben on Bataan, General Masaharu Homma refuses to accept the surrender of Corregidor alone. He wants every American and Filipino soldier in the Philippines. When Wainwright tries to limit it, Homma tells him he will only negotiate with his equal. Wainwright thinks of the eleven thousand disarmed men on Corregidor, and gives in. The next night, in a Manila radio studio, he reads the order to surrender to the whole country, in a voice husky with suppressed emotion.

## 4. The radiogram

In Australia, MacArthur's reaction is cold. Orders from General Wainwright, he radios to his commanders in the south, have no validity. To General Marshall, the Army's chief of staff, he goes further. I believe, he writes, that Wainwright has temporarily become unbalanced.

Two months later, Marshall proposes Wainwright for the Medal of Honor. MacArthur answers with a radiogram that is still in the files. The citation proposed, he writes, does not represent the truth. Giving it to Wainwright would be a grave injustice to other generals who had shown leadership greatly superior to his. It would be a grave mistake, MacArthur warns, which later on might well lead to embarrassing repercussions. Marshall withdraws the recommendation.

Wainwright never sees any of this. He is already a prisoner.

## 5. The camps

He is held first at Tarlac, on Luzon, with the other captured generals. In August 1942 the Japanese ship them to Karenko, on Formosa, By October, Wainwright weighs one hundred and thirty-two pounds, forty pounds under his normal weight.

At Karenko, a Japanese correspondent asks him a question he cannot stop thinking about. Will you be court-martialed, he asks, for surrendering? Wainwright answers that he acted on the authority of his commander in chief. But afterwards he sits down with his diary and writes out his defense, reason by reason. No help was coming. The island would have fallen within a day or two. In the tunnels of Corregidor lay a thousand sick and wounded men, and more than a hundred nurses. If he had sacrificed them all, he wrote, some would have called him a fool, or a criminal.

He is moved again, and again: to other camps on Formosa, then through Japan and Korea to Manchuria. In December 1944, he and fifteen other senior prisoners end up at Sian, a small camp in the cold north, about a hundred miles beyond Mukden.

## 6. The question

On the sixteenth of August 1945, the day after Japan announced its surrender, a six-man team from the OSS, America's wartime intelligence service, parachutes onto the airfield at Mukden. Their mission is to find the prisoners. Three days later, two of them reach the camp at Sian: an Army doctor, Major Lamar, and an interpreter named Hal Leith. They are the first free Americans Wainwright has seen since the sixth of May 1942.

He has one question for them. What do people at home think of him? Leith later remembered that Wainwright expected to be blamed for the surrender. The Americans tell him he is a hero. He does not believe them.

It takes them a week to get out. A Soviet column, rain, a derailed train. They reach Mukden at one in the morning on the twenty-seventh and fly out the same day. On the twenty-eighth of August, in western China, Wainwright sees the American flag go up for the first time in thirty-nine months.

## 7. The reunion

Two days later, MacArthur lands in Japan in his plane, which he had named Bataan. On the evening of the thirty-first of August, at the New Grand Hotel in Yokohama, Wainwright walks into the dining room, leaning on the cane MacArthur had left him in 1942.

What happened next depends on who tells it. In his own account, published that fall, Wainwright says both men found it hard to speak, and that MacArthur only said he was glad to see him. In his memoirs, nearly twenty years later, MacArthur wrote that Wainwright was haggard and aged, his hair white, his skin like old shoe leather, and that his voice failed. He thought he was in disgrace, MacArthur wrote, and he told him his old corps was his whenever he wanted it. A general sitting at the table remembered that MacArthur rose at once and embraced him, and that Wainwright broke into tears.

The photograph taken that night survives. MacArthur is gripping Wainwright's arms. Wainwright is smiling. It is the face of a man who had spent three years expecting the opposite.

## 8. The ceremony

Two days later, he stands behind MacArthur on the deck of the Missouri. According to Wainwright's own account, MacArthur took five pens out of his pocket. Halfway through his signature he stopped and handed the first pen to Wainwright. Then he wrote a little more and gave the next one to General Percival, the British commander who had surrendered Singapore. Two men who had signed two of the biggest surrenders of the war now stood at the front of the victory.

The next day Wainwright flew back to the Philippines. At Baguio, in the mountains of Luzon, the Japanese commander in the islands, General Yamashita, surrendered his forces. Wainwright sat at the center of the table. The American general who took the surrender handed him one of his pens too.

## 9. The medal

On the fifth of September 1945, Marshall put Wainwright's name forward for the Medal of Honor once again. This time, according to the Army's records, there were no objections.

On the tenth, Wainwright landed in Washington. More than a hundred thousand people packed the grounds of the Washington Monument to hear him. Remember Bataan, he told them. Remember Corregidor. Then he went to the White House. President Truman suggested a walk in the garden, for the photographers. In the Rose Garden, to Wainwright's surprise, Truman read out a citation and fastened the Medal of Honor around his neck. Three days later, New York gave him a ticker-tape parade.

In his private diary that June, Truman had written that America would have had a real general and a fighting man if it had had Wainwright.

## 10. The date

Jonathan Wainwright commanded the Fourth Army after the war and retired in 1947. When he told his story, he kept the cigars, the cane and the pen in it.

He died on the second of September 1953, eight years to the day after he stood behind MacArthur on the Missouri. In 1942, MacArthur had warned that honoring him would be a grave mistake. In 1945, MacArthur handed him the first pen.
