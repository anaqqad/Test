# Sources: "German POWs Found a Camp With No Fence. Then Two Knocked on a Teacher's Door."

No source, no line. Every fact in `script.md` maps to one of the entries below.

## Why this topic (competitor research, 4 Nov 2026)
YouTube search sorted by views and by date (4 Nov 2026). The "Canada's camps had no fences" format is one of the strongest
POW formats of the year: "'You Mean We Can Just Walk?' German POWs Froze When They Saw Canada's Camps Had No Fences" (WW2 With
Alex, 747K in 6 months), "'You Can Go for a Walk If You Want' German POWs Couldn't Believe Canada's Camps Had No Fences"
(Canadians At War, 314K), "German POWs In Ontario Were Taken To Niagara Falls" (421K), and several new uploads in the last three
weeks ("German POWs Expected Barbed Wire in Canada. Their Camp Had No Fence at All.", "The Canadian Trick That Turned 9,000
German POWs Into Lumberjacks"). Almost all are dramatized, with invented dialogue. The documented version: the Whitewater camp in
Riding Mountain National Park, Manitoba, described as having no fence at all, and the one night in February 1944 that a
historian reconstructed from a guard officer's report and the teacher's own account (Waiser, "The Teacher's Tale"). Searches
"riding mountain pow camp", "whitewater pow camp": only a 2021 Valour Canada short and old Parks Canada clips; nothing tells the
teacher's story. Hook = the knock on the farmhouse door in a snowstorm, 19 Feb. 1944.

## Main sources
1. **Bill Waiser, "Riding Mountain POWs: The Teacher's Tale," *Manitoba History* No. 61, Fall 2009** (Manitoba Historical
   Society, mhs.mb.ca/docs/mb_history/61/ridingmountainpows.shtml). The main source:
   - Canada's "limited liability" war; by end of 1942 over 16,000 German POWs in Canada, most at Lethbridge and Medicine Hat;
     eventually 34,000 at more than two dozen sites.
   - October 1943: 440 German prisoners moved from Medicine Hat to a woodcutting camp on Whitewater Lake, Riding Mountain
     National Park, near Dauphin; Labour and National Defence wanted to head off a winter fuel shortage; wood heated almost half
     of Canadian households. The Parks Bureau had used labour camps since 1915.
   - Cost "a third of a million dollars"; six large bunkhouses with their own washroom and showers; hospital; power plant. No
     fence, compound or guard tower; the boundary was "blazes on a ring of outlying trees."
   - Uniform: blue denim, red stripe down the outer trouser leg, large red circle on the back of shirt and jacket.
   - Supervised by the Veterans Guard. Prisoners trusted with the power plant, horses, trucks, trips to town for supplies.
     Leisure: talk, reading, games, performances, crafts, dugout canoes.
   - Night visits: crude compasses made from watches ordered from the "Eaton's catalogue"; visits to immigrant communities on the
     park's southern boundary (Olha, Horod); back before morning roll call; popular at dances; brought rationed sugar for local
     stills; farmers with sleighs picked them up at the boundary, gave clothing. Excuse: they had "simply gone for a hike and
     become lost." Not treated as escapees because they always came back. "The Mounties would not get involved unless the
     prisoners were reported missing or a civilian lodged a complaint."
   - Saturday 19 Feb. 1944: Lieutenant Mann, officer in charge of the Veterans Guard, led a late-night patrol; raided Peter
     Chrun's home near Seech; found two prisoners with Catherine Chastko, teacher at the Zaporoza School. Report: "The prisoners'
     visit had evidently been pre-arranged"; the teacher "admitted having previously met them at a dance." The patrol then
     broke up a wedding dance at Seech, near fight with local men incl. Chrun, who insisted the prisoners had never visited the
     teacher before. Seven Germans rounded up by the next morning. Mann notified the provincial Department of Education.
   - Chastko's account: teachers scarce; she was 17; after Grade 11, a six-week course at Wesley College, Winnipeg, summer 1943;
     placed at Zaporoza School about fifteen miles north of her family home in Shoal Lake; boarded with Peter and Anne Chrun and
     their two young daughters; lonely; at a Seech dance early in 1944 she first met the prisoners. 19 Feb.: the Chruns at a
     wedding dance about two miles away; around 10:30 p.m. two prisoners, Willi and Reinhard, knocked, seeking warmth in a
     snowstorm; jigsaw puzzle; less than an hour later Mann arrived and took them away; she, in tears, said she had never invited
     them; shouting and pushing at the wedding.
   - About two weeks later Willi and Reinhard came back at night to apologize, with a ship in a bottle and a handmade wooden box
     of chocolate bars; someone on the road had told them she was alone, and that person had told the guards; footprints outside
     a window (at least two people watching). Mann had stopped at Shoal Lake on his way back to complain to her father.
   - She never saw the two men again; others kept visiting. In April a prisoner watching her teach repaired the classroom clock;
     another sat on the school steps at recess, apologized and left. At the end of the school year "her teaching days were over";
     that summer, an assembly line in a munitions factory in Ontario.
   - She later became Catherine (Chastko) Dobrowski; phoned Waiser, upset about a passage (p. 235) of his book *Park Prisoners*
     on the raid; they met over coffee near Saskatoon (she was visiting her daughter).
   - The camp operated until spring 1945; the Germans had grown bored with woodcutting and deliberately slowed production;
     prisoners moved to other projects; a Winnipeg wrecking firm demolished the buildings; the Parks Bureau tried to erase all
     traces. Closing: the prisoners found a "home-away-from-home"; "The dances at Seech were never quite the same again."
2. **Michael O'Hagan, "Prisoners in the Park: German PoWs in Riding Mountain National Park," NiCHE, 21 June 2013**:
   440 PoWs arrived 26 Oct. 1943; occupied Oct. 1943 to Oct. 1945; Wartime Housing Ltd. first ran the camp; ~$300,000; more
   than fifteen buildings (bunkhouses, administration, guardhouse, kitchen and mess hall, barn, garage, powerplant), electricity,
   running water, sewer. Gangs; three-quarters of a cord per man per day; 8 hours a day, 6 days a week; early quotas rarely met
   (inexperience, dissent); output rose after training and after some pro-Nazi troublemakers were removed. No barbed wire or
   guard towers; planners believed the wilderness would contain the men; about seventy-five guards. **Five days after arrival,
   nineteen PoWs were found missing.** Contacts with civilians near the park edge, mostly Eastern European immigrants. Mid-1944
   the Department of Labour took over and cut the population to about 200. Fuelwood production ended 31 March 1945; remaining
   PoWs loaned to local farmers for the summer. Closed 1 Sept. 1945; demolished. Bear cub photo, 1944 (RMNP Collection).
3. **Michael O'Hagan, "Prisoner of War Canoes," powsincanada.ca, 26 June 2017**: May 1943 Ottawa approved POW labour in the
   lumber and agricultural industries; ~300 labour projects 1943-46; remote bush camps had no barbed wire or guard towers.
   Riding Mountain opened Oct. 1943 with 440 POWs from Camp 132 (Medicine Hat). Canoe idea reportedly from a Canadian magazine
   with a birch-bark canoe on the cover; dugouts from large spruce logs despite the park warden's request to spare the spruce;
   12-16 ft, one- and two-man; launched in the creek on the camp's southern boundary; paddling allowed if away from the shoreline
   and back before roll call. A few POWs drowned at other camps; orders from Ottawa restricted canoeing; at Riding Mountain first
   limited to supervised paddling, then prohibited. Some canoes still floating in the creek ~30 years later; two at the Fort
   Dauphin Museum; remains in the reeds.
4. **Ned Morgan, "Captive Canoemaker," *Canoeroots*, Early Summer 2012 (paddlingmag.com)**: Unteroffizier Konstantin
   Schwartz's diary: "The lake was ours." Most prisoners were Afrika Korps captured in Egypt after the Second Battle of El
   Alamein (late 1942). Adrian Myers (Stanford): trees big enough were set aside for recreational use; tools: bow saw, axes,
   chisels, hammers, knives; six canoes found; "The historical documentation mentions a flotilla"; "They might've made 20 or 30."
5. **"Camp that held Rommel's men surrenders its secrets," *The Scotsman*, 25 Aug. 2011**: Red Cross tins, letters,
   photographs, uniform buttons, mess tins; Myers: "They volunteered to come here on the understanding that they would agree to
   work hard once they arrived"; choir, sports associations, reading clubs; many wrote home of their "good treatment"; many
   personal grooming and hygiene articles ("keen to look and smell their best"); carcasses of small dogs kept as pets. Klaus
   Meyer, near Cologne: "Escape was impossible. So we made the best of it. The treatment was very good on the whole. We weren't
   die-hard Nazis; we were young soldiers who got caught and wanted to go home when the war was finished."
6. **Donna Gamache, "Prisoner of war camp in RMNP," *Manitoba Co-operator*, 22 July 2010**: first planned for Lake Audy's south
   shore, built ~15 km west on the northeast shore of Whitewater Lake; fire-damaged trees; isolation; Mennonite conscientious
   objectors helped build; building began early August 1943 after heavy rain; ~15 buildings incl. six bunkhouses, office, guard
   quarters, cookhouse and dining room, store, barns, machine shop, blacksmith shop, small hospital; ~$300,000; red blazes on
   trees. Visits to Olha, Seech and Crawford Park. Arrival 26 Oct. 1943. 50 cents a day; quota usually missed, tensions, some
   ardent Nazis, slowdowns. Jobs: power plant, driving to Dauphin for supplies, canteen, fishing, pigs. Firewood south to
   Elphinstone and north to Dauphin. Radio, theatre, music, sports, carving incl. model battleships. Black bear cub mascot raided
   the kitchen, hibernated under a building. March 1944: a prisoner killed by a falling tree; funeral in Dauphin attended by
   prisoners. Buildings removed by spring 1946. Campground, picnic tables; foundations; canoe remains in the old creek bed;
   a ~12 ft canoe at the Fort Dauphin museum.
7. **Wikipedia, "Whitewater (POW camp)"** (and refs: Parks Canada; Waiser; Toronto Star 2011; CTV 2011; Winnipeg Free Press
   2011): ~300 km NW of Winnipeg; fifteen buildings; 440-450 prisoners; 50 cents a day; Eaton's catalogue; threatened strike
   when "pyjamas they'd ordered from the Eaton's catalogue failed to arrive on time"; "only POW camp in North America" without a
   fence; Veterans' Guard; dances; juvenile black bear; many locals of Ukrainian descent; buildings advertised for sale in the
   *Winnipeg Evening Tribune*, 10 Sept. 1945; digs 2010-2011 (Stanford and Brandon University).
8. **Bill Waiser, "Park Prisoners," *Canada's History*, Aug.-Sept. 2013**: 440 prisoners captured in North Africa; $330,000;
   red blazes; bear cub; boats and dugouts; night trips with Eaton's watches as compasses; work stoppages in spring 1945 led to
   closure; dismantled that fall.
9. **Caity Monroe, "Digging up WWII," *Stanford Daily*, 15 July 2010**: Adrian Myers's excavation; ~400 Afrika Korps
   soldiers; prisoners volunteered; artefacts: bottles, jars, ceramics, tin cans, canoes.
10. **Wikipedia, "Veterans Guard of Canada"**: First World War veterans, unfit for overseas duty; formed May 1940; guarded
   internment and POW camps.
11. **Medicine Hat**: Legal Archives of Alberta review of Nathan Greenfield, *Hanged in Medicine Hat* (2022): two prisoners,
   August Plaszek and Dr. Karl Lehmann, killed at Camp 132 by the camp's Nazi leadership as "traitors"; *Time* (archive) on
   Plaszek: called an anti-Nazi "swine" and hanged by fellow prisoners in 1943. Script says only: in 1943 a prisoner suspected
   of turning against Hitler was killed by his own comrades.

## Disagreements
- **Number of prisoners**: 440 (Waiser, O'Hagan, Gamache; Wikipedia 440-450); "an estimated 400" (Stanford Daily); "500"
  (Scotsman). Script: four hundred and forty.
- **Cost**: ~$300,000 (O'Hagan, Gamache), "a third of a million" (Waiser 2009), $330,000 (Waiser 2013). Script: "about three
  hundred thousand dollars".
- **"The only camp without a fence"**: Wikipedia and the Scotsman (Myers) say the only one in North America / among Allied
  camps; O'Hagan writes that the remote bush camps in general had no barbed wire or guard towers. Script says "some called it the
  only one", then that other bush camps had none either.
- **Closure**: spring 1945 (Waiser 2009, operations), production ended 31 March 1945 and camp closed 1 Sept. 1945 (O'Hagan),
  decommissioned August 1945 (Gamache), "late 1945" (Wikipedia), occupied to October 1945 (O'Hagan). Script: woodcutting ended
  in March 1945, the camp closed in September.
- **The nineteen missing prisoners**: O'Hagan gives no outcome. Script says only that they were found missing.
- **The teacher**: Mann's report says the visit was pre-arranged and that she admitted meeting them at a dance; she said she
  never invited them. Both are told.
- **Why her teaching ended**: Waiser says only that "her teaching days were over" and that Mann notified the Department of
  Education; he does not say the department acted. Script says the record does not show what was done with the report.
- **Guards**: Veterans Guard (Waiser, Wikipedia); about seventy-five guards, civilian and military (O'Hagan).
