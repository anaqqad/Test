# German POWs Found a Camp With No Fence. Then Two Knocked on a Teacher's Door.

## 1. The knock

On a Saturday night in February 1944, in a snowstorm, a seventeen-year-old schoolteacher was alone in a farmhouse in Manitoba, in western Canada. At about half past ten, someone knocked on the door.

Two young men stood in the snow. They wore blue denim, with a red stripe down each trouser leg and a big red circle on the back of the jacket. They were German prisoners of war. They said their names were Willi and Reinhard, and they asked to come in out of the storm.

She let them in. They sat at the table and helped her with a jigsaw puzzle. Less than an hour later, the door opened again, and a Canadian officer walked in.

This is the documented story of a prisoner of war camp in the Canadian forest that had no fence, of the men who walked out of it at night and came back by morning, and of the teacher who paid for one of those nights.

## 2. Rommel's men

Most of the prisoners came from the Afrika Korps, the German army in North Africa. Thousands were captured in Egypt after the British victory at El Alamein, late in 1942. Britain had no room to hold them. Ships carried them across the Atlantic, and trains took them north into Canada.

By the end of 1942, Canada held more than sixteen thousand German prisoners, most of them in two huge camps in Alberta, at Lethbridge and Medicine Hat.

Medicine Hat was crowded, and hard Nazis ran the barracks. In 1943, a prisoner suspected of turning against Hitler was killed there by his own comrades. Whitewater would be a long way from that compound.

## 3. The shortage

In the winter of 1942, Canada ran short of firewood. Almost half the homes in the country were heated with wood. In May 1943, Ottawa decided that prisoners of war could be sent to work in the forests and on the farms.

The government chose Riding Mountain National Park, in western Manitoba, about three hundred kilometres northwest of Winnipeg. Old fires had left dead timber there, and the place was remote. The camp went up in the late summer of 1943, on the northeast shore of a lake called Whitewater.

## 4. The camp

It was a well built camp. There were about fifteen buildings: six large bunkhouses, each with its own washroom and showers, a kitchen and dining hall, a small hospital, barns, a machine shop, and a power plant. It cost about three hundred thousand dollars.

It did not have a fence. There was no barbed wire and no guard tower. The boundary was a ring of trees, marked with red blazes. Beyond the trees there was forest, and beyond the forest, a continent at war with Germany. Some called it the only camp in North America without a fence. Historians who study Canada's bush camps say others had none either.

## 5. Volunteers

On October twenty-sixth, 1943, four hundred and forty prisoners arrived from Medicine Hat. According to the archaeologist who later dug at the site, they had volunteered to come, on the understanding that they would work hard once they arrived.

The work was cutting firewood. The men worked in gangs, eight hours a day, six days a week. Each man was expected to cut and stack three quarters of a cord a day. The pay was fifty cents a day.

Five days after they arrived, nineteen prisoners were found missing.

## 6. The forest

The planners had counted on the forest. Many years later, one of the prisoners, Klaus Meyer, put it simply. Escape, he said, was impossible. So they made the best of it.

The guards were men of the Veterans Guard of Canada, soldiers of the First World War who were too old to fight overseas. The camp trusted the prisoners with the power plant, the horses and the trucks, and some of them rode with the guards into the town of Dauphin to pick up supplies.

The prisoners spent their wages through the Eaton's catalogue, the mail-order book that reached farms all over Canada. Once, they threatened to go on strike because the pyjamas they had ordered were late. They also ordered watches. In the forest, some of them turned those watches into crude compasses.

## 7. The night trips

South of the park lived farm families, many of them immigrants from Ukraine and Eastern Europe, in small places called Olha, Horod, Seech, and Crawford Park. At night, prisoners slipped out of the camp, walked to the edge of the park, and were met by farmers with sleighs.

They went to the dances, where they were popular. They brought sugar, which was rationed, and which the farmers needed for their stills. Some farmers gave them clothes. Before the morning roll call, they were back in camp.

If they were caught, the story was always the same. They had gone for a hike, and got lost. The officers did not treat them as escapees, because they always came back. And the Mounted Police would not get involved unless a prisoner was reported missing, or a civilian complained.

## 8. The teacher

Catherine Chastko was seventeen. During the war, rural Manitoba was short of teachers. In the summer of 1943, after finishing grade eleven, she took a six-week course at a college in Winnipeg, and was sent to teach at the Zaporoza School, about fifteen miles north of her family's home in Shoal Lake.

She boarded with a farm family, Peter and Anne Chrun, and their two little daughters. She was lonely. Early in 1944, she went to a dance at Seech. That was where she first met the prisoners.

## 9. The night

On February nineteenth, 1944, the Chruns went to a wedding dance about two miles away. Catherine stayed home. It was snowing hard. At about half past ten, there was the knock at the door. Willi and Reinhard.

That same night, the officer in charge of the guards, Lieutenant Mann, had taken a patrol out of the camp. Less than an hour after the two prisoners came in, he walked into the Chrun house and ordered them out. Catherine was in tears. She told him she had never invited them.

Mann went on to the wedding dance. There was shouting and pushing between his men and the local men, among them Peter Chrun, who insisted the prisoners had never visited the teacher before. By the next morning, seven Germans had been rounded up and brought back to camp.

## 10. The report

Mann wrote a report. The prisoners' visit, he wrote, had evidently been pre-arranged. The teacher had admitted meeting them before, at a dance. He sent word to the provincial Department of Education. And on his way back, he stopped in Shoal Lake, to complain about her to her father.

## 11. The apology

About two weeks later, Willi and Reinhard came back, at night, to apologize. They brought a ship in a bottle, and a wooden box they had made, full of chocolate bars. They told her that someone on the road had said she was alone, and that the same person had told the guards. Outside a window there were footprints. At least two people had been watching.

She never saw Willi or Reinhard again. But the prisoners kept coming. In April, one of them stood watching her teach, noticed that the classroom clock was broken, and fixed it. Another sat on the school steps during recess, apologized, and left.

At the end of the school year, Catherine Chastko went home. Her teaching days were over. That summer she went to Ontario, and worked on an assembly line in a munitions factory. The record does not show what the Department of Education did with Mann's report.

## 12. The lake

Back at Whitewater, the prisoners had found another way out. With saws, axes and chisels, they carved dugout canoes from big spruce logs, twelve to sixteen feet long, even though the park warden asked them to spare the spruce. One account says the idea came from a Canadian magazine with a birch-bark canoe on the cover.

The guards let them paddle on Whitewater Lake, as long as they stayed away from the shore and were back before roll call. One prisoner, Konstantin Schwartz, wrote in his diary about paddling out of the reeds onto open water. The lake, he wrote, was ours.

Then prisoners at other camps drowned, and orders came from Ottawa. At Whitewater, canoeing was first limited, and then banned.

## 13. The bear

There was a choir, sports, reading clubs, a camp radio, and theatre. The camp had a mascot, a black bear cub, which raided the kitchen and spent the winter asleep under one of the buildings.

Not every day was easy. In March 1944, a prisoner was killed by a falling tree. The others went to his funeral in Dauphin. And not every prisoner was content. Some were ardent Nazis, and there was tension in the work gangs.

## 14. The end

In the middle of 1944, the camp was cut to about two hundred men. By the spring of 1945, the prisoners were bored with cutting wood, and slowed down on purpose. Woodcutting ended in March 1945. That summer, the last men were loaned to local farmers. In September, the camp closed.

The buildings were advertised for sale in a Winnipeg newspaper, and a wrecking firm took them down. By the spring of 1946, they were gone. The Parks Bureau tried to erase every trace of a prison camp in the park.

## 15. What happened to them

Klaus Meyer went home. Many years later, living near Cologne, he remembered the fresh air and the open spaces, and said the treatment had been very good. They were not die-hard Nazis, he said. They were young soldiers who got caught, and wanted to go home.

Catherine Chastko married, and became Catherine Dobrowski. Decades later, she read a book by the historian Bill Waiser that described the raid on the farmhouse, and she phoned him, upset by how it was told. In 2009, Waiser published her side of the story.

Between 2009 and 2011, archaeologists dug at Whitewater. They found Red Cross tins, buttons, letters, and photographs, the graves of small dogs the prisoners had kept as pets, and a surprising number of grooming and hygiene items. Men who lived deep in the forest had wanted to look their best. Two of the canoes were pulled from the creek, and are kept at the Fort Dauphin Museum.

## 16. Echo

Today there is a campground at Whitewater Lake, with picnic tables and a fire pit. In the grass are concrete foundations. And along the old creek, in the reeds, lie the remains of canoes that German prisoners carved from Canadian spruce, at a camp where the only wall was a ring of marked trees.
