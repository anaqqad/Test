# The Air Raid Was a Trap. 11 of 150 POWs Swam Out Alive.

## 1. The alarm

Two o'clock in the afternoon, the fourteenth of December 1944. Puerto Princesa, on the Philippine island of Palawan. An air raid alarm sounds over a prison camp holding one hundred and fifty American prisoners of war. The guards shout that hundreds of American planes are coming. And for the first time ever, the guards force every prisoner down into the shelters.

The men in the trenches let themselves hope. American planes over Palawan meant American ships close behind. Sergeant Douglas Bogue, a Marine, later swore that they thought it might be the real thing. Then, through the narrow openings of their shelters, they watched about sixty Japanese soldiers walk toward them. They carried machine guns and rifles. Some of them carried buckets.

Out of one hundred and fifty men, eleven lived. This is the documented story of the Palawan massacre: how eleven men got out, through a fence, down a cliff and across five miles of open water, and how what they told the Army may have helped save five hundred other prisoners a few weeks later.

## 2. The airfield

The story starts in August 1942. Four months after the surrender of Bataan and Corregidor, about three hundred and fifty American prisoners were shipped from the camp at Cabanatuan to Palawan, a long, narrow island at the western edge of the Philippines. Their job was to build an airfield for the Japanese, by hand.

For two years they cut down jungle with axes, crushed coral and poured concrete under the tropical sun. They lived on wormy rice and soup made of vines. A camp cook broke two men's arms for picking papayas. One Marine, Glenn McDole, had his appendix taken out without anaesthetic. And the camp had one rule above all others. For every prisoner who escaped, ten would be executed.

In September 1944, one hundred and fifty-nine of the men were shipped back to Manila. One hundred and fifty stayed behind on Palawan. Nobody knew it yet, but the ones who left were the lucky ones.

## 3. The warnings

On the nineteenth of October 1944, American B-24 bombers appeared over Palawan for the first time. One pilot flew low over the camp and waggled his wings at the prisoners, then went on to bomb the airfield they had built. On the twenty-eighth the bombers came back. The guards took it out on the prisoners. After every raid, an Army report said later, the treatment got worse.

After the raids, the prisoners were ordered to dig air raid shelters. Three trenches, five feet deep and four feet wide, each long enough for fifty men, covered over with logs and earth. The Japanese wanted one entrance for each trench. The American officers argued until they were allowed two. Marine Rufus Smith noticed something else. Just over the fence, a little way from the trenches, stood drums of gasoline.

The guards had been saying it for months. Smith later swore that one of them told the prisoners that if the Americans landed, they would never live to see the American Army. On the tenth of December, a Japanese lookout spotted a huge American convoy at sea. It was heading for another island, Mindoro. But on Palawan, the Japanese expected a landing. According to the historian Stephen Moore, on the thirteenth of December an order came down from the Japanese air division commander. If the prisoners were harboring hostile feelings, they were to be disposed of at the appropriate time.

## 4. The trenches

At about two in the morning on the fourteenth, the prisoners heard Japanese soldiers moving around outside the compound. At dawn they went out to work as usual, but the soldiers were in full field gear. At noon, all the work parties were called back to the camp. The guards called one air raid alarm after another. Bogue saw two American fighters high overhead. Fern Barta, a Navy radioman, later swore that he never saw a single plane.

At about two o'clock, the men were forced under cover. Bogue wrote that it was the first time the Japanese had ever made them do that. Then the soldiers came. They went to the shelter of A Company first, where about forty men were crowded together. They threw a lit torch into the entrance, followed by a bucket or two of gasoline. The gasoline exploded.

Men ran out of the shelter on fire, straight into machine gun fire. Four American officers who shared a foxhole near A Company, among them the camp's doctor, never had a chance. In the other shelters, the prisoners heard the explosion and understood. Bogue turned to the two men in his foxhole, which was close to the fence. Our only chance, he told them, is to get through the fence.

Some men clawed through the barbed wire with their bare hands. Others dug out through the far end of their trench. Barta crawled through a drain that ran under the fence and came out at the top of a cliff, forty or fifty feet above the beach. Thirty or forty men made it down to the rocks along the shore. For most of them, it was not far enough.

## 5. The beach

The soldiers came down to the beach after them. They hunted through the rocks with rifles, bayonets and grenades. They blasted men out of crevices with dynamite. A barge moved slowly along the shore, firing at anyone who tried to swim. Bogue wedged himself into the rocks and lay still. He stayed there for seven hours, listening.

Army private Eugene Nielsen covered himself with coconut husks. The soldiers found him, and he played dead until they moved on. He crawled into a cove where fifteen Americans were hiding. Eight of them were shot from the barge. When the soldiers came for the rest, Nielsen ran into the sea. Bullets hit him in the leg, in the head and in the ribs. He kept swimming. A strong current carried him out, and at three in the afternoon, the Army report says, he reached the far side of a bay five miles wide.

On the cliff above, Army sergeant Willie Balchus and another prisoner, Elmo Deal, were cornered by three soldiers carrying torches. Balchus hit the first one on the head with a big piece of coral, and the two Americans jumped the others. More soldiers came running. Deal went over the cliff. Balchus ran down the beach and into the water.

## 6. The bay

At about nine that night, Bogue crept out of the rocks and found three more survivors: Barta, Edwin Petry and Alberto Pacheco. A fifth man, a Marine named Donald Martyn, joined them. There was only one way out. The five men waded into the black water and started to swim. Behind them, Bogue later swore, they could see fires on the shore.

Halfway across, Barta passed out. When he came to, he was floating out of the mouth of the bay, about fifty yards from a beach. He reached it at sunrise. Martyn, who Barta said was a strong swimmer, was never seen again. Rufus Smith crossed too. He later said that a shark bit him on the arm and shoulder in the water, and that he made it anyway.

Glenn McDole hid near the beach with a wounded man named Hamrick, the same man Barta had tied a tourniquet on during the attack. McDole stayed with him for four days, until Hamrick died of gangrene on the eighteenth of December. Then McDole swam the bay alone. Too exhausted to go on, he spent a whole night clinging to a fish trap in the water.

## 7. The convicts

The men who found McDole the next morning were prisoners themselves. Across the bay lay the Iwahig Penal Colony, a Philippine prison farm, and its convicts were out gathering the fish from their traps. Bogue, after five days and nights in the jungle with nothing but rainwater, was rescued by the same convicts. The prisoners of one camp had saved the prisoners of another.

From there, Filipino guerrillas took over. They dressed the Americans in civilian clothes and moved them south along the island to the guerrilla base at Brooke's Point. On the sixth of January 1945, a Catalina flying boat landed off Brooke's Point and took out the first six survivors. On the twenty-first, a second flying boat came for Bogue, Barta and McDole.

Eleven men had made it out alive: five soldiers, three Marines, two airmen and one sailor. Nine of them were off the island by the end of January. Their sworn statements were classified secret.

## 8. The five hundred

By the seventh of January, Army intelligence had heard Nielsen's story. Japanese guards, facing an American landing, had killed an entire camp of prisoners. And on Luzon, the American army was about to land and push past another camp, at Cabanatuan, where about five hundred prisoners were still being held.

On the twenty-sixth of January, the guerrilla leader Major Robert Lapham warned that the prisoners at Cabanatuan were in danger of being killed. Four days later, Army Rangers and Filipino guerrillas slipped behind the Japanese lines, attacked the camp at night and carried out five hundred and thirteen prisoners. Historians link the raid to what the Army had learned from Palawan. The records do not spell out how much it weighed. But by then, the Army knew what guards on that island had done when they believed the Americans were coming.

## 9. What they found

On the twenty-eighth of February 1945, American troops of the Forty-first Infantry Division landed at Puerto Princesa. In the empty prison camp they found identification tags, personal belongings and scraps of records. In March, an Army graves unit worked through the trenches and buried the remains of seventy-nine men one by one.

In early June, through the Swiss government, the United States sent Tokyo a formal protest over the massacre of one hundred and fifty American prisoners of war. A few days later, General Douglas MacArthur came to see the camp for himself.

## 10. The verdict

In August 1948, sixteen Japanese soldiers went on trial at Yokohama for the Palawan massacre. Six were acquitted. The air division commander who sent the order received life in prison. A sergeant of the military police was sentenced to death, but his sentence was cut to thirty years. The camp commander, known to the prisoners as the Weasel, and the lieutenant who led the soldiers to the trenches were never tried.

On the thirty-first of December 1958, the last of the men convicted for Palawan walked out of Sugamo prison, free. Fourteen years after the fire, none of them was still behind bars.

## 11. The grave

In 1952, the remains from Palawan were brought home and buried together in a single grave at Jefferson Barracks National Cemetery, in Saint Louis. One hundred and twenty-three men lie in that one grave, because their remains could not be told apart. In Puerto Princesa, a marker now stands on the site of the camp. It says that some one hundred and fifty American prisoners of war were burned to death there.

And in the files of the war crimes trials, there is still the map that Sergeant Bogue drew of the camp. The fence, the shelters, the cliff and the bay. And underneath, the date he never forgot. Fourteen December 1944.
