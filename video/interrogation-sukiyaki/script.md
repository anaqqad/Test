# Japanese POWs Were Sure They'd Be Tortured. Then They Were Served Sukiyaki.

## 1. Cold open

On September sixteenth, 1944, a group of Japanese prisoners of war sat down to lunch on an island in San Francisco Bay. Every one of them knew what capture was supposed to mean. Torture first, and then death. Lunch was sukiyaki, cabbage salad, rice, and caramel pudding.

Nobody had beaten them. The prisoners played cards and Mah-Jong, listened to the camp gramophone, and whispered among themselves, trying to work out what their captors really wanted.

They were right to wonder. The food was not an accident. It was the first step of a plan. Within days, many of these men would be driven to a former luxury hotel in the hills east of the bay, where the walls were wired for sound.

## 2. Why so few

These men were rare. The Japanese military field code told every soldier that rather than live and bear the shame of imprisonment, he should die. Soldiers were told to save the last round for themselves. From the first shots of the Pacific war through Midway, Guadalcanal and Tarawa, the Allies took only six hundred and four Japanese prisoners.

Some of that was the code. Some of it was the Americans. The army's own history admitted that American troops, fearful of the enemy's reputation for treachery, were reluctant to take prisoners at all. One confidential intelligence memo conceded that it had taken the promise of three days' leave and some ice cream to get soldiers to bring in the first live prisoner.

On Guadalcanal, in the first six weeks of 1943, the American Fourteenth Corps took eighty-four Japanese prisoners. Thirty-three of them were too sick or wounded to walk. To army intelligence, a prisoner who could talk was rare, and worth a great deal.

So the Americans kept the ones they wanted. Under an agreement with Australia in September 1942, most Japanese captives were handed over to the Australians. The men shipped to the United States were chosen largely for one reason: what they might know.

## 3. Angel Island

They came ashore at Angel Island, a small, steep island in San Francisco Bay that had been a quarantine station for immigrants before the war. They were deloused, given serial numbers, and examined for malaria, skin disease, worms and wounds.

Then came a postal card. Every prisoner could send one to tell his family he was alive, and his name could be passed to the Red Cross. Nearly all of them asked for the opposite. They asked that their families not be told. Better to be thought dead than known to be a prisoner.

## 4. The contradiction

They expected to be tortured and killed, and from where they stood, the fear made sense. They despised their captors, and they knew they were hated in return. What they got instead was the menu of September sixteenth. Sausages, rice and apples for breakfast. Sukiyaki and caramel pudding for lunch. Spaghetti and hash, baked tomatoes, cakes and cocoa for dinner.

The meals were written down by Red Cross and State Department inspectors that day. The prisoners, the record shows, were better fed in captivity than they had been in their own army. But for a newly captured man, the first meal in America was hard to explain.

## 5. The plan

The War Department had its reasons. These prisoners had been brought to America to be questioned, and frightened men made poor sources. Army intelligence had already learned that Japanese prisoners passed through three distinct phases. Get the timing right, and a man who expected to die would talk freely.

In the first forty-eight hours, a prisoner was nearly useless. He was sure he was about to die, and he either said nothing or said things that could not be trusted. After several days, something changed. He realized nobody was going to hurt him. Fear turned into gratitude, and gratitude into a wish to repay his captors by talking.

That window lasted ten days to two weeks. Then came the third phase. The prisoner got used to the food and the kindness, grew annoyed at the questions, and withdrew into a shell. After that, he was no longer a reliable source. The sukiyaki was not the end of the plan. It started the clock.

## 6. The hotel

The clock ran at a place called Byron Hot Springs. A resort had opened there in 1889, around mineral springs in the hills of Contra Costa County. A four-story brick hotel opened in 1914. In the nineteen-twenties it drew wealthy guests and celebrities to its mud baths and mineral soaks.

The resort closed in 1938, after a series of lawsuits. In May 1942, the War Department chose it for one of only two secret interrogation centers in the United States. The other was Fort Hunt, near Washington. On paper, it was known only by an address that gave nothing away: Post Office Box six fifty-one, Tracy, California.

The construction plans were labeled Officers' School. As one historian put it, the workers must have wondered why officers needed eight-foot fences, barred windows and heavy gates. Microphones were hidden in the light fixtures. Wires ran through hollow walls and down an elevator shaft to a transcription room, where the prisoners' conversations were listened to and written down.

The camp never held more than fifty-one prisoners at a time. Some vents let a man standing on a toilet see into the next room, which researchers believe was meant to get prisoners talking to each other. Even the trees outside carried listening devices.

## 7. The interrogators

There were forty-five interrogators, and many were Japanese Americans, trained at the army's Military Intelligence Service Language School in Minnesota. Each interrogator was given one prisoner, and stayed with him until there was nothing left to learn.

On the surface, the sessions were informal: forty-five minutes to an hour, two or three times a day, for a week or more. Underneath, every question was planned. A long checklist covered weapons, fuel, rations, morale, rumors, commanders, and what a man expected after the war.

Every four days or so, the findings went out in short reports. They ranged from the falling quality of Japanese clothing to the number of boiler rooms in the aircraft carrier Hiryu. The camp's report for 1944 counted one thousand and seventy-seven Japanese prisoners questioned, from thirteen in January to one hundred and seventy-four in August.

## 8. What worked

What made them talk was rarely force. Army intelligence found that no threat of violence or solitary confinement worked as well as a simpler one: the threat to send a prisoner's name to his family in Japan. The men who had asked that their families never be told now had the most to lose.

Another lesson surprised the Americans. German prisoners had to be questioned alone, before older prisoners could pressure them. With the Japanese, it worked the other way. Older prisoners told new arrivals to tell everything, so that the whole group would not be blamed for lying. Some came back the next day, on their own, to correct their answers.

## 9. The cost

None of this was strictly legal. The secret centers, the historian Arnold Krammer concluded, bent or broke about a dozen articles of the Geneva Convention of 1929. Japan had never ratified that convention. The War Department decided the information was worth the risk, and reasoned that the treatment of American prisoners in Japanese hands could hardly be made worse. The comfort was real. So was its purpose.

And the questions had an end. Once a prisoner had nothing more to give, he was put in a group and shipped out to an ordinary camp: McCoy in Wisconsin, Clarinda in Iowa, or back to Angel Island.

## 10. A new page

For some, the change went far deeper than talking. They had been taught that their lives as Japanese ended at capture. When their requests to be allowed to kill themselves were refused, many started over as model prisoners. In 1946, the anthropologist Ruth Benedict described old army hands who located ammunition dumps, explained the positions of Japanese forces, wrote American propaganda, and flew with American bombing pilots to guide them to military targets.

One group of prisoners went further still. They announced that they had been badly misled by the Emperor and the military clique, and asked to fight their way back to Japan alongside Allied soldiers. Benedict's explanation was simple. Having put everything into one way of living and failed at it, they took up another.

In September 1945, the army ordered the camp closed and took its equipment away. One of the two centers was not revealed to the public until the summer of 1947. That same year, the Greek Orthodox Church bought Byron Hot Springs for one hundred and five thousand dollars and turned it into a monastery.

## 11. Echo

The brick hotel still stands in the hills, abandoned. Nothing about it says what happened inside. The prisoners who arrived there had prepared themselves for pain. What the Americans used instead began with a plate of sukiyaki, and someone willing to listen.
