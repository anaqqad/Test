# Sources: "Japanese POWs Were Sure They'd Be Tortured. Then They Were Served Sukiyaki."

No source, no line. Every fact in `script.md` maps to one of the entries below. Where two sources disagree, the
note says which one the script follows and why.

## Main sources
1. **Arnold Krammer, "Japanese Prisoners of War in America," *Pacific Historical Review* 52, no. 1 (Feb. 1983): 67–91.**
   Internet Archive copy: https://archive.org/details/JapanesePrisonersOfWarInAmerica (full text: `_djvu.txt`). Used for:
   - Japanese Military Field Code (adopted 8 Jan. 1941): "rather than live and bear the shame of imprisonment by the enemy, he
     should die and avoid leaving a dishonorable name!"; save the last round (p. 69).
   - Official history (Miller, *Guadalcanal: The First Offensive*, 1949, p. 310): "American troops, who were fearful of the widely
     publicized treacherousness of the enemy, were reluctant to take prisoners" (p. 69).
   - Confidential memo: "it took the promise of three days leave and some ice cream [to tempt U.S. soldiers] to bring in the first
     live prisoner" (U.S. Fourth Army G-2, "Interrogation of Japanese Prisoners in the Southwest Pacific," Intelligence Memo no. 4,
     22 July 1943, RG 165) (p. 69 n. 6).
   - Guadalcanal, 1 Jan.–15 Feb. 1943: XIV Corps took 84 prisoners, 33 too sick or wounded to walk (p. 69).
   - 604 Japanese taken prisoner by the Allies from the opening of the Pacific war through Java Sea, Coral Sea, Midway, Guadalcanal,
     Savo Island, Bismarck Sea, New Guinea, Kula Gulf, Bougainville, Tarawa and Makin (p. 70).
   - Sept. 1942 agreement with Australia: all captured Japanese handed to Australia except those whose potential intelligence value
     required shipment to the U.S. (or who were captured closer to the U.S.) (p. 70).
   - Angel Island: "small mountainous island in San Francisco Bay", a quarantine station of the Immigration Service before the war;
     transit center before Tracy. Deloused, belongings disinfected, forms, serial numbers, medical exam (malaria, syphilis, skin
     disease, intestinal worms, minor combat wounds) (pp. 70–71).
   - Mandatory postal card to families and Red Cross listing: "nearly all the Japanese captives resolutely requested that their
     families in Japan not be advised of their imprisonment. Better they be considered dead than dishonored by captivity." (p. 71)
   - Menu, Camp Angel Island, **16 Sept. 1944** (Report of Inspection by the ICRC and the Special War Problems Division, Dept. of
     State, RG 389): Breakfast: sausages, rice, browned crusts, apples, coffee, milk, sugar. Lunch: sukiyaki, cabbage salad, rice,
     caramel pudding, water. Dinner: spaghetti and hash, baked tomatoes, lettuce and tomato salad, rice, cakes, cocoa.
     "better fed in captivity than in their own army" (p. 71).
   - National menus from 1 July 1944 so prisoners "would eat more and throw away less" (p. 71 n. 13).
   - Gramophone, cards, Mah-Jong, "whispering among themselves as they strove to understand the ulterior motives of their captors" (p. 72).
   - "The American authorities did, indeed, have ulterior motives"; the main reason for shipment to the U.S. was interrogation;
     the prisoners "feared and despised their captors … no less, in fact, than they were themselves hated in return" (p. 72).
   - **Three phases** (pp. 72–73): up to 48 h after capture, certain they would be tortured and killed, unresponsive or unreliable;
     after several days, fear changing to gratitude, anxious to reciprocate by talking freely, most receptive for the next ten days to
     two weeks; third phase, accustomed to the food and kind treatment, annoyed at questioning, "a shell of indifference".
   - No threat of physical violence or solitary confinement worked as well as "the simple threat of forwarding his name to his
     relatives in Japan" (p. 73).
   - Requests to kill themselves denied → many became model prisoners. Quote via Ruth Benedict (below). One group: "they had been
     badly misled by the Emperor and the Japanese military clique … and wished to fight back to Japan side by side with Allied
     soldiers!" (*New York Times*, 8 July 1945, p. 2) (p. 73).
   - Unlike Germans (questioned in isolation), new Japanese prisoners talked more after older prisoners consoled them; old prisoners
     advised new ones to disclose everything "lest they all be blamed"; a doubtful prisoner "would come of his own volition the
     following day" to say whether his version was right or wrong (p. 73).
   - Two interrogation installations: Fort Hunt (17 miles from Washington) and Byron Hot Springs near Tracy. Plans labelled
     "Officers' School"; "these men must have wondered why officers needed eight-foot fences, hidden microphones in the long rows of
     cells, barred windows, and heavy gates" (p. 74).
   - The centers "contravened or, at the very least, 'bent' a dozen or so articles of the Geneva Convention of 1929"; the War
     Department judged the information worth it and that the treatment of American prisoners in Japanese hands "could not have been
     made much worse" (p. 74). Japan had not ratified the 1929 POW Convention (p. 68 n. 4).
   - Secrecy: "its existence was not revealed to the public until mid-1947" (*NYT*, 11 July 1947, p. 34) (p. 74 n. 18). The footnote
     sits on Moore's article about Fort Hunt, so the script says only "one of the two centers".
   - Informal sessions by intelligence officers, "many of whom were Japanese-American specialists from the army's Military
     Intelligence Service Language School (MISLS) in Minnesota"; "each of the forty-five interrogators was assigned to one prisoner"
     until nothing more could be learned; 45–60 minutes, two or three a day, for a week or more (p. 74).
   - Every question planned; checklist: military equipment, fuel, rations, morale at the front and at home, rumors, personalities of
     commanders, postwar expectations. Reports of 2–3 pages "nearly every four days", from "the deteriorating quality of Japanese
     clothing to the number of boiler rooms in the enemy aircraft carrier Hiryu" (p. 75).
   - **1944 at Tracy**: Jan 13, Feb 5, Mar 36, Apr 132, May 105, Jun 87, Jul 146, Aug 174, Sep 74, Oct 101, Nov 75, Dec 129;
     **total 1,077** ("Report of Activities, MIS Section, P.O. Box 651, Tracy, California, for 1944") (p. 75).
   - Afterwards shipped in groups to POW camps; most spent the war at McCoy (WI), Clarinda (IA) or Angel Island (pp. 75–76).
   - **P.O. box:** Krammer's text says "P.O. Box 541" for Tracy, but his own footnote 23 and the 1946 War Department record (entry 4)
     both say **P.O. Box 651**. The script uses 651.
   - Krammer calls Byron Hot Springs "west of San Francisco"; it is east of the bay (Contra Costa County). The script says east.
2. **Ruth Benedict, *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword*** (1946; London ed. 1967, pp. 28–29), as quoted in Krammer p. 73:
   "Old [Japanese] Army hands and long-time extreme nationals located ammunition dumps, carefully explained the disposition of
   Japanese forces, wrote our propaganda and flew with our bombing pilots to guide them to military targets. It was as if they had
   turned over a new page…" Benedict worked on Japan for the U.S. Office of War Information. Note: this describes Japanese prisoners
   in general, not Tracy prisoners specifically; the script does not tie it to Tracy.
3. **California State Military Museum, "Historic California Posts: Camp Tracy (Byron Hot Springs)"**,
   https://militarymuseum.org/ByronHotSprings.html (citing the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers real-estate history, R. Osborne's
   *World War II Sites in the United States*, Rick Lemyre "In the Shadows of Camp Tracy" (2008), and the "Report on the Activities
   of the CPM Branch, MIS, G-2"). Used for:
   - War Department memo of 12 May 1942 directing a West Coast interrogation center; Byron Hot Springs selected.
   - Inner fenced interrogation area with Japanese and German sections; hidden microphones in light fixtures, wires through hollow
     walls and down an elevator shaft to a ground-level transcription room; "all buildings used by the prisoners were electronically
     bugged"; vents that let a prisoner standing on a toilet see into the next room (researchers believe to encourage talk); devices
     in the trees outside.
   - Maximum held at one time did not exceed 51.
   - Ordered closed 1 Sept. 1945; equipment removed when the Army released the property.
   - Conflict: it gives ~921 Japanese and 645 Germans interrogated in 1944. The script follows Krammer's 1,077, which he takes from the
     camp's own 1944 activity report, and the bar card shows Krammer's monthly figures.
4. **War Department, "Origin of the Interrogation Centers for the Interrogation of Prisoners of War"** (1946), RG 165, NARA;
   transcribed at https://www.uboatarchive.net/POW/POWInterrogationCenters.htm. "Interrogation Center, P.O. Box 651, Tracy,
   California"; May 1942 memo naming Byron Hot Springs and Fort Hunt; commanding officer Col. Daniel W. Kent, 5 Nov. 1942 – 7 Feb. 1945.
5. **Wikipedia, "Byron Hot Springs"**, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byron_Hot_Springs (retrieved 27 Oct. 2026). Used for:
   resort opened 1889 by Lewis Mead; fires in 1901 and 1912; four-story brick hotel built 1913, reopened 1914; 1920s wealthy patrons
   and celebrities, mud baths and mineral soaks; closed 1938 after a series of lawsuits; Camp Tracy held German and Japanese POWs;
   1945 orders to dismantle; "over 3,500 Japanese prisoners of war" interrogated (cited to a local history); bought in 1947 by the
   Greek Orthodox Church for $105,000 (Monastery Saint Paul); abandoned hotel still standing after the 2005 carriage-house fire.
   Wikipedia dates the government lease to 1941; the script avoids the lease year and uses the 1942 selection (entries 3, 4).

## Lines deliberately left out
- Gen. Styer's "work like piss ants" order: off-topic for this episode (labor, not interrogation).
- Any named prisoner at Tracy: none found in a citable source, so the cold open uses the documented group lunch of 16 Sept. 1944
  instead of an invented individual.
- "Families in internment camps" for the Nisei interrogators: true for many MIS linguists, but not in these sources for Tracy.
- The 16,100 American captives figure (Krammer n. 4): it refers to "enemy hands" generally, so it is not used.
