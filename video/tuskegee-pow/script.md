# A Tuskegee Pilot Was Captured. The Germans Had His High School Grades.

## 1. The book

In the late summer of 1944, a German interrogator sat down with an American fighter pilot who had just been shot down over southern France. The pilot was Second Lieutenant Alexander Jefferson of Detroit. The German did not wait for him to speak. He named the unit himself. The three hundred and thirty-second Fighter Group. Negroes. Red Tails.

Then he opened a book. It had photographs of every class of Black pilots that had graduated before Jefferson's. It had his marks from college in Atlanta. It had his marks from Chadsey High School in Detroit. It had how much tax his father paid on the family house.

Jefferson said they knew more about him than he knew about himself. He would spend nine months in German prison camps. And when he came home, he walked down a gangplank in New York toward a sign.

This is the documented story of Alexander Jefferson, one of thirty-two Tuskegee Airmen captured in the war, told from the interviews he gave and the record around them.

## 2. The airfield

He was born in Detroit in November 1921. He called himself a World War One brat. He built model warplanes and read all the flying magazines. In third and fourth grade he cut school and walked to a small grass airfield, where he cleaned and washed the planes. That was where he got his first ride, in the back seat of a Waco.

The Army Air Corps refused to train Black pilots. In 2006, Congress wrote down why, in a law. Studies commissioned by the Army War College between 1924 and 1939 concluded that Blacks were unfit for leadership roles and incapable of aviation.

That changed in 1941. According to the same law, President Roosevelt overruled his top generals and ordered an all-Black flight training program, one day after the NAACP sued on behalf of a Howard University student. Because the country was segregated, the pilots would train at a separate airfield, near Tuskegee, Alabama.

Jefferson finished college in Atlanta in June 1942, with a degree in chemistry and biology. He went to the armory in Detroit to take the test. The minimum weight was one hundred and sixteen pounds. He weighed just under. He was told to go downstairs, drink some water and eat some bananas. He came back at one hundred and sixteen pounds and two ounces.

In April 1943 he was called to Tuskegee. He would remember following the leader over big white clouds against a blue sky, then skimming the treetops through farmers' backyards. He called it the greatest experience of his life. He got his wings in January 1944.

## 3. The Red Tails

That June he reached Ramitelli, on the Italian coast, as a replacement pilot in the three hundred and first Fighter Squadron. His group parked at the end of the runway and watched a fighter take off, stall and go straight into the ground. That was his welcome. He flew his first Mustang combat mission after about three hours of practice in the plane.

The job was escort. The tails of their fighters were painted red, and they stayed with the bombers. For decades it was said they never lost a single one. Air Force historian Daniel Haulman later counted twenty-seven. The average for the other Mustang groups in the same air force was forty-six.

Jefferson remembered the flak as a round black cloud over the target, like a hockey puck, and the sound of it on his plane, like pebbles thrown on a tin roof. One day he watched a bomber come out of that cloud half on fire. A few parachutes came out. Then it exploded. He got sick inside his oxygen mask, at twenty-four thousand feet.

## 4. Number four

On the twelfth of August, 1944, the invasion of southern France was three days away, and the radar stations near Toulon had to go. Jefferson had flown eighteen escort missions. His nineteenth was his first attack on the ground. He was number four in his flight.

They went in at about four hundred miles an hour, down on the deck. The first planes got through. By then the side of the cliff was lit up with anti-aircraft fire. He put his hits on the target and crossed it at treetop height. Then there was a bang, and a hole in the top of his canopy. The shell had come up through the floor in front of the stick. Fire came up after it.

In nine months of training, he said, nobody had spent a minute teaching him how to get out of an airplane. He got out. He saw the tail go by and pulled the ring at once. When the parachute opened he was already in the trees. The men who had shot him down were two or three hundred yards away.

His wingmen did not see him bail out. In Detroit, his parents were told he had been killed in action. It took about a month for a Red Cross letter to tell them he was a prisoner. By then he had met the man with the book. For what was in it, we have only Jefferson's word, given in a radio interview sixty years later.

## 5. Stalag Luft Three

A train took him to Stalag Luft Three, near Sagan, about a hundred miles southeast of Berlin. It is the camp of the Great Escape. He arrived in September 1944. It held about five thousand American officers. By his own count, he was the fourth Black one.

The Army that sent him had trained him apart, at a separate airfield, in an all-Black group. Here there was no such line. He got the same Red Cross clothing as everyone else, and the same short rations. In his words, he had all the rights and privileges of an American officer. No segregation, no discrimination.

The men followed the war on a hidden radio tuned to the BBC. And new prisoners kept coming through the gate. One day a bomber crew arrived, and one of them looked at Jefferson and told him that if the Red Tails had been with them, they would not have been shot down.

## 6. The march

Just before midnight on the twenty-seventh of January, 1945, with Soviet troops about sixteen miles away, the Germans emptied the camp. About eleven thousand men walked out into six inches of snow.

Jefferson remembered eighty kilometers on foot, and twenty below zero. At the end of the road they were loaded into cattle cars and taken south to Bavaria, to Stalag Seven-A, at Moosburg.

That camp had opened in 1939 for up to ten thousand Polish prisoners. By the end, more than seventy-six thousand men were in it. Jefferson spent his last months of the war there.

On the twenty-ninth of April, the American fourteenth Armored Division, part of Patton's Third Army, came up to the camp. After a short fight at the bridges, the two hundred and forty guards surrendered. Jefferson had been a prisoner for nearly nine months.

## 7. The road to Dachau

The next day, somebody told him there was a place down the road with a lot of dead people. He and some others found a jeep and drove to see it. It was Dachau. He said the ovens were still warm. He said he would never forget the smell. He remembered a long table covered with gold teeth. Sixty years later, he had one phrase for it. Man's inhumanity to man.

## 8. The gangplank

A transport plane took him to Le Havre. A ship took him to England, and from there he crossed the Atlantic on the Queen Mary, into New York, in the middle of 1945.

He walked down the gangplank in an officer's uniform. In front of him was a big sign. Whites to the right, colored to the left. A white soldier at the bottom pointed the way, and he used a racial slur to do it. Jefferson had a phrase for that too. Back to segregation.

When NPR told his story in 2006, it put the matter simply. He had been given more respect as a prisoner of the Nazis than as a Black man in America.

## 9. Home

He went back to Tuskegee as an instrument instructor until the field closed in 1946. Two years later, on the twenty-sixth of July, 1948, President Truman ordered the armed forces to end segregation.

Jefferson went home to Detroit and taught elementary school science for more than thirty years. He stayed in the reserves and retired as a lieutenant colonel. His Purple Heart, for the day he was shot down, came in 2004, sixty years later. In 2005 he published his memoir, with the sketches he had made as a prisoner.

In 2006, Congress voted the Tuskegee Airmen its gold medal, and the law counted the cost. Sixty-six of these pilots were killed in combat, while another thirty-two were either forced down or shot down and captured to become prisoners of war.

Near the end of that NPR interview, Jefferson gave his own verdict on the country at the bottom of the gangplank. He called it the best country in the world. The only obligation, he said, was to make it better. It wasn't perfect, he said, but it was home.

## 10. The field

Alexander Jefferson died in Detroit on the twenty-second of June, 2022. He was one hundred years old.

Two years later, the city put up a bronze statue of him in Rouge Park, on a field that carries his name. The local club still flies model airplanes there. He flew them there himself, late in life. So the boy who built model warplanes stands at the edge of an airfield again, and the small planes go over his head.
