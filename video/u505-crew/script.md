# The Navy Captured a U-Boat. Then It Hid 58 Germans From the Red Cross.

## 1. Dead men

In the summer of 1944, families across Germany got the news that every sailor's family feared. The submarine U five-oh-five was long overdue. Her crew, the German Navy told them, had to be considered dead.

They were not dead. Fifty-eight of them were alive, behind American barbed wire in a corner of a prison camp in Louisiana. No letter of theirs left the camp. The Red Cross was not told their names. Their own country had no idea where they were.

The rules of war said their families should have known. The United States Navy decided they could not. One American captain had already written down why the secret mattered, in a memo to his men, under the words top secret.

This is the documented story of the crew of U five-oh-five, told from the Navy's own reports, from their captain's statement, and from what came out after the war.

## 2. The unlucky boat

U five-oh-five was a long-range submarine, commissioned in August 1941. She did not stay lucky. In November 1942, a bomb from an Allied patrol plane nearly sank her in the Caribbean. She limped home to Lorient, in France.

After months of repairs she put to sea again, and turned back within days. Then she did it again. Six times in a row she came home early, with one defect after another. Sabotage by French dockyard workers was suspected. Other U-boat crews made a joke of her.

In October 1943, near the Azores, two Allied destroyers caught her with depth charges. Her captain, Peter Zschech, shot himself in the control room, in front of his crew. His second in command brought the boat home.

In November 1943 she got a new captain, Harald Lange, a reserve officer. By late spring 1944 she was off the coast of West Africa, at the end of a long patrol.

## 3. The hunters

The Americans hunting her had an unusual plan. Captain Daniel Gallery commanded Task Group twenty-two point three: the escort carrier Guadalcanal and five destroyer escorts. He did not want to sink a U-boat. He wanted to take one.

Before the cruise, Gallery ordered every ship to plan for a capture and to organize boarding parties. The task group's plan of the day for the fourth of June was printed the evening before. It already listed the men who would crew a captured German submarine.

## 4. The surrender

At ten past eleven in the morning on the fourth of June, 1944, two days before D-Day, the destroyer escort Chatelain reported a possible sound contact, about a hundred and fifty miles off the coast of West Africa. Six minutes later she fired. Two fighter pilots overhead could see the submarine below the surface.

Inside the boat, Lange had seen three destroyers through his periscope and dived. Then came two heavy shocks. Water came in. The lights went out and the rudders jammed. Not knowing how bad it was, he ordered the boat to the surface.

She came up eight hundred yards from Chatelain, and the escorts and fighters opened fire. Lange was hit in both legs, and splinters cut his face and his right eye. A blast threw him down onto the main deck. He ordered his men to leave the boat and to sink her.

They opened valves and jumped. But the engines were still running, and the empty submarine kept circling. From a life raft, Lange had the men around him give three cheers for their sinking boat. At eleven twenty-seven, the Navy recorded the official time of surrender.

## 5. The boarding

A boat from the destroyer escort Pillsbury came alongside. Lieutenant Albert David led eight men onto the circling submarine. One German sailor lay dead on the deck. He was the only man killed.

David went down the hatch first, not knowing if the boat was rigged to explode. His men closed a valve that was letting the sea in, and started passing up papers: codebooks, charts, and an Enigma cipher machine. A second boarding party found and disarmed thirteen demolition charges. The fourteenth was only found weeks later.

The carrier's chief engineer, Earl Trosino, kept her afloat. He let the turning propellers drive the electric motors as generators, to recharge the batteries, so her own pumps could empty her tanks. The Guadalcanal took her in tow, and on the nineteenth of June she was towed into Bermuda.

## 6. Keep your mouths shut

Now the Navy had a problem. The codebooks were only useful if Germany believed the boat had gone down with all hands. About three thousand American sailors had seen it.

On the fourteenth of June, Gallery wrote to every man in the task group. The capture of the U-505 can be one of the major turning points in World War number two provided repeat provided we keep our mouths shut about it.

The enemy must not learn of this capture, he wrote. Their friends would read about it one day in the history books. In Bermuda the crews were told to stay silent, and every souvenir was turned in. According to the historian Clay Blair, Admiral Ernest King was so angry at the risk that he considered court-martialling Gallery.

## 7. Camp Ruston

The bigger risk was the prisoners. Fifty-eight Germans could tell their navy that the boat had not sunk. One letter home, or one Red Cross visit, could end the secret.

In the summer of 1944 they were taken to Camp Ruston, in northern Louisiana. It was a big camp, about seven miles west of the town, built in 1942 on seven hundred and seventy acres. At its peak it held more than four thousand prisoners, the first of them from Rommel's Afrika Korps.

The U five-oh-five men did not join them. They were kept in an isolated part of the camp, away from every other prisoner. Their names were withheld from the Red Cross. The letters they tried to send were confiscated.

The rules of war said prisoners could write home, and that the Red Cross would know where they were. The National World War Two Museum put the Navy's view plainly. It could take no chances.

Around them, other prisoners picked cotton, cut timber and built roads, for pay in camp money. They had an orchestra, a theater and a library. How much of that life the isolated sailors shared, the published record does not say.

## 8. Dead on paper

In Germany, the boat was long overdue. By August 1944, the German Navy had told the families of her crew that their men must be considered dead. For the rest of the war, their families believed them lost at sea.

On the eighth of May, 1945, Germany surrendered. Eight days later, the Navy finally told the world. It called the capture the first boarding of a foreign enemy warship on the high seas since 1815. Fifty-eight of the fifty-nine Germans aboard had survived, and were prisoners in the United States.

Albert David received the Medal of Honor, the only one given to a Navy man in the Battle of the Atlantic. He died of a heart attack in September 1945, before he could receive it. President Truman gave it to his widow.

Only after the war did the families learn the truth. The last prisoners left Camp Ruston in February 1946. The crew of U five-oh-five did not get home to Germany until 1947.

## 9. Chicago

The Navy kept the submarine. Gallery's brother, a priest in Chicago, contacted the city's Museum of Science and Industry. In 1954 the Navy gave the boat to the museum, and people in Chicago raised two hundred and fifty thousand dollars to bring her in through the Great Lakes.

In 1964, twenty years after the capture, Gallery and Lange met at a reunion at the museum. Gallery gave the German captain back his binoculars.

For years, the museum told its visitors the crew had been held in Bermuda. In 1998, a researcher brought it declassified National Archives records showing they had been in Louisiana, and the exhibit was corrected.

## 10. Underground

In 2004, the museum moved U five-oh-five into a new hall below ground, and she reopened on the fifth of June, 2005, sixty-one years and a day after her capture. In Louisiana, two buildings of Camp Ruston are still standing, on the National Register of Historic Places. They are what is left of the camp where fifty-eight men listed as dead waited out the war.
