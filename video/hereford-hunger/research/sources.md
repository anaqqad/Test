# Sources: "Hungry Italian POWs Ate Rattlesnakes in Texas. Then a Church Fed Them."

No source, no line. Every fact in `script.md` maps to one of the entries below.

## Why this topic (competitor research, 2 Nov 2026)
YouTube search sorted by upload date (2 Nov 2026): the "POWs and American food" format is everywhere and still pulling
views ("German POWs Were Told America Was Starving. Then They Saw the Food", 39K in 2 weeks; "Nazi POWs in Tennessee Were
Shocked by What Americans Called a 'Small' Meal", 681K in 5 months; "German POW Generals Were Shocked When They First Saw
America", 294K and 263K in 2 weeks). Almost all of them are dramatized ("female POWs", invented scenes). Our documented
version flips the format: the plenty was real, and then in 1945 it was taken away. Camp Hereford is the best-documented case
(a book, a bishop's letter, an Army investigation), with one concrete scene (the bishop's lunch) and one object still
standing (the paintings at St. Mary's, Umbarger). Not covered by any earlier episode of ours.

## Main sources
1. **Richard Phelan, review of Donald Mace Williams, *Interlude in Umbarger: Italian POWs and a Texas Church* (Texas Tech
   University Press, 1992), *The Texas Observer*, 24 Dec. 1999** (texasobserver.org/177-richard-phelan-on-donald-mace-williams-interlude-in-umbarger-reviewed/):
   - Murals of the Annunciation and the Visitation, a large oil of the Assumption of Mary, a wood carving of the Last Supper;
     the work is still there.
   - The commander "reduced the prisoners' rations to the point where, over a long haul, they would have died"; the men
     killed and ate the pets in their compounds (dogs, cats) and birds, lizards and rattlesnakes; the painters worked
     "chiefly because they were hungry", for the dinners the church women cooked and served in the basement.
   - More than 3,000 prisoners at one time, four compounds (three enlisted, one officers); all Italians. Lt. Franco Di Bello,
     23, career officer, captured in North Africa in 1943, athlete; refused to collaborate; the refusers called themselves "Nons".
   - Cutback began after V-E Day, when Americans learned of the treatment and starvation of Allied prisoners in German
     camps. On 4 May 1945 the Eighth Service Command in Dallas ordered 2,300-2,500 calories a day for non-working prisoners
     and 3,500 for workers. Williams: "If the (Army's) order … had been meticulously carried out, the cats and dogs would
     have survived."
   - Col. Joseph R. Carvolth went beyond the order: stopped canteen sales of snacks and drinks, returned food packages,
     complaint letters read and thrown away. Some men fried grasshoppers in brilliantine bought at the PX on their barracks
     stoves. Carvolth had commanded the German POW camp at Mexia.
   - By late October the prisoners were hollow-eyed, listless and thin; many had given up sports, writing, painting.
   - Father John Krukkert, 55, saw an exhibit of the prisoners' art and negotiated for seven prisoners to decorate St.
     Mary's; he supplied materials, the parish women cooked dinner.
   - 7 Sept. 1945: Bishop Laurence J. FitzSimon of Amarillo, alerted by a chaplain, ate lunch in the prisoners' mess.
     Letter to Congressman Gene (Eugene) Worley: "a bowl of weak, watery soup, containing pieces of gummy-like spaghetti",
     made with little or no salt; "a dry, over-salted herring"; men held back soup to dip the herring in it; "Then we had
     bread and water and that was all." Kitchen: "I saw some tubs of pig's feet, sacks of moldy-looking potatoes and a
     basket of overripe tomatoes."
   - Sgt. John Coyle, from Pennsylvania, left school in eighth grade, worked at a knitting machine in a sock mill; drove
     the painters each morning past Hereford to Umbarger, ate lunch with them. Shot jackrabbits from the front fender of a
     farmer's Model A with a borrowed shotgun; skinned and gutted; the painters hung them inside their pants legs on
     strings tied to their belts and smuggled them past the guards; he risked his stripes and the guardhouse.
   - Food the church women offered (Williams): roasts, hams, sausages, salads, homemade breads, sauerkraut, mashed
     potatoes, jellies and jams, pies and cobblers from home-grown and home-canned cherries. Krukkert feared the amounts
     would damage their organs; the excesses lessened as the work went on. The parish: German-American farmers; the
     priest Dutch-born.
   - Investigation (Italian Embassy, Red Cross, State Department): average weight loss over seven months 20 pounds per man.
     Brig. Gen. B. M. Bryan, assistant provost marshal general, on the phone to Carvolth: "We're getting a little hot about
     your camp down there, but I don't want you to get worried because I am going to bat for you whole hog." Carvolth said
     he did not know anyone was hungry.
   - Decorating took 41 working days; dedicated in December with the artists and Coyle in the front row. In the murals,
     High Plains wheat fields and the farmhouses of church members. Di Bello later returned to the army and became a
     general; Williams wrote to him in 1981 and only then learned about "la fame", the hunger.
2. **Wikipedia, "Camp Hereford"** (citing Walker 1980, Keefer 1992, Rogers 1989, Henegar 1986, Wegman 2020, Williams 1992):
   Geneva Article 11 food "equal in quality and quantity" to American troops (Walker p. 116); many gained weight (Keefer
   p. 142); *Amarillo Daily News*, 14 July 1943: apple butter, tomato sauce, chicken cacciatore; spring 1945 cut amid
   press campaigns accusing the government of "coddling" (Walker pp. 121-123); camp physician Maj. Luigi Cabitto: the
   2,500 limit "rarely achieved", menus often about 2,000 and sometimes as low as 1,500 (Wegman p. 40; Keefer p. 144);
   "la fame" from spring to late fall (Williams p. 42); average loss 20 lb, some as much as 40 (Keefer p. 144); broths of
   fish heads, grass and bellflowers; enlisted men in the potato fields could boil potatoes; enlisted men threw food over
   the fence to officers; complaints "largely substantiated"; by November the canteen reopened and rations were "slightly
   increased" (Walker p. 178). Over 85% of the 51,000 Italian POWs in the U.S. joined the Italian Service Units; those who
   did not were often sent to Hereford (Rogers p. 91).
3. **Lucielle Henegar, "Hereford Military Reservation and Reception Center", *Handbook of Texas Online* (TSHA), 1995**:
   3½ miles southeast of Hereford; War Department authorization 30 June 1942; first internees April 1943, nearly all
   Italian; enlisted men worked on farms for ten cents an hour; from April through December 1945 the officers' compound was
   under a "retaliatory starvation order"; chapel built Oct.-Dec. 1945 for five comrades who died; in January 1946 the last
   3,999 prisoners left on special troop trains for Italy; surplus 1 Feb. 1946; water tower and chapel remain; restoration
   ceremony 30 Apr. 1988 with 16 former POWs; dedicated 1989.
4. **Robyn Ross, "The Bittersweet Legacy of Hereford's POW Camp", *Texas Highways*, Nov. 2023**: Hereford about 2,300
   people, 45 miles SW of Amarillo; isolated flat open land so escapees could be seen; first prisoners April 1943,
   captured in North Africa, shipped through the Panama Canal to the West Coast, by train to Texas, on foot to Hereford;
   at its fullest 3,860 prisoners; those who refused to collaborate were sent to Texas; planted potatoes, harvested
   carrots; 80 cents a day in scrip; canteen: cigarettes, candy bars, beer; soccer, bocce, college courses, musicals;
   August 1945 art exhibition in an empty barracks; Rev. John Krukkert of St. Mary's, Umbarger; nine artists led by Capt.
   Franco Di Bello, in exchange for home-cooked meals; chapel inscription "From the Italian prisoners to the companions
   who will not return"; the five dead reburied in an Oklahoma POW cemetery; water tower and chapel still stand.
5. **Wikipedia, "German prisoners of war in the United States"**: prisoners ate the same rations as American soldiers
   (Geneva); some burned leftover food fearing their rations would be cut; one German gained 57 lb in two years ("I had
   gotten so fat you could no longer see my eyes", Carlson, *We Were Each Other's Prisoners*, 1997, p. 208); May 1945: the
   Provost Marshal General limited food and ended canteen food sales (Carlson). Figures 128 and 185 lb: Jim O'Neal,
   Intelligent Collector (Heritage Auctions), 15 Jan. 2016, quoting the same prisoner.
6. **Kathryn E. Russell, "The Homefront Food Fight: The Conflict Between the American Public and Enemy Prisoners of War
   During World War II", M.A. thesis, University of Memphis, 2010** (abstract): the War Department cut POW rations under
   public and congressional pressure and blamed food shortages; the timeline of press reports and investigations suggests
   the cuts were not necessary. Used only for "one later study argues".
7. **Geneva Convention of 1929, Article 11**: "The food ration of prisoners of war shall be equivalent in quantity and
   quality to that of the depot troops."
8. **Wikimedia Commons**: Fort Reno POW cemetery headstone of Pvt. Innocent Ortelli, born 26 Mar. 1920, died 2 Aug. 1945
   at the Hereford POW camp (tuberculosis and bronchial pneumonia).

## Disagreements
- **Camp size:** "more than 3,000" (Phelan), 3,860 at its fullest (Texas Highways), about 5,000 (Handbook), capacity nearly
  6,000 (Wikipedia). Script: "At times there were more than three thousand of them."
- **The church team:** seven prisoners (Williams/Phelan) vs nine artists led by Capt. Di Bello (Texas Highways; Di Bello was
  a lieutenant in 1945 per Williams). Script: "Accounts differ … seven men in one book, nine in a later magazine story."
- **Calorie limit:** 2,300-2,500 / 3,500 (Eighth Service Command order, via Williams) vs 2,500 / 3,400 (Keefer, Walker).
  Script: "about two thousand five hundred … about three thousand five hundred".
- **Why the cut:** War Department: food shortages. Russell (2010): public pressure. Script gives both.
- **Start of the hunger:** April (Handbook, officers' compound) vs May (the order; Phelan). Script: the order on May 4; the
  officers' compound "from April to December 1945" as the Handbook says.
