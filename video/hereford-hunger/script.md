# Hungry Italian POWs Ate Rattlesnakes in Texas. Then a Church Fed Them.

## 1. The soup

On September seventh, 1945, a Catholic bishop sat down to lunch in a prisoner of war camp in the Texas Panhandle. He had asked to eat exactly what the prisoners ate. They served him a bowl of thin, watery soup with pieces of gummy spaghetti in it, made with almost no salt. Then a dry, over-salted herring. Then bread and water. That was all.

He watched the prisoners around him save their soup, and dip the salty fish into it, to give the soup some taste. Later he walked into the kitchen to see what was coming for dinner. He found tubs of pig's feet, sacks of moldy-looking potatoes, and a basket of overripe tomatoes.

The men at those tables were Italian soldiers. For months they had been killing and eating the dogs and cats in their compounds, and whatever birds, lizards and rattlesnakes they could catch. This is the documented story of the hunger at Camp Hereford, and of the small Texas church that fed some of them, in exchange for paintings that are still on its walls.

## 2. The camp

Hereford was a farm town of about two thousand three hundred people, forty-five miles southwest of Amarillo. In 1942 the Army built one of the largest prisoner of war camps in the country just outside it, on flat open land where an escaping man could be seen for miles.

The first prisoners arrived in April 1943. They had been captured in North Africa, shipped through the Panama Canal to the West Coast, carried by train to Texas, and marched on foot to the gate. Almost all of them were Italian. At times there were more than three thousand of them behind the wire.

In September 1943, Italy surrendered and changed sides. Its new government asked the prisoners in America to work with the Allies. More than eighty-five percent of the fifty-one thousand Italian prisoners in the country agreed. Those who refused called themselves the Nons. Many of them were sent to Hereford.

## 3. The good months

At first, the Nons ate well. Under Article eleven of the Geneva Convention, prisoners had to be fed the same food, in the same quantity, as American troops. A local newspaper in 1943 listed what was on their plates: apple butter, tomato sauce, chicken cacciatore. Many of the men gained weight.

They earned camp scrip working on the farms around Hereford, planting potatoes and harvesting carrots, and spent it in the canteen on cigarettes, candy bars and beer. They played soccer and bocce, staged musicals, and took college courses.

It was the same across America. In other camps, German prisoners burned their leftovers, afraid that if the Americans saw them, the rations would be cut. One German later said he went in at one hundred twenty-eight pounds and came out, two years later, at one hundred eighty-five.

## 4. May 1945

Then the war in Europe ended. American troops opened the German camps, and the country saw what had been done to Allied prisoners there: men starved down to bone. Newspapers were accusing the government of coddling the enemy. Now many Americans wanted the prisoners here to feel it.

On May fourth, 1945, the Army command in Dallas ordered a limit on prisoner food: about two thousand five hundred calories a day for men who did not work, and about three thousand five hundred for men who did. Across the country, canteens stopped selling food. The War Department said there was a food shortage. One later study argues that public pressure mattered at least as much.

Two thousand five hundred calories was a real cut. It was not starvation. But each camp commander decided how to carry out the order.

## 5. The commander

Hereford's commander was Colonel Joseph Carvolth. Before Hereford, he had run the big camp for German prisoners at Mexia. At Hereford, he went far beyond his orders.

He closed the canteen to snacks and drinks. He sent back the food parcels that arrived for the prisoners. Letters of complaint were read inside the camp and thrown away. The camp physician, Major Luigi Cabitto, reported that the limit of two thousand five hundred calories was rarely reached. Many days the menus came to about two thousand, and sometimes only fifteen hundred.

The officers' compound was hit hardest. A history of the camp later described it as living under a retaliatory starvation order, from April to December 1945.

## 6. La fame

The prisoners had a name for it: la fame. The hunger.

They hunted anything that moved inside the wire. Dogs and cats disappeared. Men caught rattlesnakes, birds and lizards. Some bought hair oil at the camp store and used it to fry grasshoppers on their barracks stoves. Others boiled fish heads, grass and wildflowers into broth. Men who worked in the potato fields boiled potatoes out there. Enlisted men threw food over the fence to the officers.

By the fall, the men were hollow-eyed, thin and listless. Many had given up soccer, writing and painting. They no longer had the energy.

## 7. The exhibition

And yet, in August 1945, the prisoners held an art exhibition in an empty barracks: paintings of the Panhandle, portraits, carvings, rings hammered out of coins. One of the visitors was Father John Krukkert, a Dutch-born priest of fifty-five who served St. Mary's Catholic Church in Umbarger, a small farm town halfway to Amarillo.

St. Mary's had bare white walls. Father Krukkert asked the Army to let a few of the prisoners decorate them. He would supply the materials. The women of the parish would cook the painters a meal every day.

One of the men chosen was Lieutenant Franco Di Bello. He was twenty-three, a career officer captured in North Africa in 1943, an athlete who spoke good English. Like everyone at Hereford, he had refused to collaborate. Accounts differ on the size of the team: seven men in one book, nine in a later magazine story.

## 8. The bishop's letter

Meanwhile, the camp chaplain had alerted the Bishop of Amarillo, Laurence FitzSimon. That was why, on September seventh, the bishop sat down to lunch in the prisoners' mess.

He wrote to his congressman, Eugene Worley, and described the meal: the watery soup, the salty herring, the bread and water. He asked for an investigation.

## 9. The truck to Umbarger

In late October, the work at St. Mary's began. Every morning an Army truck carried the painters out of the camp, past the town of Hereford, to Umbarger, twenty-six miles away. The man at the wheel was Sergeant John Coyle, a skinny young man from Pennsylvania who had left school in the eighth grade to work in a sock mill.

Upstairs, the prisoners climbed the scaffolds. Downstairs, in the church basement, the women of Umbarger laid out dinner: roasts, hams, sausages, homemade bread, sauerkraut, mashed potatoes, and pies made with cherries from their own trees. The parish was made up of German-American farm families.

The Italians ate so much that Father Krukkert grew worried. He was afraid they would hurt themselves. Over the weeks, they slowed down.

## 10. The jackrabbits

Sergeant Coyle knew the men back in the camp were still hungry. So he borrowed a shotgun, sat on the front fender of a farmer's Model A, and shot jackrabbits in the pastures, where they were a pest.

He and the farmer skinned and cleaned them. The painters hung the rabbits inside their trouser legs, on strings tied to their belts. In the evening Coyle drove them back to the camp, and they walked the rabbits past the guards at the gate. That night, a few hungry men ate roasted jackrabbit.

If he had been caught, Coyle could have lost his stripes and gone to the guardhouse. The smuggling went on anyway.

## 11. The investigation

The bishop's letter worked. Investigators came from the Italian embassy, the Red Cross and the State Department. They found that over about seven months, the prisoners at Hereford had lost an average of twenty pounds each. Some had lost forty.

The Army also looked into itself. In a recorded phone call, an assistant provost marshal general in Washington told Carvolth not to worry, that he was going to bat for him. Carvolth told investigators he had not known his prisoners were hungry. But the complaints were largely confirmed. By November the canteen reopened, and the rations went up, a little.

## 12. The walls

The painting at St. Mary's took forty-one working days. On the walls, the prisoners painted the Annunciation and the Visitation, scenes from the life of Mary, inside Renaissance halls with marble floors. Through the arches, instead of Italian hills, you see High Plains wheat fields and Texas sky. The farmhouses in the distance belong to the families who fed them.

Two wood carvers cut the Last Supper into the front of the altar. A large oil painting of the Assumption of Mary was hung as well. In December 1945, the work was dedicated. The artists sat in the front row, and Sergeant Coyle sat with them.

## 13. Going home

That same autumn, prisoners built a small chapel inside the camp for the five men who had died at Hereford. On the floor, in Italian, they carved: from the Italian prisoners to the companions who will not return.

In January 1946, the last prisoners left Hereford on troop trains, on their way home to Italy. The camp was declared surplus and its barracks were sold off.

Franco Di Bello went back into the army, and rose to the rank of general. In 1981, a Texas newspaperman named Donald Williams wrote to him about the paintings. That was how Williams first learned about la fame. His book about it came out in 1992.

## 14. Echo

If someone in your family was held at Hereford, guarded it, or sat at those tables in Umbarger, tell us in the comments.

The camp is gone now. A water tower still stands in the fields south of Hereford, and the little chapel, restored by the county in the 1980s, with sixteen of the former prisoners there to see it. And in Umbarger, the paintings are still on the walls of St. Mary's: Texas wheat fields behind the Virgin Mary, painted by hungry men, in exchange for dinner.
