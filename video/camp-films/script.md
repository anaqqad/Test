# German POWs Had to Watch One Film. A Thousand Burned Their Uniforms.

## 1. The theater

On the twenty-sixth of June 1945, about a hundred and fifty German prisoners of war were brought into the movie theater of Halloran General Hospital, on Staten Island, New York. The lights went down, and the Army ran a film made only weeks earlier, inside the camps the Allied armies had found in Germany. The New York Times reported what happened in the seats. A few men held handkerchiefs over their eyes. One sat with his head bowed and both hands pressed over his ears for most of the film.

The next day's headline did not speak of shame. It said the Germans were unmoved, and that the prisoners blamed the crimes on the men at the top. Yet that same summer, at a camp in North Carolina, about a thousand prisoners did something no one had ordered. They burned their own German uniforms.

This is the documented story of the films the U.S. Army made German prisoners in America watch in 1945: a father and son who sat in the same camp, a thousand burned uniforms, and the survey that asked the prisoners, on their way home, whether they believed any of it.

## 2. The prisoners

By the spring of 1945 there were nearly three hundred and eighty thousand German prisoners in the United States, spread over hundreds of camps. Many had been captured in Tunisia, Italy and France. They picked cotton, apples and corn, and harvested wheat for American farmers. In the evenings, many still changed back into their German uniforms. In some camps, hard-line Nazis ran the barracks like a little Germany.

Gerhard Hennes was one of those prisoners. He was a young officer from Koblenz, on the Rhine, who had served in Rommel's army in North Africa. He was captured in May 1943, in the last pocket to surrender in Tunisia, and was sent to an officers' camp at Crossville, Tennessee. Years later he admitted that in the camp, most of them still clung to their faith in Hitler.

In 1944 a prisoner postcard reached him from a camp in Trinidad, Colorado. It was from Major Friedrich Hennes, his father, who had been captured in France. His father asked for a transfer, paid for his own train ticket and for the ticket of the guard who watched him, and arrived in Crossville in time for Christmas 1944. They were the only father and son in the camp.

## 3. The program

In Washington, officials worried about sending these men home exactly as they had arrived. In 1944 the War Department agreed to a secret program to change their minds. Teaching politics to prisoners looked like a breach of the Geneva Convention, so the program borrowed a harmless line from Article Seventeen, and took a harmless name: the Intellectual Diversion Program.

Its headquarters, nicknamed the Idea Factory, opened in October 1944 at a former Civilian Conservation Corps camp in upstate New York, and later moved to Fort Kearney, Rhode Island. About eighty-five German prisoners worked there, writers and teachers chosen as anti-Nazis. They printed books that had been banned in Germany, and from March 1945 they published a newspaper for the camps, called The Call. Hard-line Nazis called it Jewish propaganda and burned copies.

Films were part of the plan from the start. Nearly every camp had bought a sixteen millimeter projector, and prisoners paid fifteen cents a seat. The Idea Factory approved a list of a hundred and fifteen American features, among them Abe Lincoln in Illinois and The Adventures of Tom Sawyer. Gangster films and cowboy films were left off the list.

## 4. The film

Then, in the spring of 1945, the camps received footage of a different kind. Army cameramen had filmed the concentration camps the American and British armies entered in April. After Germany surrendered on the eighth of May, the films were sent to the prisoner camps in America. On the twelfth of May, the Des Moines Register reported that three hundred and forty-three thousand German prisoners in America were watching them.

Attendance was compulsory. In many camps, every man signed a register afterwards, and American officers wrote reports on how each audience behaved. With the films came a booklet in German, with photographs: German Concentration and Prisoner of War Camps, as the American and British Armies Found Them, April 1945. In some camps the prisoners had their own name for the screenings. They called them the bone films.

The footage is still very hard to watch: survivors so thin they could barely stand, and the dead lying in the open. We will not show it here. What matters in this story is what happened in the dark, in the rows of seats, among men who had fought for the state that built those camps.

## 5. Crossville

At Crossville, the screening came in the spring, at the movie house outside the wire. Gerhard Hennes described the survivors' empty eyes, the mass graves and the ovens. We saw, he wrote, and stared in silence, struggling but unable to believe what we Germans had done to Jews, gypsies, prisoners of war and many others.

He remembered the theater as dark and suddenly very hot. He admitted he had heard the name Dachau before the war, and had seen the pogrom of November 1938 in his own town. But he had no idea, he wrote, of the scale of the killing. That spring day, in his words, he turned from being a hero into being a villain. None of us in Crossville, he wrote, will ever forget that documentary.

## 6. A thousand uniforms

At Camp Butner, North Carolina, a camp built for about five thousand prisoners, the Army showed a film that ran twenty-five minutes. Afterwards, according to two histories of the prisoner camps, about a thousand men burned their German uniforms. The historian Judith Gansberg, who worked from the Army's own camp reports, wrote that they did it voluntarily.

It is the most dramatic reaction on record, but it was not the only one. At Eglin Field in Florida, prisoners took up a collection and gave two thousand, three hundred and seventy-one dollars to American war charities. In other camps, men collected money for the survivors. At Camp Shelby in Mississippi, a prisoner named Josef remembered the screening this way. It was hard for the Americans to believe, he said, but we really didn't know.

In Iowa, at Camp Clarinda, the two hundred and twenty-five prisoners who saw the film wrote a letter to the camp commander, Lieutenant Colonel George Ball. They were veterans of Tunisia, Italy and France. The camp spokesman signed first, then every man who had seen the film. They called the crimes deplorable and wanted it on record that they had no part in what was done at Buchenwald. The letter went up the chain to the Army command in Omaha.

At Camp Algona, in northern Iowa, the prisoners printed the story in their own camp newspaper. They wrote that they had turned away from the pictures with the utmost revulsion, and they declared their complete and total solidarity with the men at Clarinda.

## 7. Unmoved

But many audiences did not react that way. At Halloran, the Times reported, most of the men stayed outwardly unmoved. At Fort DuPont, in Delaware, the prisoner who ran the projector told a reporter what the men were saying. Some of the men think, he said, the bodies in the movie are Germans whom the Russians tortured.

The Army had expected that. The prisoners had been raised on propaganda, and they expected propaganda from their captors. Gansberg noted that the films convinced most where they mixed the camps with familiar German streets and faces, scenes that could not have been staged in America.

## 8. Ninety-five percent

By the autumn of 1945 the war was over everywhere, and the prisoners knew they would be sent back. Rumors spread that cooperative men would go home first. Suddenly, democracy was popular. At Camp Butner, the camp where a thousand men had burned their uniforms, the prisoners voted ninety-five percent in favor of democracy.

The prisoner editors of The Call did not believe it. In an article written for the fifteenth of November 1945, and cut from the paper at the last moment, they wrote: Ninety-five percent, it sounds like an old, well-known fairy tale. As you know, Goebbels never allowed lower figures.

The Army wanted to know too. Before the prisoners sailed, it gave an anonymous questionnaire to more than twenty-two thousand of them at the embarkation camps. Most gave the answers their captors hoped for. But only thirty percent accepted as true the reports of the concentration camps. The rest, the historian Ron Robin wrote, dismissed the films, the pictures and the booklets as mere propaganda. More than half still placed at least part of the blame for Germany's troubles on the Jews.

## 9. Going home

Many of the prisoners who sailed from America did not go home. They were handed over to Britain and France as laborers. Gerhard Hennes and his father left Crossville in December 1945. Before he went, Gerhard hid two medals in the lining of his toilet bag, and buried the rest of his medals and ribbons under his barrack.

They were taken to a camp at Attichy, in France, where they went hungry. Each of them fell to about a hundred and ten pounds. They were released on the thirtieth of January 1946, thirteen years to the day after Hitler came to power. In 1948, the French occupation authorities removed Friedrich Hennes from his pulpit in Koblenz, because he had gone along with the Nazi Party.

Gerhard went to work for Church World Service, a relief agency for refugees, and in 1958 he became an American citizen. He wrote that he had never fully recovered from the shock of that hot, dark theater in Crossville.

## 11. The medals

In 1964, Gerhard Hennes went back to Crossville with his wife and daughters. The guards' barracks had become a four-H camp, run by one of his former guards. The prisoners' barracks were gone. There were concrete foundations, rotten floor joists, and a few shreds of barbed wire. For a few minutes, he and his daughters dug in the soil where his barrack had stood. They never found the medals.
