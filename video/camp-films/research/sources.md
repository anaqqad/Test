# Sources: the 1945 camp films and German POWs in America

## Why this topic
Competitor scan, 15 Nov 2026 (YouTube search sorted by views, last month, and competitors' Videos tabs):
- "The US Army Forced German POWs to Watch One Film. Historians Say 1,000 Burned Their Uniforms" (Frank Holden WWII): 130K views in 9 days.
- The POW-in-America format is still the hottest in the niche: Kansas lumberyard 572K (3 wks), train windows 631K (2 wks), Texas farmer answered in German 468K (2 wks; our New Ulm episode), captured nurses 243K (2 wks; our Cherbourg episode), youngest POWs were 16, 154K (2 wks).
- Our documented version: the screenings (Halloran, Butner, Clarinda, Algona, Crossville, Fort DuPont, Eglin, Shelby), told through Gerhard Hennes's memoir, ending with the Army's own exit poll (only 30 % believed the reports). Not covered by our past episodes (New Ulm only mentioned letters about Dachau).

## 1. Arnold Krammer, *Nazi Prisoners of War in America* (Stein and Day, 1979)
(archive.org `naziprisonersofw00kram`, read via search-inside.)
- p. 228: "The War Department placed particular emphasis on the showing of atrocity films both as a lesson in 'collective guilt' and as a tool in the reeducation effort. Attendance for all prisoners was mandatory, and in many camps they were required to sign a register afterward. The prisoners' reactions were carefully monitored and, as expected, varied widely. During a showing to 150 POWs at Halloran General Hospital, for example, 'a few men held handkerchiefs over their eyes, and one sat with bowed head and with hands tightly covering his ears for most of the film. . . . The majority, however, remained outwardly unmoved.' At Fort DuPont, Delaware, the POW projector operator confided to an interested reporter that 'Some of the men think the bodies in the movie are Germans whom the Russians tortured.' On the other hand, after viewing a 25-minute atrocity film, a thousand prisoners at C[amp Butner]…"
- n. 55 (p. 323): "Germans Are Unmoved by Atrocity Film: Prisoners Lay War Crimes to 'Higher-Ups,'" *New York Times*, June 27, 1945, p. 5.
- p. 228: prisoners were charged 15 cents admission; after the fall of Germany, atrocity newsreels and anti-Nazi films were introduced; AEOs reported on attendance and audience reaction.
- p. 72: nearly every camp bought a 16 mm projector from canteen profits in spring 1944.
- p. 65: after work, prisoners changed "usually into their German uniforms".
- p. 88: "German prisoners at Eglin Field, Florida, outraged at their government after viewing a series of atrocity films, were moved to contribute the substantial sum of $2,371 to American war charities."
- p. 108: wheat, apples, corn; cotton in the South.
- p. 182: James H. Powers (Boston Globe) on Nazi noncoms as "a police force in the camp… a little Germany".
- p. 206: War Department officially concurred with a reeducation program in September 1944.

## 2. Judith M. Gansberg, *Stalag: U.S.A.* (Crowell, 1977)
(archive.org `stalagusarema00gans`; drew on the Army's camp reports.)
- p. 34: "Camp Butner, North Carolina, a five-thousand-man camp…"
- p. 127: the most convincing films "blended many familiar German scenes and personalities with concentration camp footage. Since the films demonstrated credibility through home-front scenes that could not be faked… The POWs, naturally, expected to be subjected to propaganda."
- p. 127: the booklet *Deutsche Konzentrations- und Gefangenenlager was die amerikanischen und britischen Armeen vorfanden April 1945* ("German Concentration and Prisoner-of-War Camps as the American and British Armies Found Them, April 1945").
- p. 127: "POW Josef K. and his comrades at Camp Shelby were shocked to see the films. He remembered: 'We were forced to watch the movies about the concentration camps. This was the first knowledge we had of these things happening. It was hard for the Americans to believe, but we really didn't know.'"
- p. 127: "A thousand men at Camp Butner voluntarily burned their German uniforms. At other camps, prisoners took up collections for the survivors of the German camps."
- p. 123: Butner's segregation compound for hard-core Nazis, "Little Siberia".

## 3. Ron Robin, *The Barbed-Wire College: Reeducating German POWs in the United States During World War II* (Princeton, 1995)
(archive.org `barbedwirecolleg0000robi`.)
- p. 102: an article "scheduled for publication in the November 15, 1945, edition of Der Ruf, but subsequently deleted at the last moment": rumors that cooperative prisoners would be repatriated first; "So Camp Butner, North Carolina voted ninety-five percent in favor of democracy, thereby obediently falling into the ranks of a new ideology… Ninety-five percent—it sounds like an old well-known fairy tale. As you know, Goebbels never allowed lower figures."
- p. 178: "the U.S. Army decided to poll a large sample of over 22,000 prisoners gathered at embarkation centers… the departing prisoners dutifully completed their anonymous questionnaires with politically correct answers."
- p. 179: "Fifty-seven percent of all replies placed at least partial blame for Germany's misfortunes leading to the Second World War on the shoulders of Jews… Only 30 percent accepted as true the reports of concentration camps and Nazi atrocities. The rest dismissed the films, pictures, and booklets which they had been required to view as mere propaganda."
- p. 156: PMG Archer Lerch held back the release of the SPD atrocity film "to edit out what he considered to be 'objectional features'" (cut from the script for length).
- p. 25: graduates of the crash courses went home directly, "even as their comrades were being sent to France as forced laborers".

## 4. Gerhard H. Hennes, *The Barbed Wire: POW in the U.S.A.* (Brunswick, 2004)
(archive.org `barbedwirepowinu0000henn`.)
- p. 19: captured in the Enfidaville pocket, "the last to surrender in Tunisia on May 13, 1943".
- p. 69/99: officers' camp; "we clung to the faith in the Führer. Most of us, that is."
- p. 87: a POW postcard from "Major Friedrich Hennes… from Trinidad, Colorado. So Father was a POW, too… He said that he would apply for a transfer to Crossville."
- p. 95: father captured near Falaise (France); "arriving in Crossville just in time for Christmas, 1944"; "He had to pay for his ticket and that of a guard watching him!"
- p. 97: "the only father-and-son team in the POW camp"; father known as a preacher.
- p. 100: "mandatory attendance at the moviehouse outside the camp. A documentary was shown about German concentration camps. We saw the emaciated bodies and empty eyes of the survivors. We saw the piles of naked bodies, starved to death. We saw the mass graves. We saw the ovens… We saw and stared in silence, struggling but unable to believe what we Germans had done to Jews, gypsies, prisoners of war, and many others deemed inferior or expendable. None of us in Crossville will ever forget that documentary."
- p. 101: had heard of Dachau, had seen the pogroms of November 1938; "I had no idea of the extent and ferocity of murdering innocent people. And I have never totally recovered from the shock sustained in that dark and suddenly uncomfortably hot movie theater in Crossville… That day, when spring was at its most beautiful around Crossville, was also the day when I turned in one profound transformation from being a hero to being a villain."
- p. 106: kept his two Iron Crosses in the lining of his toilet bag; other medals and ribbons "put away under our barrack".
- p. 107/125: left Crossville in December 1945.
- p. 115: Attichy, France: "each of us would weigh no more than 110 pounds."
- p. 118/119: discharge on January 30, 1946, "on this very day thirteen years earlier Adolf Hitler had come to power"; father also released and on the same train.
- p. 125: father classed a Mitläufer; removed from preaching at St. Florin's, Koblenz, in 1948 by a French colonel.
- p. 125: "In 1964, my daughters, wife, and I visited camp Crossville, where the guards' barracks had been turned into a 4-H camp. One of our former captors was its supervisor… The barracks were gone, except for some termite-eaten floor joists and concrete foundations… a few shreds of barbed wire… we could not find the medals that I had hidden on that day in Crossville in December 1945."
- p. 132: Church World Service; U.S. citizen since 1958.

## 5. Ronald H. Bailey, "The Secret and Controversial Attempt to Teach German POWs About Freedom," HistoryNet (2008)
- Nearly 380,000 Germans held in the U.S.; secret program from summer 1944; nearly 500 camps.
- Obstacle: a Geneva Convention provision barring indoctrination; cover name Intellectual Diversion Program, from Art. 17: "So far as possible, belligerents shall encourage intellectual diversions and sports organized by prisoners of war."
- The Idea Factory: Camp Van Etten (former CCC camp, upstate New York) from October 1944, later Fort Kearney, Rhode Island; 85 German POWs.
- *Der Ruf* (The Call): first issue March 1, 1945; "Hard-core Nazis denounced it as 'Jewish propaganda' and burned copies."
- 115 approved feature films incl. *Abe Lincoln in Illinois* and *The Adventures of Tom Sawyer*; gangster and cowboy films excluded.
- Concentration camp films: "Knochenfilme" (bone films); Gerhard Hennes quote; Eglin $2,371; Butner "one thousand prisoners were 'so ashamed that they burned their German uniforms.'"
- Andersch and Richter revived *Der Ruf* in Germany; the military government suspended it (cut from the script).

## 6. "Buchenwald" page, *Traces: US-German relations* (usgerrelations.traces.org), citing POW camp newspapers
- *Drahtpost* no. 15 (Camp Algona, 3 June 1945), citing the *Des Moines Register* of 12 May 1945: "343,000 German prisoners in America watch films".
- *Lagerzeitung* no. 1 (Camp Algona, 21 July 1945), reprinting the *Des Moines Register* of 4 July 1945: at Camp Clarinda, the 225 POWs who saw the film called the atrocities "deplorable" and wrote a protest letter to the commander, Lt. Col. George Ball, signed first by the camp spokesman, then by every man who saw the film, forwarded to the 7th Service Command in Omaha; veterans of Tunisia, Italy and France. The Algona POWs turned away "with utmost revulsion" and declared "complete and total solidarity".

## 7. Photograph caption, International News Photo / OWI Radiophoto, 3 July 1945 (ww2online.org, National WWII Museum, accession 2012.019.161)
- "German prisoners shocked by atrocity pictures": German POWs shown official films of German murder camps on June 26 in the theater of Halloran General Hospital, Staten Island; many covered their eyes. (Photo itself not used: rights unclear.)

## 8. 1929 Geneva Convention, Art. 17 (verbatim)
"So far as possible, belligerents shall encourage intellectual diversions and sports organized by prisoners of war."

## Disagreements and limits
- **Butner, 1,000 uniforms.** Gansberg (1977) and Krammer (1979) both give it; HistoryNet repeats it. No 1945 newspaper report was found; the number may trace to one Army camp report. The script says "according to two histories of the prisoner camps".
- **When the screenings began.** Robin (p. 137) dates PMG screenings of atrocity footage to late summer 1945; the *Des Moines Register* (12 May 1945), the Halloran screening (26 June) and Hennes ("spring") show screenings from May–June. The script follows the contemporary reports.
- **Halloran.** The *Times* headline stressed that most were "unmoved"; the INP caption stressed that many covered their eyes. The script gives both.
- **The poll.** Robin gives "over 22,000" departing prisoners and 30 % accepting the reports as true; HistoryNet's rounding ("twenty thousand… nearly two-thirds refused to believe") is consistent. The script follows Robin.
- **"Gypsies"** is Hennes's own word, quoted.
