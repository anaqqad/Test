# German POWs Ate at the Tables. The Black Soldiers Were Sent to the Kitchen.

## 1. Cold open
It was a morning during the Second World War, at a railroad station somewhere on the line between Louisiana and Arizona. Nine American soldiers in uniform had been waiting there since the day before for their train. They were Black, and in that town not one lunchroom would sell them a cup of coffee.

The only place that would feed them was the lunchroom at the station itself. But they were not allowed to sit down in it. They had to eat in the kitchen.

Then, at eleven thirty, about two dozen German prisoners of war came into the station with two American guards. The Germans sat down at the tables. Their meals were served to them. They talked, they smoked, and one of the nine Americans stood outside, looking on.

His name was Rupert Trimmingham, a corporal in the Corps of Engineers. What he saw that morning, he wrote down in a letter. And when the Army's own magazine printed it, hundreds of soldiers wrote back.

This is the documented story of the German prisoners who were served where American soldiers were not. It is told from the letters the soldiers wrote, from the newspapers of the time, and from the words of a famous singer who saw the same thing and walked off the stage.

## 2. Old Man Jim Crow
To understand that morning, you have to know what the country looked like in 1944. About nine hundred thousand African Americans served in uniform during the war. They served in a segregated Army, in their own units, under rules that kept them apart from white soldiers in camps, in mess halls and in theaters.

Off the base, in much of the country, the law was harder still. In the South it was written into every bus station and every restaurant door. The signs said "White" and "Colored", and a man in an American uniform was still, first of all, colored.

Trimmingham was not a young man. He was born in Port of Spain, Trinidad, in 1899. He had been a merchant seaman out of Wales after the First World War, and he came to New York in 1925. He joined the Army Corps of Engineers in 1942, as an electrician. He was forty-four years old.

He was stationed at Fort Huachuca, in the Arizona desert, near the Mexican border. At the height of the war it held the largest concentration of Black soldiers in the country. The ninety-third Infantry Division, the first all-Black division formed in the war, was brought back to life there.

He and eight other soldiers were on their way from Camp Claiborne, in Louisiana, to the hospital at Fort Huachuca. Somewhere on the way, they had to lay over until the next day. The letter never names the town.

## 3. The prisoners
At the same time, another army was crossing America by train. They were German prisoners of war. More than three hundred and seventy thousand Germans would end up in camps in the United States, and two thirds of them were held in the South.

The United States treated them by the book. The book was the Geneva Convention of 1929. Its eleventh article said the food of prisoners of war "shall be equal in quantity and quality to that of troops at base camps."

So the prisoners ate the same rations as American soldiers. And when they travelled, they ate where their guards took them.

The Convention said nothing about which room they should eat in. That was decided by the people who ran the lunchroom, and in 1944, in that town, those people decided that white enemy soldiers could sit at the tables, and Black American soldiers could not.

## 4. The letter
Trimmingham went back to Fort Huachuca, and he wrote to Yank, the Army Weekly. Yank was written by enlisted men for enlisted men. At its peak it printed two million six hundred thousand copies of every issue.

He began with a question. What is the Negro soldier fighting for? On whose team are we playing?

He told them about the lunchroom. He wrote that the Germans "entered the lunchroom, sat at the tables, had their meals served, talked, smoked, in fact had quite a swell time."

Then he wrote the lines that would be quoted for the rest of the century. "Are these men sworn enemies of this country? Are we not American soldiers, sworn to fight for and die if need be for this our country? Then why are they treated better than we are?"

He ended with a bet. Some of the boys, he wrote, are saying that you will not print this letter. I'm saying that you will.

Yank printed it, in the issue of April the twenty-eighth, 1944.

## 5. The answers
The answers came from all over the world. In the June ninth issue, Yank printed a letter from a corporal at an air field in California, Henry Wootton. He wrote that he was a Southern rebel. "If this incident is democracy," he wrote, "I don't want any part of it!"

A staff sergeant in Bermuda, Arthur Kaplan, thanked the editors for having the courage to print it. He asked whether anyone had thought that those German prisoners must still be laughing at us.

A private at a port of embarkation, Gustave Santiago, wrote that he was not a Negro, but he had been around, and he knew what the score was.

And from the jungle of Burma came a letter signed by four white soldiers: an Italian, a Frenchman, a Swede and an Irishman, as they described themselves. They wrote that Black units worked beside them and did more than their part. "We are proud of the colored men here," they wrote.

They had read the letter, and they wrote: Corporal Trimmingham asked what is a Negro fighting for? "If this sort of thing continues, we the white soldiers will begin to wonder: What are we fighting for?"

Yank printed that letter on the twenty-eighth of July, under the heading Democracy, with a question mark. Next to it was a second letter from Fort Huachuca. Trimmingham thanked them for printing his first one. Then he told them what had happened since. "To date I've received 287 letters, and, strange as it may seem, 183 are from white men and women in the armed service."

Most of them, he wrote, were from the Deep South. They were proud of the South, and ashamed. He called it new hope. All that the Negro is asking for, he wrote, is to be given half a chance.

The editors added a note. They had received a great number of comments, almost all of them outraged by the way the corporal had been treated. The incident had been dramatized on the radio. That June, the New Yorker printed a short story based on it, called "A Short Wait between Trains."

## 6. Salina, Kansas
Trimmingham's lunchroom was not the only one. In the fall of 1942, an all-Black outfit came to Salina, Kansas, to open the new Smoky Hill Army Air Field. One of its men was Lloyd Brown, who rose to sergeant and later became a writer.

German prisoners of war were in Salina too. One day at noon, Brown and several other men from his outfit walked into a lunchroom in town. They had come to see something with their own eyes.

Brown told the story years later, in the New York Times. As they came in, he wrote, the counterman hurried to the back to get the owner. The owner hurried out to tell them, politely: "You boys know we don't serve colored here."

They did not leave. They stood inside the door, staring at what they had come to see: the German prisoners of war, having lunch at the counter.

Brown wrote that the people of Salina "would serve these enemy soldiers and turn away black American G.I.'s." And he finished with a sentence that is hard to forget. "If we were untermenschen in Nazi Germany, they would break our bones. As 'colored' men in Salina, they only break our hearts."

## 7. Not one town
The historian Matthias Reiss later studied letters like these. He found the same complaint from many parts of the country, written by men who had never met each other.

In February 1944, a Black soldier named Bert Babero wrote from a camp in Texas that one part of the latrine was set aside for Negro soldiers, and the other for the German prisoners and the white soldiers. At least two camps in Mississippi gave the prisoners white water fountains too.

In South Carolina, Charles Dryden, one of the Tuskegee Airmen, wrote that German prisoners could walk into the white side of the post exchange cafeteria, and his men could not.

The historian John Hope Franklin, then a young man, remembered a segregated train. German prisoners on board watched the Black passengers packed into their car, and to them, he said, this was the funniest thing. They were laughing.

And in January 1945, a Black newspaper reported that the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People had protested to the War Department about the prisoner of war camp at Florence, Arizona. Black Army nurses at the camp hospital had been ordered to eat in a separate dining room, while German prisoners served as cooks and waiters in the officers' mess. The prisoners, the complaint said, looked on with great glee.

## 8. Lena Horne
By 1944, Lena Horne was one of the most famous singers in America. She toured Army camps for the USO, and her contract said she would not sing for segregated audiences.

That year, she came to Camp Robinson, outside Little Rock, Arkansas. Camp Robinson held German prisoners of war. The night before, she had sung for the white soldiers and workers on the post. The next night was meant to be for the Black soldiers.

She came out on stage and looked at the audience. The Black soldiers were at the back of the hall. In the rows in front of them sat men she took for white soldiers. They were German prisoners of war.

More than fifty years later, she told the story herself. "I went to the back where they were sitting," she said, "and sang with my back to all the other people."

Her daughter, the writer Gail Buckley, told the rest. Her mother demanded to see the NAACP. And the NAACP arrived, Buckley said, in the person of one woman: Daisy Bates, who later became a hero of Little Rock.

Then her tour was over. In Horne's words, they threw her out of the USO. So she paid her own way, and kept going to the camps to sing for Black soldiers whenever she could get space on a train.

In January 1945, a Black newspaper in Omaha, Nebraska, reported that Lena Horne had called a halt to her Army tour. It told the story a little differently: that the commander at Camp Robinson had kept the camp's fifty Black soldiers out of her shows, while German prisoners of war were let in. Either way, it was the same insult.

## 9. What changed
Some things were already changing. At first, Black soldiers had not been allowed to serve as escort guards for prisoners at all. In April 1944, the Army disbanded its segregated escort guard system.

The story of the lunchroom went on without Trimmingham. After the New Yorker story, a Chicago theatre group made it into a play, first staged in October 1945. It came back on stage in 1957, and as a film on television in 1999.

The last German prisoners went home from the United States by July 1946.

Two years later, on the twenty-sixth of July, 1948, President Truman signed Executive Order nine nine eight one. It said "there shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion or national origin."

It took years more to carry out. But the order was signed, and the segregated Army that Rupert Trimmingham had served in began to come to an end.

## 10. Afterwards
Trimmingham was discharged in 1946. He moved to Gary, Indiana, and worked as an electrician for the Singer Sewing Machine Company. He became an American citizen in 1950. In 1956 he moved to Ann Arbor, Michigan.

He died there on the ninth of May, 1985, and was buried in Fairview Cemetery. His wife Harriet, whom he married in Arizona during the war, was buried beside him.

Lloyd Brown went on to work with Paul Robeson and wrote books of his own. He died in New York in 2003.

Lena Horne kept singing for decades. She died on the ninth of May, 2010, twenty-five years to the day after the corporal from Fort Huachuca.

## 12. Today
Nobody knows the name of that town, or of that station. The lunchroom has no plaque.

What is left is a letter, written by a corporal who bet that the Army's magazine would print it. He was right. And the question he asked that morning, standing outside, is still reprinted in history books. Then why are they treated better than we are?
