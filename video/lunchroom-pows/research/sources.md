## A. Rupert Trimmingham and Yank

### 1. Library of America, *Reporting World War II: American Journalism 1944-1946* (1995), pp. 470-473, "Democracy? by Rupert Trimmingham and others" (Story of the Week PDF)
- URL: https://www.loa.org/images/pdf/Trimmingham_Democracy.pdf (intro page: https://storyoftheweek.loa.org/2017/10/democracy.html)
- What it is: the Library of America reprint of the Yank letters, set from the original issues. Header: "Originally published the April 28, June 9, and July 28 issues of Yank." This is the best text of the first letter we could read (no free scan of the 28 April 1944 issue was found, see Media).
- FULL TEXT of the first letter, exactly as printed by LOA (the ". . ." ellipses are LOA's, i.e. Yank or LOA cut words there):

> Dear YANK:
> Here is a question that each Negro soldier is asking. What is the Negro soldier fighting for? On whose team are we playing? Myself and eight other soldiers were on our way from Camp Claiborne, La., to the hospital here at Fort Huachuca. We had to lay over until the next day for our train. On the next day we could not purchase a cup of coffee at any of the lunchrooms around there. As you know, Old Man Jim Crow rules. The only place where we could be served was at the lunchroom at the railroad station but, of course, we had to go into the kitchen. But that's not all; 11:30 A.M. about two dozen German prisoners of war, with two American guards, came to the station. They entered the lunchroom, sat at the tables, had their meals served, talked, smoked, in fact had quite a swell time. I stood on the outside looking on, and I could not help but ask myself these questions: Are these men sworn enemies of this country? Are they not taught to hate and destroy . . . all democratic governments? Are we not American soldiers, sworn to fight for and die if need be for this our country? Then why are they treated better than we are? Why are we pushed around like cattle? If we are fighting for the same thing, if we are to die for our country, then why does the Government allow such things to go on? Some of the boys are saying that you will not print this letter. I'm saying that you will.
> Fort Huachuca, Ariz. -Cpl. RUPERT TRIMMINGHAM
> Yank, April 28, 1944

- Replies printed in Yank, June 9, 1944 (per LOA):
  - Cpl. Henry S. Wootton Jr., Fairfield-Suisun AAF, Calif. (also signed by S/Sgt. A. S. Tepper and Pfc. Jose Rosenzweig): "Gentlemen, I am a Southern rebel, but this incident makes me none the more proud of my Southern heritage! Frankly, I think that this incident is a disgrace to a democratic nation such as ours is supposed to be." / "If this incident is democracy, I don't want any part of it!" / "I wonder what the "Aryan supermen" think when they get a first-hand glimpse of our racial discrimination." / "let me say that a lot of us, especially in the South, should cast the beam out of our own eyes before we try to do so in others, across the seas." Note: Wootton describes the POWs as fed "with the rest of the white civilians in the restaurant".
  - S/Sgt. Arthur J. Kaplan, Bermuda: "You are to be complimented on having the courage to print Cpl. Trimmingham's letter in an April issue of YANK." / "It seems incredible that German prisoners of war should be afforded the amenities while our own men-in uniform and changing stations-are denied similar attention because of color" / "It is, I think, high time that this festering sore was cut out by intelligent social surgeons once and for all." / "Has it occurred to anyone that those Boche prisoners of war must be still laughing at us?"
  - Pvt. Gustave Santiago, Port of Embarkation: "I'm not a Negro, but I've been around and know what the score is. I want to thank the YANK . . . and congratulate Cpl. Rupert Trimmingham."
- Letters of July 28, 1944: see source 2 (checked against the original scan).
- LOA intro facts: Yank first issue June 1942; staff "numbering 127 near the end of its run, were active duty soldiers from the enlisted ranks"; "At its peak 2,600,000 copies of each issue (with a total readership estimated at ten million)"; last issue December 1945. Also: "Both he and the editors were amazed by the response: hundreds of letters, virtually all of them denouncing Trimmingham's treatment". LOA dates the New Yorker story "June 14, 1944" (see Disagreements).

### 2. Yank, the Army Weekly, 28 July 1944 (Vol. 3 No. 6), "Mail Call" page, original scan (archive.org)
- URL: https://archive.org/details/per_yank_yank-the-army-weekly_1944-07-28_3_6/page/n13/mode/1up (page index n13; OCR: https://archive.org/download/per_yank_yank-the-army-weekly_1944-07-28_3_6/per_yank_yank-the-army-weekly_1944-07-28_3_6_djvu.txt). Same issue also at https://www.indianamilitary.org/YANK/1944-07-28%20Yank%20Magazine.pdf
- What it is: the original printed page, under the heading "Democracy?". Verified in the scan's OCR.
- Editor's bracket on the Burma letter: "(Cpl. Trimmingham described the way he was forced to eat in the kitchen of a station restaurant while a group of German prisoners was fed in the dining room.—Ed.)"
- Burma letter, signed "Pvt. JOSEPH POSCUCCI (Italian)*", "*Also signed by" a corporal (French), a Pfc. (Swedish) and a private (Irish) (OCR garbles their surnames; LOA prints Cpl. Edward A. Kreutler, Pfc. Maurice E. Wenson, Pvt. James F. Malloy). Quotes: "We are white soldiers in the Burma jungles, and there are many Negro outfits working with us. They are doing more than their part to win this war. We are proud of the colored men here." / "We are ashamed to read that the German soldier, who is the sworn enemy of our country, is treated better than the soldier of our country, because of race." / "Cpl. Trimmingham asked: What is a Negro fighting for? If this sort of thing continues, we the white soldiers will begin to wonder: What are we fighting for?"
- Trimmingham's second letter (scan + LOA agree): "Allow me to thank you for publishing my letter. Although there was some doubt about its being published, yet somehow I felt that YANK was too great a paper not to. . . . Each day brings three, four or five letters to me in answer to my letter. I just returned from my furlough and found 25 letters awaiting me. To date I've received 287 letters, and, strange as it may seem, 183 are from white men and women in the armed service. Another strange feature about these letters is that the most of these people are from the Deep South. They are all proud of the fact that they are of the South but ashamed to learn that there are so many of their own people who by their actions and manner toward the Negro are playing Hitler's game. Nevertheless, it gives me new hope to realize that there are doubtless thousands of whites who are willing to fight this Frankenstein that so many white people are keeping alive. All that the Negro is asking for is to be given half a chance and he will soon demonstrate his worth to his country. Should these white people who realize that the Negro is a man who is loyal—one who would gladly give his life for this our wonderful country—would stand up, join with us and help us to prove to their white friends that we are worthy, I'm sure that we would bury race hate and unfair treatment. Thanks again." Signed "Fort Huachuca, Ariz. —Cpl. RUPERT TRIMMINGHAM".
- Editors' note (same page): "Since YANK printed Cpl. Trimmingham's letter we have received a great number of comments from GIs, almost all of whom were outraged by the treatment given the corporal. His letter has been taken from YANK and widely quoted. The incident has been dramatized on the air and was the basis for a moving short story published recently in the New Yorker magazine."
- Note: the Burma writers say they read the letter "in a May edition of Yank" (Yank printed many regional editions on different dates).

### 6. Matthew Taub, "'Are We Not American Soldiers?' When the U.S. Military Treated German POWs Better Than Black Troops", TIME, 28 July 2020
- URL: https://time.com/5872361/wwii-german-pows-civil-rights/
- Trimmingham framed as "1944, and the Deep South". Other documented incidents (Taub cites Reiss 2004 and others):
  - Feb 1944, Black soldier Bert Babero wrote that a POW camp in Texas segregated "a section of the latrine for Negro soldiers, the other being used by the German prisoners and the white soldiers," placing "the tyrant…over the liberator."
  - "At least two camps in Mississippi treated POWs to "white" latrines and water fountains as well."
  - Charles W. Dryden, *A-Train: Memoirs of a Tuskegee Airman* (1997), base in South Carolina: German POWs could go "into the 'White' side of the post exchange cafeteria and WE COULD NOT!"
  - Camp Gordon Johnston, Florida: Black servicemen assigned to "dispose of the human waste of the whole camp," including the POWs'.
  - Utah work detail: German POWs refused to work for a Black soldier; officers said "colored BOYS are not allowed to detail or work prisoners of war".
  - Florida: Black men "were constantly guarded by armed guards" "while the Nazis arrogantly walk around this field free and scoff at us."
  - John Hope Franklin (1995 interview), segregated train: to the German POWs "this was the funniest thing. They were laughing and looking up there at us, standing on top of each other practically".
  - Pvt. Harold Hardy, 1944 letter to the NAACP: the Germans could "laugh at columns of our troops walking separately from the white soldiers." [sic spellings kept in TIME]
  - Taub: "Segregation was official military policy until 1948".

## C. Other incidents and context

### 17. Lloyd L. Brown, "Brown v. Salina, Kansas", New York Times, 26 February 1973 (op-ed) - read only through book quotations
- The NYT original could not be opened (paywall). Book fragments read via Open Library "search inside" (https://openlibrary.org/search/inside?q=%22The%20people%20of%20Salina%20would%20serve%20these%20enemy%20soldiers%22). Books that quote it: Leon Litwack, *How Free Is Free? The Long Death of Jim Crow* (2009) p. ~218 (note 47 cites the NYT piece); John Morton Blum, *V Was for Victory* (1976) (notes: "The account of Salina is from Lloyd L. Brown, "Brown v. Salina, Kansas," NYT, February 26, 1973"); William Loren Katz, *Eyewitness* (headed "War and Race in Salina, Kansas", and mentions "The Congressional Record"); Arnold Krammer, *Nazi Prisoners of War in America* (1979); Paul Spickard, *Almost All Aliens*; AP *World War II: A 50th Anniversary History*.
- NOTE: this account is NOT from Terkel's *The Good War*; no source read places it there.
- Setting: Brown's all-Black outfit came to Salina "in the fall of 1942 to open up the Smoky Hill Army [Air Field]" (Katz/Spickard fragment). Spickard places the lunchroom "counter on Santa Fe Street". Katz: "One day at high noon several of us enlisted men from Squadron [...]".
- Exact fragments (each verified in at least two books):
  - "As we entered, the counterman hurried to the rear to get the owner, who hurried out front to tell us with urgent politeness: "You boys know we don't serve colored here.""  (the closing words "serve colored here" appear in Blum, Katz, Spickard; Kallen/Bailey paraphrase "You know we don't serve colored here")
  - "We ignored him, and just stood there inside the door, staring at what we had come to see — the German prisoners of war who were having lunch at the counter." (Litwack; Kallen has "We ignored [the counterman]")
  - Litwack continues: "There were about ten of them [...]" then "[...] This was really happening. It was no jive talk. It was the Gospel truth. The people of Salina would serve these enemy soldiers and turn away black American G.I.'s. ... If we were untermenschen in Nazi Germany, they would break our bones. As "colored" men in Salina, they only break our hearts."
- Unit: Wikipedia "49th Aviation Squadron" (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/49th_Aviation_Squadron): "an all-Black squadron stationed at Smoky Hill Army Air Field in Salina, Kansas during World War II", re-designated "upon arrival at Smoky Hill Army Air Field (SHAAF), Salina, Kansas" effective 1 April 1943. (Brown's exact squadron number is not confirmed in anything read; the article's member list has a "Sergeant Lloyd R. Brown", not "L.") 
- Brown's life (Wikipedia "Lloyd L. Brown", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd_L._Brown): "Lloyd Louis Brown (April 3, 1913 – April 1, 2003) was an American labor organizer, Communist Party activist, journalist, novelist, friend and editorial companion of Paul Robeson's"; "joined the US Army Air Corps and rose to the rank of sergeant"; managing editor of *New Masses* 1946-48; helped Robeson write *Here I Stand* (1958); "Lloyd Brown died in New York on April 1, 2003." (NYT obituary 14 April 2003 cited there.)

### 19. Matthew Wills, "Prisoners Like Us: German POW and Black American Solidarity", JSTOR Daily, 25 Feb 2023 (on Matthias Reiss, "Solidarity among 'Fellow Sufferers'", *Journal of African American History* 98:4, Fall 2013, pp. 531-561)

### 20. Wikipedia, "German prisoners of war in the United States" (raw wikitext)
- URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_prisoners_of_war_in_the_United_States

### 21. Geneva Convention relative to the Treatment of Prisoners of War, 27 July 1929 (Avalon Project, Yale)
- URL: https://avalon.law.yale.edu/20th_century/geneva02.asp
- Art. 2: "They must at all times be humanely treated and protected, particularly against acts of violence, insults and public curiosity."
- Art. 11: "The food ration of prisoners of war shall be equal in quantity and quality to that of troops at base camps." ... "Prisoners may be employed in the kitchens."
- Use: the law required POWs be fed like US base troops; it said nothing that required seating them in a whites-only lunchroom. (That last point is our own reading, not a quote.)

### 22. Executive Order 9981, 26 July 1948 (Truman Library)
- URL: https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/executive-orders/9981/executive-order-9981
- "It is hereby declared to be the policy of the President that there shall be equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion or national origin. This policy shall be put into effect as rapidly as possible, having due regard to the time required to effectuate any necessary changes without impairing efficiency or morale." Signed "July 26, 1948."
- LOA intro (source 1) adds, citing Alan Gropman: the Army "tied the committee in semantic knots, claiming even after the committee had disbanded that segregation and Truman's order were harmonious".

## Disagreements

1. **Where was the station?** Trimmingham's letter never names the town or state: only "lay over until the next day for our train" on the way from Camp Claiborne, La., to Fort Huachuca, Ariz. Wikipedia says "a small Louisiana town" and Sowards "Still in Louisiana" (both inferences, no source); TIME says only "the Deep South". Script should say "a railroad town on the way west" or "somewhere between Louisiana and Arizona", not a named town or state.
2. **Letter wording.** LOA (from the book): "about two dozen ... came to the station", "destroy . . . all democratic governments", "this our country". TAH/Wikipedia: "about a two dozen ... came into the station", "destroy all democratic governments", "this country". Use LOA's wording; LOA's ellipsis shows something was cut.
3. **Where the POWs ate.** Trimmingham: "sat at the tables". Yank editor (July 28): "fed in the dining room". Wootton: fed "with the rest of the white civilians". All agree the Black soldiers were sent to the kitchen.
4. **Which issue.** LOA/TAH: Yank of 28 April 1944. Burma letter: "a May edition of Yank"; Kaplan: "an April issue". Yank printed regional editions on different dates; 28 April 1944 is the date to use.
5. **New Yorker date of "A Short Wait between Trains".** LOA intro and Wikipedia: 14 June 1944; TAH and TIME: 17 June 1944. 17 June 1944 was a Saturday (New Yorker issue day); 14 June was a Wednesday. Prefer "June 1944".
6. **Count of reply letters.** Wikipedia says the 287 letters were "supporting his position"; Trimmingham's own words say only that he received 287, 183 from white service men and women, and describes their content. Use his words.
7. **Horne venue.** Camp Robinson, Little Rock/North Little Rock, Arkansas: Omaha Guide 13 Jan 1945 (contemporary), Horne herself in 1996 (first says "I think it was Kansas", then corrects to "outside Little Rock, Arkansas"), daughter Gail Buckley 2001 ("Camp Robertson, Arkansas, in 1944"), Arkansas Democrat-Gazette 2013. Fort Riley, Kansas: NPS page, Broadcast 41 (via WaPo obituary), LA Times obituary. Army.mil: no place. VERDICT: Camp Robinson, Arkansas is better supported (the only wartime source plus Horne's own correction). Fort Riley likely comes from her own "I think it was Kansas" slip. Date: around late 1944; the story ran in January 1945 (Buckley says 1944).
8. **What Horne did.** Horne (1996): went "to the back where they were sitting and sang with my back to all the other people". LA Times obit: "Marching off the platform, she turned her back on the POWs and sang to the black soldiers in the back of the hall." Army.mil: walked past the POWs "to perform at the first row where the black Soldiers sat." Buckley: "I'm not going to sing. Bring me the NAACP". Omaha Guide 1945: different story (Black soldiers kept out of her shows altogether while POWs were admitted). Safe script line: she stepped down and sang to the Black soldiers at the back, with her back to the Germans (Horne's own words), then went to the NAACP.
9. **Her tour afterwards.** Horne: "they throw me out the USO. So I took my own money". Buckley: "kicked out of the USO". NPS: "MGM Studios pulled her off its tour" and she "financed her own travel". Wikipedia: "After quitting the USO in 1945". Omaha Guide 1945: she herself called "a halt to the itinerary" and planned to resume. USO.org (2010): toured "extensively with the USO during WWII". Script should say the tour ended and she paid her own way to keep singing for Black troops, attributing "threw me out" to Horne.
10. **NAACP contact.** Buckley names Daisy Bates; the 1996 transcript is garbled at that point ("I'm dissipates"). Attribute Daisy Bates to Buckley only.
11. **POW totals.** ~425,000 (Wikipedia; may include all nationalities) vs "more than 371,000 Germans and some 51,000 Italians" (JSTOR Daily/Reiss). Use "about 371,000 Germans" and "more than 400,000 Axis prisoners" for the total.
12. **Lloyd Brown source.** The user brief places it in Terkel's *The Good War*; every book read cites Brown's own NYT op-ed "Brown v. Salina, Kansas" (26 Feb 1973). Credit the NYT piece. Paraphrases differ: "at the counter" (Brown) vs "sat at the tables" (Trimmingham) - two separate incidents.
13. **Trimmingham's unit.** No source read names his company or battalion; only "U.S. Army Corps of Engineers", electrician, corporal. The letter says the nine men were going "to the hospital here at Fort Huachuca" (Wikipedia: "the military hospital"); no source read says whether they were patients or staff, so do not say.
