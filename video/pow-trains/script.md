# What 370,000 German POWs Saw When Their Trains Crossed America

## 1. The number

Three hundred seventy-one thousand. That is how many German prisoners of war the United States was holding in May 1945. Most of them crossed America the same way: by train, looking out of a window.

They had been told what they would find. American cities in ruins. A country starving and afraid. In 1944, an Iowa newspaper ran a headline that said German prisoners believed New York was being bombed every day.

Then the trains rolled west, mile after mile, and the men at the windows saw something else. This is the documented story of what roughly three hundred seventy thousand German prisoners saw from American trains, and what it did to them.

## 2. The crossing

It started in North Africa. In May 1943, about two hundred seventy-five thousand Axis soldiers surrendered in Tunisia. Britain could not hold them all. The year before, the United States had reluctantly agreed to take fifty thousand prisoners. Now they came by the shipload.

Most crossed the Atlantic in Liberty ships that would otherwise have sailed home empty. Some came on great ocean liners painted wartime gray. A single trip on the Queen Mary could carry about fifteen thousand prisoners.

The convoys zigzagged to dodge U-boats. The prisoners knew the danger better than anyone: the submarines out there were their own. But the meals on board were big.

## 3. The ports

The ships landed at Norfolk, Virginia, at New York, and at Boston. At the peak, as many as thirty thousand prisoners a month were arriving. Between August and November 1944 alone, more than a hundred and ten thousand came through.

Günter Gräwe was eighteen when the Americans captured him in a field hospital in Normandy. In August 1944 he crossed on the Queen Mary with a few thousand other prisoners. He remembered the voyage as strangely luxurious. Then came a long train ride, all the way across the country to Fort Lewis, in Washington State.

## 4. The coaches

On January second, 1944, the liner Empress of Scotland unloaded its prisoners at Norfolk. One of them was a German soldier named Reinhold Pabel. The men were deloused, lined up, and marched to the railway station.

Many of them had ridden to war in freight cars. The train at Norfolk was made of passenger coaches with upholstered seats. Some prisoners later remembered them as Pullman cars. These modern coaches, Pabel wrote, were a pleasant surprise to everyone.

Then a Black porter came down the aisle with coffee and sandwiches. Pabel never forgot the way he offered them: politely, as though the prisoners were human beings. Most of us, he wrote, forgot our anti-American feelings.

## 5. The rules

The comfort had limits. A guard stood at each end of the coach. A prisoner who needed the washroom raised his hand and was escorted there and back.

The Army told its guards to be correct, never friendly. One instruction warned against soft, sticky sentiment, against what it called marshmallow friendliness. A model guard put it simply: you don't have to be cruel to these men, but you have to show them they won't get away with anything.

## 6. The window

And then there was the window. Pabel's train ran west through Virginia and Kentucky. We pressed our noses against the windowpanes, he wrote. The first thing that struck them was the number of automobiles. They were everywhere. So were the wooden frame houses, especially in Kentucky.

The prisoners had come from a continent at war. Out here, the war was nowhere to be seen. The sheer size of the country amazed them, and so did its prosperity, untouched by bombs.

Some of them saw Black Americans for the first time. Many saw paper plates for the first time. After a meal, they asked the guards how to wash them, and were told to throw them out of the window. One museum exhibit says the trains ran the same routes so often that the embankments filled with paper plates and napkins, like banks of snow.

## 7. Not every ride

Not every journey was comfortable. Hans Galhard rode to a camp in Colorado. He called the trip brutalizing. It lasted about a hundred hours. The men slept sitting up, and in each coach two guards carried automatic pistols and two carried clubs.

A few prisoners later made worse claims, of crowded freight cars and spoiled food. Historian Arnold Krammer found such stories extremely rare, and never proven.

## 8. The stations

At every stop, people came to look. William Oberdieck, captured in Africa in 1943, rode a train that went slowly chugging across the country to Kansas. Crowds gathered on the platforms. They came, he said, to see if we had horns or something.

Not everyone was curious. The head of the Army's prisoner of war division received hundreds of letters a week. One suggested putting the prisoners in Death Valley with a side of beef and letting them starve.

## 9. The arrival

At Mexia, in Texas, three thousand two hundred fifty prisoners arrived at once, in a town of about six thousand people. Still wearing their desert khaki and short pants, they marched to the camp. One resident remembered that the line stretched the full three miles.

In Kansas, Oberdieck was taken to a dining hall. He looked at the tables and thought: are they going to shoot us? Is this our last meal? The tables were set with real dinnerware.

## 10. The other passengers

There were other men on those trains. In 1944, Corporal Rupert Trimmingham and eight other Black soldiers were travelling from Louisiana to an Army post in Arizona. At a lunchroom on the way, they were sent to eat in the kitchen, while about two dozen German prisoners, with their guards, were served at the tables.

Trimmingham wrote to the Army magazine Yank. He asked a simple question: why are they treated better than we are? He was not alone. The historian John Hope Franklin remembered Black soldiers packed into one coach, while German prisoners rode with more room. The prisoners, he said, found it the funniest thing.

The Army would stay segregated until President Truman ordered it ended, in 1948.

## 11. What the window changed

In the camps, the prisoners worked on farms and in forests, for up to eighty cents a day in canteen coupons. Most were still in America in 1946.

Decades later, Günter Gräwe went back to Fort Lewis to say thank you. We were always treated with fairness, he wrote, and we got sufficient food, and eighty cents a day.

Reinhold Pabel, the prisoner from the Norfolk train, never forgot the porter with the coffee. In September 1945 he escaped, and lived in Chicago under another name for almost eight years. That is another story, and we have told it on this channel.

## 12. Echo

If someone in your family rode one of those trains, as a prisoner, a guard, or a passenger, tell us in the comments.

Three hundred seventy thousand men were told America was in ruins. They found out the truth the same way, one window at a time.
