# Sources: "What 370,000 German POWs Saw When Their Trains Crossed America"

No source, no line. Every fact in `script.md` maps to one of the entries below.

## Why this topic (competitor research, 1 Nov 2026)
The owner asked for the idea behind The War Archives' biggest video, "What 370,000 German POWs Saw Through the Windows of an
American Train" (49K views in 4 days; the channel is 3 weeks old), with the number shown first. Its other hits use the same
flip ("Braced for Execution. Americans Served Fried Chicken", 34K). Same week: "German POWs in Wisconsin Got Donuts at Midnight"
(663K), "German POWs Were Sent to Kansas…" (459K). Our version: own script, own order of scenes, only documented accounts,
real archive film (their thumbnail is an AI image; their fried-chicken video says its names and scenes are reconstructed).
The number comes first: title, opening card and first spoken line.

## Main sources
1. **uboat.net, "German POWs in North America"** (uboat.net/men/pow/pow_in_america.htm): more than 371,000 German POWs in the
   U.S. in May and June 1945 (over 425,000 Axis prisoners in all); over 110,000 entered between August and November 1944;
   entry through Camp Shanks, New York, or Norfolk, Virginia; over 640 camps, mostly in the South and Southwest; in August
   1942 the U.S. reluctantly agreed to take an emergency group of 50,000.
2. **uboat.net, "Journey to America"** (uboat.net/men/pow/pow_in_america_journey.htm, compiled by Glenn A. Sytko from Arnold
   Krammer, *Nazi Prisoners of War in America*, 1979; Reinhold Pabel, *Enemies Are Human*, 1955; Beverly Smith, "The Afrika Korps
   Comes to America", *American Magazine*): the *Empress of Scotland* landed prisoners at Norfolk on 2 Jan. 1944; delousing,
   march to the station; Pabel: "These modern upholstered coaches were a pleasant surprise to everyone"; a Black porter served
   coffee and sandwiches "as though we were human beings"; guards at each end of the coach; raise a hand to be escorted to the
   washroom; route through Virginia and Kentucky; the prisoners were struck by the number of automobiles. Hans Galhard (to
   Camp Trinidad, Colorado): the trip "lasted about one hundred hours", slept sitting up, two guards with automatic pistols and
   two with clubs in each coach. Henry Kemper's claims (boxcars of 60–100 men, only orange jam) are called "extremely rare" and
   "unsubstantiated". Guards were warned against "soft, sticky, sentiment, or marshmallow friendliness"; a model guard: "You
   don't have to be cruel to these guys, but you have to show them they're not going to get away with anything." Mexia,
   Texas: 3,250 prisoners, a resident said the line "stretched the full three miles out to the camp!" (town of about 6,000).
   *Collier's*: Col. Francis E. Howard, head of the POW Division, received hundreds of letters a week; one said "Put them in
   Death Valley, chuck in a side of beef, and let them starve to death." Wages paid in canteen coupons.
3. **Wikipedia, "German prisoners of war in the United States"**: usually shipped in Liberty ships returning home empty (Ron Robin,
   *The Barbed-Wire College*, 1995, p. 5; Wikipedia spells it "Rabin"); as many as 30,000 a month; "the comfort of the Pullman cars that carried them to
   their prison camps amazed the Germans" (Carlson, *We Were Each Other's Prisoners*, 1997, pp. 32, 70), as did "the country's
   large size and undamaged prosperity"; they risked being sunk by their own U-boats; substantial meals aboard ship.
   Peak census (List of POW camps / Populations pages): 371,683 Germans in May 1945.
4. **TRACES, "Held in the Heartland" museum panels** (usgerrelations.traces.org): liners such as the *Queen Mary*, painted gray,
   carried "some 15,000 POWs at a time"; convoys zigzagged; most ships landed at Newport News, New York or Boston; many POWs went
   on in Pullman cars to the Midwest; many saw Black people for the first time; first paper plates; told to throw them out of
   the windows; embankments of plates and napkins "like banks of snow" (panel text, no source given: told as "one museum
   exhibit says"); farmers paid 50–60 cents an hour, prisoners got up to 80 cents a day in scrip.
5. **Cynthia Mayer, "German POWs Recall Captivity in U.S. Camps", Knight-Ridder, *Seattle Times*, 29 Nov. 1992**: William
   Oberdieck, captured in Africa in 1943, on a train "slowly chugging across the United States to a place called Kansas";
   people came to the stations "to see if we had horns or something"; in the dining hall he thought "is it our last meal?";
   real dinnerware.
6. **Marie McCaffrey, "Former German POW Günter Gräwe Visits Fort Lewis…", HistoryLink.org essay 20472, 2017**: captured at 18
   in a field hospital in Normandy; shipped on the *Queen Mary* with "a few thousand other prisoners", August 1944; the long
   train ride across the U.S. to Fort Lewis, Washington; "We always were treated with fairness, got sufficient food and 80 cents
   a day."
7. **Matthew Taub, "'Are We Not American Soldiers?'…", *TIME*, 28 July 2020** (after Matthias Reiss's study of letters):
   Cpl. Rupert Trimmingham and eight other Black soldiers, travelling from Camp Claiborne, Louisiana, to Fort Huachuca, Arizona,
   had to eat in the kitchen of a lunchroom while about two dozen German POWs with their guards were served at the tables; his
   April 1944 letter to *Yank*: "Then why are they treated better than we are?" John Hope Franklin: Black soldiers packed into
   a coach while German POWs with white guards had more room; the prisoners found it "the funniest thing". Segregation stayed
   official policy until Truman's 1948 order.
8. **Iowa Heritage Digital Collections, item 20899**: a 1944 Iowa newspaper headline, "German prisoners think New York is bombed
   every day" (headline only; the page could not be opened).
9. **Last-soldier episode sources** (`../last-soldier/research/sources.md`): about 275,000 Axis soldiers surrendered in Tunisia,
   May 1943; most German prisoners were still working in the U.S. into 1946.

## Disagreements
- **The number:** 371,683 (May 1945 peak, census figure) vs "about 372,000" (TRACES) vs 425,000 (all Axis prisoners; Wikipedia
  lead). Title and narration: "roughly three hundred seventy thousand"; card: 371,683.
- **Pullman or coach:** Pabel says upholstered coaches; Carlson's interviewees say Pullman cars. Script: "passenger coaches. Some
  remembered them as Pullman cars."
- **Bad trips:** Galhard's hundred hours is told as his account; Kemper's claims are not used as fact (Krammer: rare and
  unsubstantiated). Script says so.
- **New York bombed:** only a 1944 newspaper headline; told as a headline, not as fact about all prisoners.

## Added from the Chicago episode
10. **Reinhold Pabel, *Enemies Are Human* (1955)**, checked by full-text search for `../chicago-lights` (see its sources.md):
    landed at Norfolk on 2 Jan. 1944; "En route through Virginia and Kentucky we pressed our noses against the windowpanes to
    take in the sights. The first impression we had was the abundance of automobiles everywhere"; "the numerous frame houses,
    especially in Kentucky"; the porter "politely offered them to us as though we were human beings, most of us forgot ...
    those anti-American feelings"; escaped from the Washington, Illinois branch camp in September 1945; arrested in Chicago on
    9 March 1953.
