# The War Was Over. An American Guard Machine-Gunned Sleeping German POWs.

## 1. The waitress

On a Saturday night in July 1945, in a small farm town in Utah, an American soldier sat in a cafe on Main Street and had a cup of coffee. He was twenty-three years old, and slight. Before he left, he told the waitress that something exciting was going to happen tonight.

He walked a few blocks to the east end of Main Street, to a camp behind barbed wire, where about two hundred and fifty German prisoners of war were asleep in tents. The war in Europe had been over for exactly two months.

At midnight he reported for guard duty. He climbed a guard tower. There was a machine gun up there. It was not loaded. He loaded it.

This is the documented story of what happened at Salina, Utah, of the fifteen seconds that killed nine men who were waiting to go home, and of the American who pulled the trigger, and was never tried for it.

## 2. Salina

Salina was a farm town in the middle of Utah, in sugar beet country. With so many men away at the war, the beets still had to be thinned and harvested. The farmers needed hands.

The Army had the hands. More than four hundred thousand prisoners of war were held in America, and many of them worked on farms. Utah alone held thousands of them, Germans and Italians, and many of the Germans had been captured with the Afrika Korps.

On the eighth of June, 1945, a new branch camp opened in Salina, in an old Civilian Conservation Corps camp at the end of Main Street. It had forty-three tents with wooden floors, about six men to a tent, a high barbed wire fence, and three guard towers. It was strange for a prison camp to sit inside a town. Children lived a few hundred feet from the wire.

The prisoners were sent out to the beet fields every day. A prisoner was paid eighty cents a day in camp coupons. Germany had surrendered a month before they arrived. They were waiting for a ship.

## 3. Private Bertucci

The men who guarded them were the men the Army could spare. They were soldiers judged unfit for the front, because of their health, or because of their records.

Clarence Bertucci was born in New Orleans in 1921. He had seven brothers and six sisters. He left school after sixth grade and joined the Army in 1940. Two of his brothers were serving overseas, one of them in Germany.

He spent eight months in England with an artillery unit, and never saw combat. After five years in the Army he was still a private. He had gone absent without leave, he had refused guard duty, he had missed a train. He had been in the hospital twelve times.

People in Salina said later that he talked about getting his Germans someday. Whether he really said it cannot be proved. The Army's own record of his words came afterwards, and it was worse.

## 4. Fifteen seconds

July seventh was a Saturday. The prisoners spent the whole day in the beet fields, came back, ate dinner, and went to their tents. By midnight the camp was asleep.

The machine guns in the towers were kept empty, with the ammunition in boxes beside them. Bertucci took a belt of two hundred and fifty rounds, fed it into the gun, and turned it on the tents.

Shortly after midnight, he pulled the trigger. He fired three bursts, moving the gun from left to right, and back. It took about fifteen seconds. The bullets went through the canvas, through the beds, and through the wooden floors. Thirty of the forty-three tents were hit. In one tent alone, four men died.

The camp commander, Lieutenant Albert Cornell, was lying on a cot at the foot of the same tower. He ran outside and saw no escape, no fight, no reason at all. He shouted at the guard to stop. The belt was already empty. Up in the tower, Bertucci was calling for more ammunition. He said he was not through yet.

A corporal climbed the tower and brought him down. He did not resist.

## 5. The town wakes up

The noise woke nearly everyone in Salina. Many people thought the Germans were breaking out. A boy of six, who lived three hundred feet away, went out on the front porch with his family. They could hear the cries of the prisoners.

The town's doctor, Rae Noyes, reached the camp and called it a horrible mess. Guards and prisoners were already working on the wounded together. The men were carried to the little Salina hospital on their cots.

It filled up at once. The rooms, the hall, and the waiting room were full, and the rest were treated outside. Nurses and townspeople helped. One man, whose body had been almost cut in half, lived until six in the morning. The dead were laid out on the west lawn of the hospital.

Six men had died in the camp. Two more died in Salina. The others were taken to Army hospitals in the north. One was expected to lose a foot. Another had been hit in the eye.

These men had survived the war. They died in a beet town in Utah, two months after the peace.

## 6. Monday morning

The Army sent a board of officers from Ogden, under Colonel Arthur Ericsson. The prisoners' spokesman, a sergeant named Hans Fertig, testified before it. He told them the guards had never threatened anyone, and had never fired a shot before. But the machine guns, he said, had always made the men afraid that something like this might happen.

Fertig asked for one thing. He asked that the prisoners be given two days off.

The colonel told him it was highly desirable that the men do their work as usual. On Monday morning, with their friends dead or in the hospital, the prisoners went back to the beet fields.

They questioned Bertucci at length. He showed no remorse. He said he had often been tempted to turn the gun on them, and that he was not at all sorry. Pressed for a reason, he gave only one. He just did not like Germans.

Time magazine put it this way. He had hated Germans, so he had killed Germans.

## 7. Fort Leavenworth

The front page that reported those words on July tenth carried another story, right beside them.

That morning, at Fort Leavenworth in Kansas, the United States Army hanged five German prisoners of war. They had murdered a fellow prisoner in a camp in Oklahoma. It was the first time enemy prisoners had been executed in America. In the Ogden paper that day, the five hanged Germans and the American guard ran side by side.

The Salt Lake Tribune called the shooting not only deplorable but damnable. It said Bertucci had disgraced the army. But some telegrams reached the Army that summer in support of him.

## 8. The funeral

On Thursday, July twelfth, the eight dead men were buried at Fort Douglas, in Salt Lake City. They wore American khaki. An American chaplain read the service, and a firing squad of American soldiers gave them a military salute, as the Geneva Convention required.

The public was not allowed. About fifteen men from the Salina camp were brought north under guard to see their friends buried. A choir of German prisoners from Ogden sang the old soldiers' song, The Good Comrade. Then a bugler played Taps.

The next day, in the hospital at Kearns, the ninth man died. Friedrich Ritter was forty-eight years old, and married. He was buried beside the others.

The rest of the dead were young, most of them in their twenties. Two more had wives waiting. One history says their families were not told until 1948.

## 9. Not a trial

An Army captain from the Ogden camp, Wayne Owens, was given the case. He looked at the evidence and concluded that Bertucci was sane, and that this was a calculated act. He recommended a court-martial.

His superiors told him he had no authority to decide that. Bertucci told officers he had planned it in advance. He had told a waitress. He had waited for the other guards to go to bed. He had loaded the gun himself.

Doctors at Bushnell Hospital in Brigham City observed him for several weeks. In August 1945, they found him mentally unbalanced. There was no court-martial, then or ever. He was sent to an Army mental hospital on Long Island.

He spent much of the rest of his life in mental hospitals, and died in 1969. Nobody was ever tried for the deaths at Salina.

## 10. What came after

The wounded recovered and were sent back to Germany. A later agreement between the two countries meant they could not claim compensation from the United States. Their only help would be a German veteran's pension.

The camp emptied by the end of 1945. The next spring, German prisoners were back in the fields around Salina. Later, farm workers lived on the site. Decades on, Salina's own mayor did not learn the story until after he was elected.

In 1977, a man named Dieter Lampe came back to Salina. He had been shot in the arm that night. He said he had received excellent care, and that the war had left him strongly opposed to war.

In 1988, the German Air Force paid to restore the memorial statue at Fort Douglas. Two men wounded in 1945 stood in the cemetery for the ceremony. In 2016, the family of a farmer who had hired prisoners opened a small museum on the site. They keep tent pegs there, and a jewelry box a prisoner made out of matchsticks.

## 11. What is left

In the back corner of the cemetery at Fort Douglas, the nine men lie where the Army put them in 1945, a long way from the sea they were waiting to cross. They had outlived the war. They could not outlive one man who did not like Germans.
