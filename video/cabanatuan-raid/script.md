# The True Story of the Raid at Cabanatuan — the 121 Rangers Who Freed the Survivors of Bataan

## 1. The plane over the camp

Luzon, the Philippines. The thirtieth of January, 1945, twenty minutes to seven in the evening. A twin-engined American night fighter, a P-61 Black Widow, comes in low over a prison camp near the town of Cabanatuan. It crosses the camp twice at about two hundred feet. On the ground, every Japanese guard is looking up.

Nobody is looking at the field in front of the gate. Out there, in the last of the light, more than a hundred American soldiers are lying flat in the grass. They are Rangers, and they have been crawling for over an hour across a mile of open rice land with almost no cover. One guard in a tower raises his rifle toward the ditch where they lie. Then he lowers it.

Inside the wire are more than five hundred prisoners of war. Many of them have been prisoners since Bataan and Corregidor fell in 1942. Most are sick. Some cannot walk. At a quarter to eight, a single rifle shot cracks through the dark, and the camp erupts. Two weeks later, one of those prisoners, his name not recorded on the film, told an Army camera what that moment was like.

[CLIP pow_interviews_leyte_a.mp4 102.3 123.5] Shortly after dark, I decided to turn in my bamboo bunk. I went in the barracks and sat on the ledge of the bunk and started to take off my shoes. Suddenly a lot of shooting started. I thought the end had came. I was under the impression that it was a massacre.

This is the documented story of the raid at Cabanatuan, the night a hundred and twenty Rangers of the Sixth Ranger Battalion, a dozen Alamo Scouts and hundreds of Filipino guerrillas went twenty-five miles behind Japanese lines to bring out the last survivors of Bataan. You will hear it from the Army's own reports, the newspapers of February 1945, and the freed men themselves.

## 2. The camp

To understand that night, you have to start almost three years earlier. In April 1942 the American and Filipino army on Bataan surrendered, and in May the fortress of Corregidor followed. The men who survived the march out of Bataan were packed into Camp O'Donnell. From there, the Americans were moved to a camp near Cabanatuan, about five miles east of the town, at a place called Pangatian.

It was a grim place. The Marine Corps official history says that at Cabanatuan, Americans died at a rate of forty to fifty a day. It records one date on purpose: the sixteenth of January, 1943, the first day in the history of the camp without a death. By December 1943, more than two thousand six hundred Americans were buried in the camp's cemetery.

The men lived on rice. A freed prisoner told the Associated Press in 1945 that they had been given three handfuls a day. One of them, a veterinarian named Herbert Ott, later said they were down to about eight hundred calories a day, and still had to work. It took twelve hundred just to stay alive, he said, so a lot of men simply worked themselves to death.

The barracks were long huts with thatched roofs, built for forty men. The Japanese put about a hundred and twenty in each one. At its peak, the camp held some eight thousand prisoners.

## 3. The ones left behind

In October 1944, about sixteen hundred prisoners were marched out of Cabanatuan. The Japanese were shipping able-bodied prisoners to Japan to work, in the holds of cargo ships the men called hell ships. In December, more than sixteen hundred prisoners were loaded aboard one of them, the Oryoku Maru, in Manila harbor.

Those left behind at Cabanatuan were the ones the Japanese did not want: the sick, the blind, the crippled, men too weak to work. There were a little over five hundred of them. Most were Americans, survivors of Bataan and Corregidor. There were also British prisoners from Singapore, a few Dutch and Norwegians, and some civilians.

They kept themselves alive in small ways. An Associated Press reporter wrote that they had collected six thousand recipes in captivity. And they had built a radio inside a water canteen that could pick up San Francisco.

Early in January 1945, the regular guard company left the camp. For a few days the prisoners were nearly alone, with a senior American officer, Colonel James Duckworth, in charge. Then Japanese troops came back. Some of them were soldiers passing through on their way north. The prisoners could hear the war getting closer. They did not know whether it would free them or kill them.

## 4. The fear

On the ninth of January, 1945, General Walter Krueger's Sixth Army landed at Lingayen Gulf on the west coast of Luzon, the same beaches the Japanese had used in December 1941. General MacArthur had come back. Within three weeks the American front line had pushed inland to the town of Guimba, about twenty-five miles from the prison camp.

That was the danger. When the Distinguished Service Cross was awarded to the raid's commander three weeks later, the citation spelled out why the mission had been ordered: it was believed that the enemy would kill or remove the prisoners when the American attack reached that area.

There was reason to believe it. A month earlier, on the island of Palawan, Japanese guards had herded a hundred and fifty American prisoners into air raid shelters and set them on fire. Eleven escaped. A Special Operations history of the raid records that their accounts were quickly passed to MacArthur's headquarters. Whether Palawan was the reason for the Cabanatuan raid, the sources do not agree. But no one at Sixth Army could be sure the prisoners would still be alive when the front line arrived.

On the twenty-sixth of January, an American who led guerrillas behind Japanese lines on Luzon, Major Robert Lapham, came to Sixth Army. His Filipino runners had been watching the camp, and he proposed a rescue. On the recommendation of Krueger's intelligence chief, Colonel Horton White, Krueger gave the job to the Sixth Ranger Battalion.

## 5. The Rangers

The Sixth Ranger Battalion had begun life as an artillery battalion, the Ninety-Eighth Field Artillery, activated at Fort Lewis, Washington, in January 1941. In April 1944, in New Guinea, it got a new commander, Lieutenant Colonel Henry Mucci of Bridgeport, Connecticut, a West Point graduate of 1936. He turned the gunners into Rangers.

In September 1944 the unit became the Sixth Ranger Infantry Battalion. On the seventeenth of October, three days before MacArthur's landing on Leyte, the Rangers went ashore on the small islands guarding Leyte Gulf.

For the camp, Mucci chose Company C, under a twenty-five-year-old captain from Seattle, Robert Prince, a Stanford graduate. He added the second platoon of Company F, under First Lieutenant John Murphy. With a few others and four Signal Corps combat photographers, the force came to eight officers and a hundred and twenty enlisted men. The newspapers would call them the hundred and twenty-one Rangers.

They went in soft caps and fatigues, with no insignia or badges of rank. Mucci himself took only a forty-five pistol. Years later, Prince said they all felt privileged to be chosen.

## 6. The Alamo Scouts

Ahead of the Rangers went two teams of Alamo Scouts, Krueger's own reconnaissance men. They were small teams trained to slip behind Japanese lines, watch, and come back. The Army says they went through the whole war without a single man killed or captured.

The two teams were led by First Lieutenant William Nellist and First Lieutenant Thomas Rounsaville, a young officer from Oklahoma. They left the American lines on the evening of the twenty-seventh of January, a day ahead of the Rangers, to find the camp and count the guards.

Nellist found a way to watch the gate up close. He and one of his scouts, Private Rufo Vaquilar, a Filipino, put on local farmers' clothes and moved into an empty grass hut in a field facing the camp, a few hundred yards from the main gate. From there they could see the guards and the towers, and sketch them. On the afternoon of the raid, Nellist's sketch reached Mucci, with an aerial photograph of the camp.

## 7. Behind the lines

The Rangers left their base camp by truck at five in the morning on the twenty-eighth of January. At Guimba, on the edge of the American lines, they got down and started walking at two in the afternoon. At the village of Lobong they were joined by Filipino guerrillas under Captain Eduardo Joson, about eighty men.

After dark they crossed into Japanese-held country. Late that night they had to lie hidden while Japanese trucks and tanks went by. At midnight they waded the Talavera River. At four in the morning they crossed the Rizal highway, the main road north from Cabanatuan.

At six in the morning of the twenty-ninth they reached the village of Balincarin, after a fourteen-mile night march without being seen. There they met the Alamo Scouts and the local guerrilla commander, Captain Juan Pajota. Pajota had about ninety armed men and a hundred and sixty unarmed ones. He also brought news that changed the plan.

## 8. Pajota

Juan Pajota was from Nueva Ecija, the province around the camp. He had trained with the Philippine Scouts and joined the guerrillas during the retreat to Bataan. His men knew the trails and the villages around the camp. And what he told Mucci was alarming.

About a mile north of the camp, two to three hundred Japanese soldiers were camped along the Cabu Creek. Later reports put the force at Cabu at about eight hundred men, with tanks. And in Cabanatuan City, less than four miles to the south, there was at least one Japanese division.

Mucci made his decision. He postponed the raid for twenty-four hours. The extra day let the scouts finish their work.

Mucci asked the guerrillas to gather a train of carabao carts, Filipino farm carts pulled by water buffalo, big enough to carry two hundred freed prisoners, and to cook food for six hundred and fifty men. He asked the villagers to pen up their chickens and to tie and muzzle their dogs, so no barking would give the column away. Captain Prince later said the Filipinos took care of at least five hundred dogs.

That afternoon the Rangers moved to the village of Platero, closer to the camp, where the people welcomed them with a choir and a big meal.

## 9. The plan

By the morning of the thirtieth, the scouts had drawn the camp in detail. It measured six hundred by eight hundred yards. The main gate, on the highway, was eight feet high and held by a heavy lock. There were three guard towers, twelve feet tall, and a pillbox with four men. The prisoners were kept in the northwest corner.

There were seventy-three Japanese guards. But at eleven that morning, about a hundred and fifty more troops had marched into the compound to rest for the night.

Captain Prince drew up the assault. His company would attack the main gate from the front. Lieutenant Murphy's platoon would circle around to the rear of the camp. Murphy's first shot would be the signal. When the prisoners were out, Prince would fire a red flare, and everyone would pull back to the Pampanga River.

The guerrillas would hold the road on both sides. Captain Joson would block it eight hundred yards to the southwest, with a Ranger bazooka team. Captain Pajota would hold the bridge over the Cabu Creek, three hundred yards to the northeast, between the camp and the Japanese battalion. Under the far end of that bridge his men had placed a time bomb, which had come in by American submarine. It was set to go off between seven-forty and seven-fifty.

## 10. The idea

There was one problem left: the open field. For more than a mile in front of the gate there was nothing but flat ground and short grass. The Rangers would have to cross it in the last light, and every guard tower looked straight at it.

The answer came from Pajota. According to the Army's study, it was on his recommendation that an American plane would fly over the camp just before the attack, to pull every eye in the camp up into the sky. The Special Operations history gives part of the credit to a Sixth Army planner, Major Frank Rowale. The night fighters of the Five Hundred and Forty-Seventh Night Fighter Squadron were briefed directly by Colonel White.

Prince had his doubts. He later said he did not think the aerial decoy would work, and later still called the plane one of the biggest factors in keeping the surprise.

At five in the afternoon of the thirtieth of January, the whole force left Platero. With the guerrillas it numbered nearly three hundred and seventy-five men. Mucci's last order is quoted from his own article of 1945: all of the prisoners go, no one is left behind.

## 11. The crawl

At six o'clock, in the twilight, the Rangers reached the edge of the open plain. Prince's two platoons went down on their bellies. The camp was a mile away, and they would have to crawl for seventy-five minutes to reach it.

At twenty to seven the plane arrived. It was a P-61 Black Widow named Hard to Get, flown by Captain Kenneth Schrieber with Lieutenant Bonnie Rucks as radar operator. It came over the bridge and the camp twice, very low. One account says Schrieber cut one engine over the camp and started it again so that it backfired like a gun. Prince remembered him cutting his motor and doing every crazy thing he could to attract attention. Lieutenant Nellist, watching from his hut, said the plane just about ripped the shingles off the camp.

It worked. The guards watched the plane. Murphy's platoon slipped under the highway through a large culvert and worked around to the rear. Prince's men crawled up into a ditch about twenty yards from the main gate. At twenty-five past seven, the last of Murphy's men were in position.

The plan said seven-thirty. Murphy took fifteen more minutes to check his men. The moon was up, and it was bright. The Rangers could see the red tips of the guards' cigarettes, and men walking around in their underwear.

## 12. Seven forty-five

At a quarter to eight, Lieutenant Murphy aimed his M-1 rifle at an open window in the nearest barracks and fired. The Army's study puts it in one line: his shot was the signal.

Every Ranger opened fire at once. According to the Army's study, all the guard towers, guard shacks and pillboxes were silenced within thirty seconds. At the main gate, Staff Sergeant Theodore Richardson of Dallas shot the heavy lock off with his pistol. In the War Department's account, the pistol was then shot out of his hand. He said a Japanese soldier asked him, in perfectly good English, what was going on.

The Rangers poured in. A bazooka section under Staff Sergeant Manton Stewart blew up two trucks and a metal shed where the Japanese kept tanks. Private First Class Leland Provencher reached the first prisoner, a man who ran the camp's generator.

Inside the prisoners' huts there was panic. Some were afraid it was a Japanese trick. A Navy warrant officer, Paul Jackson, wrote two months later that when the firing started, most of them thought the Japanese were coming to kill them. A marine from Corregidor, Corporal Milton Englin, said he thought the rescuers were guerrillas, until some big Texan told him to head for the main gate. The prisoner who had been taking off his shoes had another problem.

[CLIP pow_interviews_leyte_a.mp4 164.6 177.1] I can't see very well at night. In fact, I can't see at all when it's dark. And one of the Rangers grabbed a hold of my arm and he led me through the camp.

Prince said they found two hundred and twenty-five men who had to be carried. Some Rangers took off their socks and their shoes and gave them to barefoot prisoners. A Ranger sergeant said the prisoners who could walk wanted to carry the Rangers' rifles. And Mucci wrote about one old man who told him: I made the death march from Bataan, and I can certainly make this one.

The British prisoners had their own answer. Mucci heard them shout: we're not Americans, but we're coming too.

## 13. The bridge

At the same moment, three hundred yards up the road, Pajota's fight began. When the first shots came from the camp, his men opened fire on the Japanese battalion camped beyond the Cabu Creek. The time bomb went off and blew a gap in the bridge.

Pajota had kept a secret from Mucci. Besides the men he had shown the Rangers, he had quietly sent about four hundred more guerrillas ahead, with four water-cooled machine guns. Now they were all on the creek bank, firing into the Japanese as they tried to cross.

Ranger bazooka men posted with the guerrillas knocked out two tanks and a truck. Pajota's men held the bridge until a little after ten that night. The Army's study says his guerrillas virtually destroyed a Japanese battalion.

## 14. The red flare

About twelve minutes into the attack, the shooting in the camp was down to scattered shots. Then a Japanese light mortar fired three rounds toward the main gate, where the Rangers were gathering the prisoners. Six men were wounded.

Two of them were officers. One was the Alamo Scout Lieutenant Tom Rounsaville. The other was the battalion surgeon, Captain James Fisher. Fisher was a Harvard-trained doctor, the son of the American author Dorothy Canfield Fisher. The Rangers called him Doctor Jimmy. He had argued to go forward with the assault company instead of staying at the rear. Now he lay at the gate, bleeding badly from wounds to the liver.

At a quarter past eight, Captain Prince fired the red flare. The withdrawal began. As the last Rangers pulled back, Corporal Roy Sweezy turned to fire at the Japanese and was shot in the chest. He died a few minutes later. The accounts disagree about who fired that shot. He and Captain Fisher would be the only Rangers to die in the operation.

One prisoner died of a heart attack as he was being helped out of the camp. And one was left behind. In the confusion, a sick British prisoner had fallen asleep in the latrine. He woke up to an empty camp. Filipino guerrillas found him later that night, or the next morning; the accounts differ on that too, and in one telling, he took the time to shave and put on his best clothes before walking out.

## 15. The carts

By half past eight most of the column had reached the Pampanga River, and by a quarter to nine everyone was across. A second flare told the guerrillas the Rangers were clear. At Platero, a Filipino doctor, Carlos Layug, and his wife, also a doctor, had set up an aid station in an empty schoolhouse.

Then came the carts. About a hundred and fifteen men went on to Balincarin in twenty-five carabao carts. There fifteen more carts were waiting. At two in the morning, at another village, eleven more. When the column left there at half past two, it had fifty-one carts and was a mile and a half long. By the time it reached the town of Sibul in the morning, there were seventy-one carts, three times what Mucci had planned.

The most dangerous part was the Rizal highway. The column had to march along it for a mile, in the open, while guerrilla roadblocks watched both directions for Japanese traffic. None came. The last man was across by half past four in the morning.

The Rangers walked beside the carts the whole night. The Navy warrant officer Paul Jackson had lost a leg in the camp. He wrote that after half a mile on his homemade peg he had to give up and let his rescuers carry him. And he wrote that the Rangers pushed the carabao carts through the night, and never a complaint.

## 16. Doctor Jimmy

Captain Fisher could not be moved with the column. At Balincarin, Dr. Layug and one of the freed prisoners, a surgeon named Merle Musselman, operated on him. When he needed blood, an Alamo Scout gave it to him, straight from one man's vein to the other's. The scouts cleared a rough landing strip in a field and a light plane was requested to fly him out.

The plane never came. James Fisher died on the thirty-first of January, about fifteen hours after the raid began. Filipino guerrillas buried him near Platero. One account gives his last words as: good luck on the way out.

His body was later moved to the Manila American Cemetery, where he lies today.

## 17. The flag

At Sibul, at eight in the morning, the Rangers finally got through to Sixth Army by radio. Around eleven, a Ranger at the head of the column, Patrick Marquis, met an American patrol. Trucks and ambulances came up. An hour later the freed prisoners were at the Ninety-Second Evacuation Hospital at Guimba.

Staff Sergeant Clinton Goodbla, a coast artilleryman captured on Corregidor, told an Associated Press reporter what it was like to walk through the lines that morning. It cut right through me, he said. The flag, it cut right through me. Being free cut right through me. A corporal from the same camp, Cecil Hay, told a newsreel camera the same thing in his own words.

[CLIP cabanatuan_newsreel_1945.mp4 360.3 380.6] You people do not realize how happy us boys were when we were liberated. I didn't realize myself until we... saw the flag that was flying, and then I had to break down and cry.

General Krueger came to see them. He wrote later that most of them were in pitiable condition and could not realize they were actually safe. Some had lost as much as a hundred pounds. A British signalman captured at Singapore, Thomas Potter, tasted white bread again. Blimey, he told a reporter, this is better than I got in peacetime.

## 18. The newspapers

On the first of February, General MacArthur came to the hospital at Guimba and greeted the men of Bataan and Corregidor. In a special communiqué he said: no incident of the campaign has given me such personal satisfaction. He awarded the commander the Distinguished Service Cross, every other officer the Silver Star, and every enlisted man the Bronze Star.

The story hit the front pages of America on the first and second of February. The Associated Press wrote that men of Bataan, Corregidor and Singapore, five hundred and thirteen of them, had been snatched from the Japanese in an exploit of unmatched daring. Another reporter wrote of a long, dirty, twisting lane that should become a war monument, because it had bridged two worlds.

Not everything in those first reports was right. The first Associated Press story said twenty-seven Rangers and Filipinos had died. The Rangers' own log, written on the thirty-first of January, says: mission completed. Casualties: Captain Fisher and Corporal Sweezy killed in action. Five hundred and ten prisoners released.

Even the count of the freed men never settled: five hundred and ten in the log, five hundred and eleven in the Army's study, five hundred and sixteen in a later count from the rosters. What is not in doubt is the cost: two Rangers dead, and the Japanese, by the Army's estimate, more than five hundred killed or wounded.

## 19. The voices

Within two weeks, Army and newsreel cameramen were filming the freed men. A Universal newsreel of February 1945 carried their words to theaters across America. This is Sergeant Samuel Goldie.

[CLIP cabanatuan_newsreel_1945.mp4 407.7 430.3] Well, we're finally free Americans. It took them three years, but we're sure glad to get out of there. The Rangers came in that night, and it was about as happy as I had ever seen in my life. For three years, we couldn't do anything without someone telling us what to do. And now, at last, we can do what we want when we want to. We're sure glad to get back to the United States, where I'll be a free American again, and get myself a milkshake.

## 20. Washington

On the eleventh of February, about two hundred and eighty of the freed prisoners sailed from Leyte for home. They reached San Francisco Bay on the eighth of March. The day before, a dozen of the raiders had been taken into the Oval Office to meet President Roosevelt.

Captain Prince and eleven other raiders toured American war plants that spring to tell the workers what they had done. The War Department printed what they said. Prince told them the Filipinos were wonderful people: they fed us, fought with us and died for us. And he said that when he saw the camp in daylight three days after the raid, it looked much bigger. If he had seen it before, he said, he would not have had as much nerve as he did that night.

Sixth Army's intelligence report called the raid an almost perfect example of prior reconnaissance and planning. An Army historian later called it perhaps the best conceived and executed Ranger mission of the Second World War.

## 21. What became of them

Henry Mucci was promoted to colonel and given an infantry regiment. He ran for Congress in Connecticut in 1946 and lost, left the Army in 1947, and later worked in the oil business in India. He died in Florida in 1997, at eighty-eight, and is buried at West Point.

Robert Prince left the Army as a major in 1946 and spent some forty years in the apple business in Washington State. His son Steve was killed in Vietnam in 1969. Prince died on New Year's Day, 2009, at Port Townsend, Washington.

Tom Rounsaville recovered from his wound, fought in Korea and rose to colonel. He died in 1999 and is buried at Fort Benning. The Alamo Scout Galen Kittleson took part in four prisoner rescue operations in two wars.

Eduardo Joson became governor of Nueva Ecija, the province of the raid, and served six terms. A hospital in Cabanatuan bears his name. Juan Pajota moved to the United States. He died of a heart attack in Chicago in the 1970s, days before he was to become an American citizen.

Roy Sweezy and James Fisher lie in the Manila American Cemetery, among the men of the war in the Pacific. The Filipino guerrillas who fought at the Cabu bridge received the Bronze Star, like the American enlisted men. Their losses that night were never firmly counted.

## 23. The field today

The camp is gone. Pacific Wrecks reports that the only physical remains are six concrete foundation blocks from the camp's water tower. On the site stands the Cabanatuan American Memorial, built by the survivors of Bataan and the prison camp and dedicated in 1982. Its wall carries the names of two thousand six hundred and fifty-six Americans who died there.

On the first of February, 2025, eighty years after the raid, American and Filipino soldiers stood together at that wall. Among them were Rangers of the Seventy-Fifth Ranger Regiment, who carry the lineage of the men who crawled across that field. Around them, the rice land is still flat and open, the way it was on the night a plane flew low over the camp, and every guard looked up.
