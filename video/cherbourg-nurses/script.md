# The Army Captured 16 German Nurses. Then It Drove Them Back to the German Lines.

<!-- One paragraph = one scene. Lines starting with "##" are act markers and are not narrated. Write numbers as spoken words; years as digits are converted by tools/tts_*.py. Highlighted document sentences are read verbatim. -->

## 1. The road to Caumont

On the second of July, 1944, closed American ambulances drove through the Normandy countryside toward the front line. Inside them were German women. They were prisoners of the United States Army. Before the evening was over, they would be back on the German side.

A short cease-fire had been arranged over the radio. One of the Americans who arranged it was a young captain named Quentin Roosevelt, the grandson of a president. At about six in the evening, the women were taken through the lines at a village called Caumont.

Online, there are many stories about German nurses in American hands, most of them with conversations nobody wrote down. This is the documented story of the first German nurses the American army captured in France, and of the rule that made the army give them back.

## 2. The fall of Cherbourg

After D-Day, the Allies needed a deep-water port. The nearest was Cherbourg, at the tip of the Cotentin peninsula. American troops attacked the city on the twenty-second of June. Four days later they took Fort du Roule, above the harbor, and captured the German commander, General Karl-Wilhelm von Schlieben.

The arsenal held out three more days. It surrendered on the twenty-ninth of June after two American officers bluffed its commander, making their force sound far bigger than it was. Before they gave up, the Germans had wrecked and mined the port. It would be weeks before the first ships could use it.

Tens of thousands of Germans became prisoners in the fight for the peninsula. And in an underground hospital near Cherbourg, the Americans found prisoners of a kind they had not taken before in France. An Army photographer's caption called them the first German Army nurses to fall into American hands.

Some of them were nurses of the German Red Cross. Others were auxiliaries of the German navy. The Allied papers called all of them nurses. Some called them Nazi nurses.

## 3. The rule

The question was what to do with them. The answer was in a treaty signed in Geneva in July 1929, a convention on the wounded and sick of armies in the field. The United States had signed it. It protected the wounded, and the people whose job was to care for them.

Its ninth article covered medical staff who were captured. If they fall into the hands of the enemy they shall not be treated as prisoners of war.

Its twelfth article said they could not be detained, and what had to happen next. They shall be sent back to the belligerent to whose service they are attached as soon as a way is open for their return and military exigencies permit.

Until then, the treaty said, they should keep working, caring for the wounded of their own side. Captured medical staff could be kept as long as prisoners needed their care. When that need ended, they were to be sent home. Red Cross nurses were covered too.

## 4. The hospital

On the second of July, nine of the women were brought to the forty-fifth Evacuation Hospital, near the village of La Cambe, in Normandy. They did not know what was going to happen to them. Only after they arrived were they told that they were going back. According to the hospital's account, they were overjoyed.

Before they left, the hospital's commanding officer walked them through it. They saw its supplies and its equipment. They talked to German patients and prisoners who were being treated there. What they wanted to know most was how wounded Germans were being cared for in England and in France.

The American staff had a hope of their own. They hoped the women would remember what they had seen, and treat American wounded in German hands with the same kindness.

## 5. The truce

Then they waited, while a cease-fire was negotiated with the enemy. Closed ambulances took them to Balleroy, a village close to the front. There they waited about two hours more, while the last arrangements were made with German officers on the other side.

The cease-fire was agreed over the radio. The account names three officers. Captain Fred Ghercke commanded an American team that interrogated prisoners. Captain Quentin Roosevelt served with the First Infantry Division. Major Heeren commanded the reconnaissance battalion of the German second Panzer Division.

An Army photographer was at Balleroy that day. One of his pictures shows an American ambulance parked in front of a house, four of the women, and a group of American soldiers around them. At about six in the evening, the women were taken through the lines at Caumont.

## 6. The captain

Quentin Roosevelt was twenty-four years old. He was the grandson of President Theodore Roosevelt, and he was named after an uncle who had been killed in the First World War. On D-Day he had landed at Omaha Beach with the first wave.

His father, General Theodore Roosevelt Junior, had come ashore at Utah Beach that same morning. Ten days after the truce, on the twelfth of July, the general died of a heart attack in Normandy. Father and son had spent part of that day in a long conversation.

Three days later, an Army photographer took a picture of Quentin marching in his father's funeral procession. The general was later awarded the Medal of Honor for what he did on Utah Beach. Quentin survived the war. He died in a plane crash near Hong Kong in 1948. He was twenty-nine.

## 7. The second road

A week after the first crossing, it happened again. On the ninth of July, more women were driven down the same road between Balleroy and Caumont and handed back. Sixteen German nurses and navy auxiliaries were returned in the two crossings.

An Army photo dated the eighth of July shows seven of them sitting on a bench, waiting, with an American military policeman behind them holding a rifle.

The story reached the papers. Yank, the Army's own magazine, told it at the end of July, and an Associated Press correspondent wrote about it, according to the hospital's history. But the Army's photographs went through the censor first. The caption on one of them was only cleared for publication without any restriction in May 1945, after Germany had surrendered.

## 8. What is left

What the sixteen women told anyone about the American hospital, once they were home, we have not found. The only record of that day is the American one, and the hope written into it.

The rule they were sent home under was not a favor. It was in the treaty. In a war that broke almost every rule, on two summer evenings in Normandy, this one held.

## 9. Caumont

Caumont was liberated by the First Infantry Division on the thirteenth of June, 1944. Today a memorial in the village marks it, and a plaque on the church remembers the division that Quentin Roosevelt served in. The road from Balleroy is a quiet country road. Twice in July 1944, the war stopped on it long enough for sixteen women to cross.
