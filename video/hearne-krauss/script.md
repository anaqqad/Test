# A New Yorker Came Home as a German POW. His Own Comrades Killed Him.

## 1. The visit

In 1943, a family from New York City made the long trip south to a prison camp in the cotton country of Texas. They had come to see their son. He was a corporal in the German army.

They brought him presents. A sewing machine, because he was a tailor. And a radio. In the barracks around him lived thousands of men who had fought for Hitler in North Africa. Most of them did not like what he did with that radio.

On the night of December seventeenth, 1943, a little before midnight, men with clubs walked into his barracks. The attack lasted less than a minute. Nobody in the room got up to help him.

This is the documented story of Hugo Krauss, the boy from New York who came back to America as a German prisoner, of the men who killed him for it, and of a stranger on a Texas road who made one of them confess.

## 2. Yorkville

Hugo Krauss was born in Germany, in 1919. When he was nine, his family crossed the Atlantic and settled in Manhattan, in Yorkville, the German neighborhood of New York. He went to school there. He grew up speaking English.

In 1939, when he was nineteen, he sailed back to Germany. A pro-Nazi group in America, the German American Bund, helped pay for the trip. One history says he went as a Nazi supporter, another says he went to visit relatives. Why he went, and what he believed then, is not clear.

Then the war began. The German army drafted him. At first he got out of it by saying he was an American citizen. Later he joined anyway. He fought in Russia, and then in North Africa.

In the spring of 1943, the German army in Tunisia surrendered. Krauss was captured with it. Less than four years after he had left New York, he was crossing the Atlantic again, this time as a prisoner of war.

## 3. The Fritz Ritz

He was sent to a town called Hearne, in central Texas, sixty-five miles southeast of Waco, on the bottomland of the Brazos River. About three thousand five hundred people lived there, with two cotton gins and two railroad lines.

On the edge of town stood one of the largest prisoner of war camps in the United States. The first men from the Afrika Korps arrived in June 1943. At its peak, Camp Hearne held almost five thousand prisoners, in three compounds behind the wire.

Life there was not hard. The prisoners built theaters, and played in orchestras. They built small castles, waist high, with towers and moats. They picked cotton for local farmers, for wages. Some people in town called the camp the Fritz Ritz.

But inside the compounds, the hard Nazis kept order among their own men. Prisoners who doubted Hitler kept quiet. Those who did not could be beaten at night, by men from their own company.

## 4. The interpreter

Hugo Krauss did not keep quiet. He spoke perfect English, and the Americans made him an interpreter. He told the other prisoners that Germany was going to lose the war, and that he was not going back.

He said he would get leave at Christmas, to visit his parents in New York. He said he wanted to join the American army. When his company sang German songs after meals, he got up and left the room.

He had a sewing machine, and he made shirts and hats for other prisoners. And he had his radio. He listened to American news, and told the men around him that it was more accurate than the news from Germany.

A man in his barracks, another interpreter named Heino Erichsen, later remembered the visitors. Krauss had American relatives who came to see him in the camp, and some of the prisoners said a man like that had to be a spy.

The army later put it simply. Krauss was very much disliked, a report said, and considered a traitor, especially by the younger prisoners, who were devoted to Hitler.

## 5. The radio

By December 1943, the Nazis in his compound held two things against him. Some German sergeants had been organizing slowdowns in the work program, and about a week before the attack they were moved to another camp. The Nazis believed Krauss had told the Americans about them.

Then the guards found a secret shortwave radio, hidden by prisoners to listen to news from Germany. The Americans took it away. The prisoners blamed Krauss.

On December seventeenth, the ranking sergeant of his company, Anton Boehmer, stood in front of the men and announced that there was a man among them who was disloyal to the cause. Everyone understood who he meant.

Krauss heard what was being said. According to one prisoner, he answered loudly. Let them come, he said. I am not afraid.

## 6. The night

That evening, a group met in another barracks, by candlelight. They had sticks. Some of the sticks had long spikes driven through them. One man had a piece of pipe. Lookouts went out to watch the guard towers, and a man was chosen to turn off the lights.

At about a quarter to midnight, they walked into Barracks One. The lights went out. Krauss was in his bunk near the door.

Heino Erichsen was in the same barracks that night. He remembered the noise, the lights going out, the screaming, and how quickly it was over. The attack lasted less than a minute. Krauss cried out for it to stop. No one in the room came to help him.

His skull was broken in three places. Both of his arms were broken, and bones in his hand. He was taken to the camp hospital, and the next day to an army hospital in Temple, Texas. He died there on December twenty-third, 1943, a month short of his twenty-fifth birthday.

Years later, some of the American guards told much worse stories about that night. The historians who studied the medical report and the autopsy photographs found that those things did not happen. Rumors had grown in the retelling.

## 7. The silence

The Army investigated. Almost fifty prisoners were questioned. Nobody named anyone. In the dark, they said, they had seen nothing. Heino Erichsen asked to be transferred out of the camp the next day.

In New York, Krauss's father, Henry, wrote to the Army. He was a citizen of the United States, he wrote, and he wanted a full investigation, and every detail sent to him.

The answer came in 1944. His son's attitude and conduct, the Army wrote, had been praiseworthy from an American point of view, and that had made the other German prisoners his enemies. The men who attacked him remained unidentified.

In January 1945, the New York Times printed a short story about a former Yorkville man who had been killed as a prisoner of war. The war in Europe ended that spring. The case was still open.

## 8. The road

One of the attackers that night was a corporal named Guenther Meisel. He had been in the Hitler Youth. At Camp Hearne, he worked in the truck workshop, and the Army trusted him enough to let him drive a truck alone between the camps.

One afternoon, on one of those drives, he saw a young woman on the side of the road, with the hood of her car open. He stopped and fixed the engine. Before they drove off, he asked if he could write to her.

At first he told her he was a long-distance truck driver. In his first letter, he admitted he was a German prisoner. She wrote back anyway. She was a Christian, and she offered to send him a Bible.

He arranged to be sent out picking cotton, on a farm where she could come and see him. And one day, in the cotton field, he told her that he had helped kill a man.

She told him he had to confess. He fought it for several days. Then, near the end of 1945, he went to the American officer in charge of his labor camp and told him everything. The officer asked him if he understood what he was doing to himself. Meisel said he did.

## 9. The trial

Meisel named the others. In January 1946, seven German prisoners went on trial before an American military court at Camp Swift, near Bastrop, Texas, for the murder of Hugo Krauss.

The prosecutor told the court that two of the accused had helped him break the case, and that without Meisel, the full story might never have come out.

After more than two hours of deliberation, the court found all seven guilty. Each was sentenced to life in prison, at hard labor. They were sent to the federal penitentiary at Leavenworth, in Kansas.

Then the sentences began to shrink. A review board threw out two of the convictions. Meisel's sentence was cut to fifteen years, the others to ten. In 1948, President Truman cut Meisel's to ten as well.

In 1949, all five were paroled and sent home to Germany. They had spent about three years in prison. Fourteen German prisoners convicted in other camp murders had been hanged. The men who killed Hugo Krauss went home.

## 10. Aftermath

Hugo Krauss was buried in the small cemetery at Camp Hearne. After the war, his body was moved to the national cemetery at Fort Sam Houston, in San Antonio, to a section with other German prisoners of war.

Camp Hearne closed in 1946. The buildings were sold off, and the site sat empty for decades. Today there is a museum, a rebuilt barracks, and one of the little castles still standing in the grass.

There is also a trail called the Scene of the Crime. Every December, near the anniversary of that night, the museum holds a walk there. Its program director said it plainly. He was done with Nazi Germany.

## 11. What is left

In San Antonio, his stone stands in a row of German names, far from New York and far from Germany. One visitor who found it wrote that someone had left three pennies and a dime on top, for a man who had wanted to come home.
