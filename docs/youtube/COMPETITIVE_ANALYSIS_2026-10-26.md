# The Black History Room: YouTube Long-Form Competitive Analysis

Prepared 26 October 2026 for [@TheBlackHistoryRoom](https://www.youtube.com/@TheBlackHistoryRoom).

**How this was researched.** All channel and video numbers below were read directly from YouTube on 26 October 2026: 30 YouTube searches (each sorted by view count, most of them run twice, for the past year and for all time), the public pages of 33 channels (subscribers, total views, join date, latest 15 uploads, and most popular uploads), and your own channel's YouTube Studio analytics. The raw data is saved next to this report in `docs/youtube/data/` (574 search results, about 1,500 channel videos). Monetization figures come from third-party studies because YouTube does not publish RPM by niche; they are labelled as estimates.

**Labels used.** "Verified" means read from YouTube or Studio today. "Estimate" means calculated or taken from a third-party source. "Assumption" means my judgement.

**Limits.** Public pages do not show a competitor's click-through rate (CTR), retention, revenue or audience. View counts on YouTube are rounded (for example 1.2M). Some channels only expose their latest uploads, not a "Popular" sort, so their best videos came from search instead.

# Executive Summary

1. **Your channel is a 23-day-old Shorts channel with a strong start and no long-form library.** Verified: 903 subscribers, 32 uploads, 55.6K views, 592 watch hours, 93% of views from the Shorts feed, 99.3% new viewers. Your best Short (George Washington Carver, "As a Baby, He Was Traded for a Horse") has 13K views. You are not yet eligible for the YouTube Partner Program, because Shorts watch time does not count toward the 4,000 long-form watch hours.

2. **The niche is attractive and still under-supplied with quality.** Verified: in the past 12 months, small or new Black history channels have produced long-form videos with 5 to 54 times more views than they have subscribers. Unseen Frontlines (22K subscribers, launched September 2025) has a 1.2M-view video, "The Black Soldiers Who Humiliated The KKK". Black History Exposed (36K subscribers) has a 902K-view video. Demand is real; the supply is mostly rage-bait, unsourced, or AI-generated.

3. **Length matters, and the data favours 18-35 minutes.** Estimate from 572 recent videos on 19 Black history channels: the median video of 20-30 minutes got 0.43 views per subscriber, versus 0.09 for 12-20 minutes and 0.06 for 8-12 minutes. The 134 breakout hits in this set have a median length of 17.6 minutes, with half between 9.5 and 25 minutes.

4. **What wins is conflict with a payoff, told about a specific person or event.** The repeat winners are Black soldiers fighting back (against the Klan, in wars), stolen or hidden wealth, Black royalty and African kingdoms, survival under Jim Crow, and "what they didn't teach you". Pure biography of famous figures (Tubman, King, Parks) is saturated by PBS, History and Biography channels.

5. **Your edge is trust.** Your stack is already unusual: two sources per fact, real public-domain archival images with credits, a website with citations and a map, and a consistent narrator. Most fast-growing competitors use outrage titles, unsourced claims, or AI imagery. YouTube tightened its monetization rules against mass-produced, repetitive content in 2025, which makes a documented, original channel safer to monetize.

6. **Recommended strategy.** Publish two 20-25 minute single-story documentaries per week in a "true story with receipts" style, built around injustice, resistance and payoff. Keep one Short per day, each Short linked to a related long-form video. Add one 60-90 minute compilation per month made from earlier episodes. The first goal is 4,000 long-form watch hours for monetization; at a 20-minute video and about 7 minutes of average viewing, that is roughly 34,000 long-form views.

7. **Money, honestly.** Shorts pay very little: third-party data puts them at a few cents per 1,000 views. At an estimated $4-10 RPM for a US-heavy history audience, 1,000 dollars a month needs roughly 100,000-250,000 long-form views a month. That is realistic within 6-12 months if two documentaries a week hold quality. 5,000 dollars a month needs roughly 500,000-1.25M monthly long-form views, which requires several breakout videos and a library of 80-120 documentaries.

Washington and Roosevelt, 3.0K), a man photographed more than Lincoln (Douglass, 2.5K) and the first Black senator (Revels, 2.2K). All five state a concrete, surprising fact about a named person. That matches what the competitor data shows for long-form.
- **The middle is very consistent.** Twenty uploads sit between 1,000 and 1,700 views. That is a stable Shorts-feed floor, not yet a breakout pattern.
- **The bottom is distorted by timing, not topic.** The first four uploads (Smalls, Coleman, Henson, Boston) went out when the channel had no subscribers. Two later uploads (Major Taylor 82 views, Elizabeth Jennings 67 views) look suppressed; check in Studio whether they were held, age-restricted or uploaded with a problem before drawing conclusions.
- **Audience positioning (assumption, small sample).** The Studio age data is unusual: 71% of views are from people 55 or older and 72% are male. This is based on signed-in, declared ages and on Shorts viewers only, so it may shift with long-form. If it holds, it is good news: older US viewers watch long videos, increasingly on TV, and are valuable to advertisers. It argues for calm, authoritative narration, readable on-screen text and longer runtimes.
- **Content gap on your own channel.** There is no long-form video, no playlist, no search traffic and almost no returning viewers. Shorts viewers currently have nowhere to go next.

## Strongest existing patterns to carry into long-form

1. A named person plus a hard, surprising fact in the first line.
2. Injustice followed by a payoff (pardon, victory, recognition), not injustice alone.
3. Real archival photos with dates, which the reels already use and which competitors mostly do not.
4. Topics around US history from 1850 to 1960, which match a US audience aged 45+.

# Competitive Landscape

The audience you compete for is not only "Black history channels". In the data, the same viewers are reached by five groups:

1. **Direct Black history storytellers** (Black Journals, One Mic History, Unseen Frontlines, Black History Exposed, Unsung Black History, Black Stories Untold).
2. **African history and Africa culture channels** (HomeTeam History, Historical Africa, From Nothing, AfricanCore, One54 Africa).
3. **Black wealth and culture channels** (Black Status, Official Black Wall Street, Black Culture Unlocked) and **heritage nostalgia** (Potlikker's food of Black ancestors).
4. **General untold-history and documentary channels that regularly win with Black stories** (PBS Origins "They Don't Teach You About", The Fat Electrician's Eugene Bullard video at 5M views, Serious History, Forgotten History, The People Profiles).
5. **Institutional full documentaries** (PBS American Experience, FRONTLINE, National Geographic, Netflix's 13TH) that own the broad searches like "black history documentary" or "slavery documentary".

**What the search results show (verified).** For broad searches such as "black history documentary", "slavery documentary" and "civil rights documentary", the top results are dominated by institutions and huge general channels (13TH by Netflix 16M, PBS, National Geographic, History Time). A new channel cannot win those searches. For specific searches the field is thin: "black history erased", "forgotten black history" and "black history they didn't teach you" are led by channels with 20K-200K subscribers. Of 145 recent search results with 300K+ views across the 30 searches, the most frequent themes were Africa and its kingdoms (19), wealth and business (9), soldiers and the military (7), slavery (4) and civil rights (4). No recent Haiti video reached 300K views, which suggests a gap rather than lack of interest, because Haiti searches returned older videos with millions of views.

**Quality of supply (assumption based on titles and channel descriptions).** Several fast-growing direct competitors rely on outrage framing ("White Folks FREAKING-OUT After...", "Black Folks, run now!!"), political news hooks ("Trump PANICS..."), or sensational claims ("Russia Opens Its Vaults To Reveal Black Biblical Israelites"). HomeTeam History, the biggest pure African history channel (814K), now posts about once a month and its latest uploads get about 5K views. That leaves room for a channel that is both gripping and trustworthy.

# Winning Content Patterns

## Topics that repeatedly generate high views (verified frequency in breakout videos)

Across the 134 breakout videos in the Black history set:

| Title signal | Share of breakout titles |
|---|---:|
| Contains a word in capitals (RICH, KKK, TRUTH) | 26% |
| Contains a number | 23% |
| Wealth: rich, millionaire, billionaire, empire | 13% |
| Starts with How, Why, What, Who or When | 13% |
| Erased, forgotten, hidden, untold, never taught | 12% |
| They, them, their (an implied villain) | 10% |
| Soldiers, war, the Klan, the military | 10% |
| Kings, queens, kingdoms, royalty | 7% |
| Question mark | 6% |

Median title length was 49 characters (half between 40 and 59).

**Topic clusters that repeat (assumption from the tables above):**

1. **Black people fighting back and winning** (soldiers vs the Klan, armed self-defense, courtroom wins).
2. **Black wealth that was built, then taken or hidden** (millionaires, Black Wall Streets, land).
3. **African royalty and kingdoms** before and against colonization.
4. **Clever survival under Jim Crow and slavery** (Sears catalog, Green Book, escape stories).
5. **"What they didn't teach you"** reframes of familiar history.
6. **Origins** of a phrase, a custom or a stereotype (Uncle Tom, Jim Crow, "40 acres").

## Story structures that work

| Structure | Example | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Injustice, resistance, victory | Soldiers vs the Klan | The strongest. It delivers outrage and payoff in one video. |
| Rise, success, theft or downfall, legacy | Black millionaires erased | High emotion, natural chapters, strong thumbnails (wealth, fire). |
| Forgotten person, discovery, legacy | Eugene Bullard | Evergreen; needs a strong nickname or superlative to click. |
| Mystery, investigation, revelation | Last Black queen of England | High CTR; must resolve honestly or comments turn. |
| Ordinary object or trick beat the system | Sears catalog | Rare, very shareable, short to research. |
| Myth, evidence, real story | Mansa Musa "fraud" | Contrarian clicks; fits your two-source rule perfectly. |

## Emotional drivers, ranked by what the data rewards

1. **Justice after injustice** (pride plus outrage). Present in most of the top 10.
2. **Curiosity gap** (a question, "how", "who was").
3. **Pride** (queens, inventors, millionaires).
4. **Shock** (a concrete, unbelievable fact).
5. **Outrage alone** works for clicks but builds an angry, low-trust audience; competitors who rely on it (Whispers of the Past) show weak median views (2.6% of subscribers).
6. **Nostalgia** works strongly for older viewers (Potlikker).
7. **Tragedy alone** underperforms unless it ends with consequence or vindication.

## Characters

- **Forgotten heroes with a nickname or superlative** outperform famous names. Famous names (Tubman, King, Parks) attract search traffic but face PBS, History and Biography.
- **Soldiers and lawmen** perform very well, especially with a villain (the Klan, a corrupt sheriff).
- **Entrepreneurs and millionaires** perform well when the story includes loss (fire, massacre, theft, a bank failure).

# Title Strategy

## What 100+ successful titles have in common

Based on the 134 breakout titles in the Black history set plus the top results from 30 searches (see the frequency table under Winning Content Patterns):

- **A named subject or a concrete group** ("The Black Soldiers", "Eugene Bullard", "7 Black Millionaires"). Abstract titles ("The History of Slavery") almost never break out.
- **One power word, often in capitals.** RICHEST, KKK, ERASED, TRUTH, FORBIDDEN. About a quarter of hits use capitals. Use one capitalised word at most; more reads as spam.
- **Numbers for lists** (5, 7, 10, 30, 100). About a quarter of hits.
- **An implied villain**: "They tried to erase", "They don't teach you". This works, but overuse makes a channel sound conspiratorial.
- **A contrast**: rich vs poor, enslaved vs millionaire, banned vs champion.
- **A question** only when the answer is genuinely surprising ("Who Was Uncle Tom?").
- **Length**: 40-60 characters. Front-load the hook, because phones cut titles near 50 characters.
- **Dates help when they mark a famous moment** (1898, 1921, 1944), not for obscure years.

# Thumbnail Strategy

## What competitor thumbnails do (observed on search and channel pages)

- **One face, large.** Most winners use a single archival portrait filling 40-60% of the frame, eyes in the top third. Group photos only when the group is the story (soldiers, Hellfighters).
- **Historical photos dominate** in the direct niche. Illustrated and AI-generated faces are common on the fast-growing volume channels and on Africa channels where no photos exist.
- **Text: 1-4 words**, large, high contrast, usually yellow, white or red on dark. Never a repeat of the title.
- **Strong contrast**: dark sepia or black background, one saturated accent (red, gold).
- **Symbols**: chains, fire, gold coins, a badge, a crown, a stamp ("ERASED", "BANNED").
- **Before/after and maps** appear on wealth and lost-town videos (a town, then flames; a map with a red circle).
- **Emotion**: serious or defiant faces. Smiling portraits are rare among the winners.

## A reusable system for you

**Template A, "The Portrait" (biographies, 60% of videos).**
Left 55%: one archival portrait, cropped to head and shoulders, lifted with light contrast and a soft warm grade. Right 45%: dark background, a 1-3 word label in your gold (#e0a93b) heavy serif, one small object or symbol relevant to the story. A thin gold frame edge as the channel signature.

**Template B, "The Event" (massacres, battles, raids).**
Full-bleed archival scene or engraving, darkened. One red stamp across a corner ("1898", "COUP", "THE RAID"). Small portrait inset of the central person if one exists.

**Template C, "The List" (5 queens, 7 millionaires).**
A row of 3-5 portraits, the number huge in gold on the left.

**Template D, "The Long Night" (compilations).**
Warm lamp-lit desk with archival photos and the duration ("2 HOURS").

**Brand rules.** Same gold, same serif, same frame edge on every thumbnail. No fake photos: if no portrait exists, use a period map, document or engraving, and say so. Avoid graphic violence, nooses or slurs in thumbnails; they trigger limited ads and can hurt recommendations. Make two versions of every thumbnail and use YouTube's built-in "Test & Compare" to choose.

# Retention Strategy

You cannot see competitors' retention, so these rules come from the structure of the breakout videos and from your own Shorts data (74% "stayed to watch").

**1. Hook (first 30 seconds).**
- Start on the most surprising true sentence, not on a date or a greeting. Your Shorts prove this works.
- Show the payoff image early (the pardon, the warship, the bank) and promise to explain it.
- No channel intro, no "in this video". Title card after the hook, 2 seconds maximum.

**2. Open loops.** Keep two questions open at all times. Close one, open another. Example: "He had the money. What he didn't have yet was the law on his side. That came in 1881."

**3. Escalation.** Each chapter must raise the stakes (money, danger, people involved). If a chapter does not raise stakes, cut it.

**4. Reveals on a schedule.** Plan a "document reveal" (letter, court record, newspaper, photo) every 3-4 minutes. Your archival-image library is your biggest retention asset.

**5. Chapter transitions.** End each chapter with a question card in gold text ("So why did the Navy put them on trial?"). Add YouTube chapters in the description; they help search and let older viewers navigate.

**6. Pattern interrupts every 30-60 seconds.** Alternate: archival photo with slow push, map animation, document zoom with highlighted line, date stamp, short silence, and one quote card. Never hold the same visual type for more than 20 seconds.

**7. Narration pacing.** About 140-155 words a minute for long-form (slower than your 163 wpm reels). Pause one beat after each reveal. Vary sentence length. Your audience skews older and watches on phones: keep on-screen text large and on screen for at least 2 seconds.

**8. Cliffhanger before mid-rolls.** Place ad breaks right after a question, not after an answer.

**9. The cost beat.** Breakout videos do not stop at the victory; they show the price paid. It raises emotion in the final third, where most retention is lost.

**10. End screen.** Last 20 seconds point to one specific next video in the same pillar, with a spoken reason ("If this made you angry, the next one will make you proud").

**Targets to watch in Studio:** 30-second retention above 70%, average percentage viewed above 35% for 20-minute videos, and no drop steeper than 10 points between chapters.

# Shorts → Long-Form Strategy

**What the data says.** 93% of your views come from the Shorts feed and 99% of viewers are new. Shorts reach people; they rarely watch long videos from the same channel. Expect a low conversion rate, roughly 1-3% of Shorts viewers clicking through (assumption). That still matters: 50,000 Shorts views a month would send 500-1,500 long-form viewers, who are the most likely to subscribe and return.

**System.**

1. **Three Shorts per long-form video.**
   - **Trailer Short** (published 1 day before): the cold-open fact, ending on the open question.
   - **Clip Short** (published the same day): the best reveal from the middle.
   - **Twist Short** (published 3-5 days later): the cost or twist from the last act.
2. **Link every Short to its long-form** using the "Related video" setting on each Short. Say it in the last line: "The full story is linked above."
3. **Keep one unrelated "discovery" Short a day** in your current style, because your hooks are working; link each to the closest long-form video.
4. **Pin a comment** on long-form videos pointing to the next episode in the pillar.
5. **Playlists by pillar** so a converted viewer binge-watches.
6. **Timing.** Publish long-form at a consistent time when your US audience is free (late afternoon or evening US Eastern). Studio does not yet have enough data for your audience; check "When your viewers are on YouTube" after 4 weeks.

**Signal to watch.** In Studio, compare views coming from "Shorts feed" to long-form videos each week. If it stays under 1% of Shorts views after a month, shift Shorts to trailers only.

# Final Verdict

**1. Is the Black history niche still attractive on YouTube?** Yes. Verified: new channels in 2025 reached 1M+ views per video in months (Unseen Frontlines, Black Status, Potlikker), and the established names are slowing (HomeTeam History, History With No Chaser, Black History in Two Minutes). Demand is steady and peaks every February and around Juneteenth.

**2. Is long-form worth pursuing?** Yes, and it is required. Shorts will not monetize meaningfully and do not build returning viewers; 99% of your audience is new each month.

**3. What type of long-form?** 20-25 minute single-story documentaries about a specific person or event, structured as injustice, resistance and payoff, with real archival images and visible sources.

**4. What should you avoid?** Outrage-bait titles that are not literally true; modern politics hooks; graphic violence in thumbnails and first seconds; AI-generated "historical photos"; broad topics such as "The History of Slavery" that institutions already own; fringe claims you cannot source twice.

**5. Biggest opportunities.** Black self-defense and soldiers who fought back; historical Black millionaires; "ordinary things that beat Jim Crow"; phrase and stereotype origins; myth vs record on famous figures; Haiti; African kingdoms told as events; monthly long compilations for an older audience.

**6. Biggest competitors.** For your viewer's next click: Unseen Frontlines, One Mic History, Black Journals, Black History Exposed, Unsung Black History, PBS Origins and The Fat Electrician.

**7. What you can do that they don't.** Show the receipts: every video tied to a sourced article on your website with citations and a map; real dated photographs, credited; a "myth vs record" stance; a calm, consistent narrator for an older audience; and a production pipeline that already turns research into video.

**8. What could realistically reach 100K subscribers?** About 80-120 long-form documentaries over 12-18 months, at two a week, with 4-6 breakout videos (500K+). On current competitor evidence, breakouts come from the They Fought Back and Stolen Fortunes pillars. Assumption: a disciplined channel at this quality could reach 100K in 12-24 months; it is not guaranteed.

**9. What could reach $1,000 a month?** Partner Program eligibility (4,000 long-form hours) plus about 100,000-250,000 monthly long-form views. With 30-40 documentaries live and one or two that keep getting recommended, that is realistic within 6-9 months.

**10. What could reach $5,000+ a month?** About 500,000-1.25M monthly long-form views, usually from a library of 80+ videos, two or three evergreen hits, and monthly compilations that rack up watch time, plus a sponsor or two.

## The single strategy I would choose

**"The receipts channel for Black history."** Two 20-25 minute documentaries a week, each one a specific, true story of injustice, resistance and payoff, chosen from the They Fought Back, Stolen Fortunes, Outsmarted and Myth vs Record pillars. Every title literally true, every claim sourced twice and linked to the article on theblackhistoryroom.com, every image a real archival record. One Short a day, each a trailer linked to its documentary. One long compilation a month for the older, TV-watching audience. Start with the stories already researched for the website (Smalls, Reeves, Jack Johnson, Parks, Mansa Musa) so the first month costs research time you have already spent, then move to the highest-scoring new topics: Wilmington 1898, Port Chicago, Monroe 1957, historical Black millionaires and the Clotilda.
