# Research brief: "15 Forgotten Black Towns America Erased" (8-10 min)

Researched 2026-11-23. Seneca Village left out on purpose. Every key number has two sources. **[DISPUTED]** means the sources disagree. **[1 SRC]** means only one source says it, so say it with care or leave it out.

Image rule used: only Wikimedia Commons files whose license line says **Public domain** or **CC0** (checked through the Commons API, LicenseShortName field). "Copyrighted free use" and Flickr "No known restrictions" files are listed apart and marked, since they are not strictly PD/CC0. Open each file page once more before you download it.

## Opening hook (one line)

> "They didn't fade away. They were burned, flooded, bulldozed and paved over, and today millions of people swim, drive and park on top of them without knowing their names."

## 1. Oscarville, Forsyth County, Georgia

1. **Thrived:** Formed in the Reconstruction era. A farming community; about 1,100 residents in 1911 (Black and white; a large share Black) **[1 SRC: Wikipedia]**. Forsyth County had about 1,098 Black residents in 1912 (NPR: "1,100").
2. **People:** Rob Edwards (24), lynched by a mob of about 2,000 that broke into the Cumming jail. Ernest Knox (16) and Oscar Daniel (18), hanged after sham trials before about 8,000 onlookers **[1 SRC for crowd sizes]**. Grant Smith, a Black preacher, was horsewhipped for asking for calm.
3. **How erased:** In Sept-Oct 1912, after the death of a white woman (Mae Crow), night riders burned homes and churches and ordered every Black person out of the county. Nearly all 1,098 left within weeks; their land passed into white hands. Plans for Buford Dam were approved in 1947. The US Army Corps of Engineers flooded the area to make Lake Lanier (dam finished 1956). **[DISPUTED]** Popular posts say "a Black town was flooded on purpose." The Atlanta History Center calls that "more myth than reality": Black families were driven out about 40 years **before** the lake, and white residents farmed the land until the government bought it. Script it as **expelled in 1912, drowned in the 1950s**.
4. **Now:** Under and beside Lake Lanier. Some cemeteries were never moved **[1 SRC]**. A 1987 county inquiry into returning the land gave nothing back.
5. **Sources:** NPR, Fresh Air (2016): https://www.npr.org/2016/09/15/494063372/the-racial-cleansing-that-drove-1-100-black-residents-out-of-forsyth-county-ga · Atlanta History Center, "Forsyth 1912": https://www.atlantahistorycenter.com/blog/forsyth-1912-examining-the-displacement-of-the-countys-black-residents/ · Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscarville,_Georgia
6. **Images (PD):** `File:Front Page of Atlanta Georgian Newspaper Sep 10 1912.jpg` · `File:Mob batters down jail door at Cumming, Atlanta Georgian Newspaper Sep 10 1912.jpg` · `File:Defendants in 1912 Forsyth County Trials.jpg`. 7. **Film:** none found.

## 2. Kowaliga (also called Benson), Elmore/Tallapoosa Counties, Alabama

1. **Thrived:** About 1890-1926. Size unknown; "around forty Black and White families" lived on Benson land **[1 SRC: Wikipedia]**.
2. **Founder:** **John Jackson Benson** (c.1850-1925), born enslaved, bought back land and founded the town. His son **William E. Benson** (1873-1915) founded the Kowaliga Academic and Industrial Institute (1890s) and the Dixie Industrial Company. In 1914 the company built the **Dixie Line**, a 16-mile railroad to Alexander City, called the first Black-owned railroad **[1 SRC for "first"; good Short topic, verify more]**.
3. **How erased:** Alabama Power's Martin Dam (built 1923-1926) made Lake Martin, which covered Kowaliga and the nearby Black township of Sousanna. John Benson died months before it was finished. The lake first filled to capacity in April 1928. **[Caveat]** The school and town were already in decline after William's death in 1915; the dam finished them off. Don't say "the dam destroyed a thriving town overnight."
4. **Now:** Russell Lands lake-house development. Part of the site lies under the Kowaliga Bridge; a rebuilt bell tower stands near an old foundation.
5. **Sources:** Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kowaliga,_Alabama · Alexander City Outlook (2021): https://www.alexcityoutlook.com/news/two-amateur-historians-are-putting-the-drowned-town-of-benson-back-on-the-map/article_3afaeb42-a820-11eb-a63e-5b09d47736a2.html · Encyclopedia of Alabama, Lake Martin: https://encyclopediaofalabama.org/?p=14926 · Alabama Heritage No. 76 (2005), "William Benson and the Kowaliga School"
6. **Images (PD), very good:** `File:John Jackson Benson.png` · `File:William "Will" E. Benson (1873–1915).png` · `File:General View of Kowaliga Academic and Industrial Institute.png` (also `...campus view east.png`, `Partial group of teachers from Kowaliga Academic and Industrial Institute.png`)
7. **Film:** none found.

## 3. Vanport, Oregon

1. **Thrived:** Built from August 1942 to house Kaiser shipyard workers. Peak about 40,000 **[DISPUTED: Oregon Encyclopedia ~42,000, Dec 1944]**, making it Oregon's 2nd-largest city. Black share at peak "close to 40%" **[DISPUTED; Wikipedia]**. About one-third of the ~18,500 residents were Black at the time of the flood (multiple sources agree).
2. **People:** Built by Henry J. Kaiser's shipyards with the Housing Authority of Portland. For a named resident, use survivor oral histories (Vanport Mosaic, NPR).
3. **How erased:** On **May 30, 1948** (Memorial Day), a 200-ft railroad berm gave way at about 4:05-4:17 pm **[sources differ on minute]**. The city was gone by nightfall. Official deaths: 15. About 18,000 people lost their homes. Residents had been told that morning the dikes were safe (NPR). Black families then pushed into Portland's Albina district, which later lost homes to highways and a stadium.
4. **Now:** Delta Park, Portland International Raceway and a golf course.
5. **Sources:** Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanport,_Oregon · Oregon Encyclopedia: https://www.oregonencyclopedia.org/articles/vanport/ · NPR Code Switch (2015): https://www.npr.org/sections/codeswitch/2015/03/02/389482158/the-time-nature-and-racism-teamed-up-to-wipe-out-a-whole-town
6. **Images:** `File:1948 Vanport flood.jpg` (PD) · `File:AT DELTA PARK ON THE COLUMBIA RIVER - NARA - 548116.jpg` (PD, later view of the site). Not strictly PD ("Copyrighted free use"): `File:Aerial view of vanport flooded.jpg`, `File:Vanport flood overturned cars.jpg`, `File:Vanport street scene.jpg`. Only use these if you accept that license.
7. **Film:** *The Columbia* (Bonneville Power Administration, 1949), Internet Archive `gov.fdr.353.3.4`, **CC0**: https://archive.org/details/gov.fdr.353.3.4. It probably includes 1948 flood footage, but I have not checked for Vanport frames. Scrub it.

## 4. Bruce's Beach, Manhattan Beach, California

1. **Thrived:** 1912-1924. A beach resort (lodge, café, dance hall) for Black Angelenos who were shut out of other beaches. It had a few owners and many visitors, not a residential town.
2. **Founders:** **Willa and Charles Bruce** bought the land in 1912 for $1,225.
3. **How erased:** After Klan-linked harassment, the City of Manhattan Beach took the land in **1924** by eminent domain "for a park." The Bruces sued for $120,000 and got **$14,500** in 1929. The site stayed mostly empty for decades.
4. **Now:** A small park. On June 28, 2022, LA County voted to return the parcels to the Bruces' great-grandsons. In January 2023 the family announced it would sell them back to the county for **$20 million**.
5. **Sources:** Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce%27s_Beach · ABC7 (2023): https://abc7.com/bruces-beach-manhattan-la-county-coastal-property/12647127/ · Daily Breeze (2022): https://www.dailybreeze.com/2022/06/28/la-county-returns-land-to-black-family-90-plus-years-after-manhattan-beach-took-it
6. **Images:** none found that are PD/CC0. The Commons photos are CC BY/BY-SA, and the historic photos belong to the family or county.
7. **Film:** none found.

## 5. Ocoee, Florida

1. **Thrived:** 255 Black residents and 560 white in the 1920 census. Black families owned farms and groves.
2. **People:** **July Perry** and **Mose Norman**, Black landowners who organized voter registration and paid poll taxes.
3. **How erased:** On **Nov 2-3, 1920** (Election Day), Norman was turned away from the polls. A white mob attacked Perry's home; Perry was lynched in Orlando. The mob burned at least 25 Black homes, two churches and a lodge **[DISPUTED: NYT 1920 reported "at least 20 buildings"]**. Deaths: **[DISPUTED]** official accounts are low; estimates run about 30-56 (NAACP investigator Walter White heard ~30 and ~56). About 500 Black residents were driven out. The 1930 census counted **2** Black residents. No Black family moved back until 1981.
4. **Now:** A mostly white Orlando suburb. Florida passed a 2020 law requiring the massacre to be taught, and Ocoee has a historical marker.
5. **Sources:** Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocoee_massacre · Florida OPPAGA report (2019): https://oppaga.fl.gov/Documents/Reports/19-15.pdf · Washington Post (2020): https://www.washingtonpost.com/history/2020/11/02/ocoee-florida-election-day-massacre/
6. **Images (PD):** `File:July Perry Ocoee Massacre victim.jpg` · `File:Front-page-Evening-Reporter-Star-November-3-1920-Ocoee-Massacre-Florida.jpg` · CC0: `File:Lynching of July Perry historical marker at Orange County Regional History Center 65 E Central Blvd Orlando FL.jpg`
7. **Film:** none found.

## 6. Rosewood, Florida

1. **Thrived:** Peak 355 (1915). In 1920, Rosewood and Sumner together had 638 people (344 Black). Black residents worked in turpentine, timber and a pencil mill.
2. **People:** **Sarah Carrier** (killed in her home) and her son **Sylvester Carrier**, who defended the house. Survivor **Minnie Lee Langley**. White storekeeper **John Wright** hid survivors.
3. **How erased:** On **Jan 1-7, 1923**, after a white woman (Fannie Taylor) said a Black man attacked her, white mobs from nearby towns burned almost every building; the last dozen or so went on Jan 7. Official deaths: 8 (6 Black, 2 white). **[DISPUTED]** Survivors claimed up to 27 dead; some reports say up to 150. No one was charged. The town was never rebuilt.
4. **Now:** A highway marker on SR 24 (2004) and John Wright's house. In 1994 Florida became the first state to pay compensation for racial violence. **[DISPUTED amounts]** Reports give $2.1M or "$2M" in total, with $150,000 each for 9 survivors who lost property, plus a scholarship fund.
5. **Sources:** Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosewood_massacre · Britannica: https://www.britannica.com/topic/Rosewood-riot-of-1923 · Southern Changes (Emory): https://southernchanges.digitalscholarship.emory.edu/sc16-1_001/sc16-1_005
6. **Images (PD):** `File:Rosewood Florida rc12408.jpg` and `File:Rosewood Florida rc12409.jpg` (1923, Florida Memory) · `File:Rosewood 1923 map from Tropic Magazine.jpg` · `File:Sara Carrier.jpg` · `File:Rosewood Florida Headline.jpg`
7. **Film:** none (the 1997 film *Rosewood* is copyrighted).

## 7. Sugar Hill (West Adams Heights), Los Angeles

1. **Thrived:** Black homeowners moved into the covenant-restricted mansion tract from 1938. It was LA's richest Black enclave in the 1940s-50s. Population: a few dozen elite households (31 Black owners were named in the 1945 suit).
2. **People:** **Hattie McDaniel** (first Black Oscar winner; she held strategy meetings at her house), Louise Beavers, Ethel Waters, Pearl Bailey, Norman O. Houston (Golden State Mutual). Ties in with the channel's Hattie McDaniel reel.
3. **How erased:** White neighbors sued to enforce racial covenants. In **Dec 1945**, Judge **Thurmond Clarke** toured the street and threw the case out ("Victory on Sugar Hill", *Time*). **[DATE: Wikipedia says filed Dec 6, ruling the next day; other sources call Dec 6 the hearing date]** The case is cited as paving the way to *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948). Then the state routed the **Santa Monica Freeway (I-10)** through the neighborhood. It was built in the early 1960s (Wikipedia gives 1963) and residents lost their lobbying fight in Sacramento. **[Nuance]** The freeway cut the area in half; it did not level all of it. Number of houses lost: not found.
4. **Now:** The I-10 runs through it. Since 2012 the south side has been the "Sugar Hill Historic District."
5. **Sources:** NPR (2021): https://www.npr.org/2021/05/04/993605428/beneath-the-santa-monica-freeway-lies-the-erasure-of-sugar-hill · Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Adams_Heights,_Los_Angeles · FOX 11 LA: https://www.foxla.com/news/sugar-hill-the-dark-history-the-10-freeway-has-to-a-historic-black-community
6. **Images (PD):** `File:Studio publicity Hattie McDaniel.jpg` · `File:1941hattie.jpg` · `File:"Hattie McDaniel (center), Chairman of the Negro Division of the Hollywood Victory Committee, takes time off from rehear - NARA - 535875.jpg`. No PD photo of the street itself was found (`West Adams Heights Park.jpg` is CC0 but modern).
7. **Film:** none of the neighborhood. McDaniel PD film clips: see the film-clip-reel-method skill.

## 8. Rondo, St. Paul, Minnesota

1. **Thrived:** Boom in the 1910s-20s. By the 1930s, half of St. Paul's Black population lived in Rondo (MNopedia).
2. **People:** **Roy Wilkins** (later head of the national NAACP) grew up there. Rev. Floyd Massey Jr.
3. **How erased:** **I-94** construction ran **1956-1968** (opened Dec 9, 1968). **[DISPUTED counts]** 600-700 homes and about 300 businesses were lost (KARE 11 / Star Tribune say 600+; the MN Attorney General's deck and PBS say 700). Planners picked the route through the Black neighborhood over a route along rail lines.
4. **Now:** A sunken freeway. In **July 2015**, Mayor Chris Coleman and MnDOT apologized. "Reconnect Rondo" proposes a land bridge over I-94, and Rondo Avenue got its name back in 2024.
5. **Sources:** MNopedia (MN Historical Society): https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/rondo-neighborhood-st-paul · KARE 11: https://kare11.com/article/news/local/rondo-neighborhood-gets-apologies-for-i-94/105454642 · PBS NewsHour: https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/could-a-land-bridge-project-help-minority-communities-recover-from-displacement
6. **Images:** none found as PD/CC0 (Commons has only CC BY parade photos). MNHS holds the historic photos; ask them for licensing if needed.
7. **Film:** none found.

## 9. Black Bottom and Paradise Valley, Detroit, Michigan

1. **Thrived:** Grew after World War I with the Great Migration; jazz peak in the 1930s-50s on Hastings and St. Antoine streets. About a third of Black Detroiters lived in Paradise Valley **[1 SRC]**.
2. **People:** **Joe Louis** grew up here (also Coleman Young, Sugar Ray Robinson, Della Reese). Duke Ellington and Billie Holiday played the clubs.
3. **How erased:** Slum clearance under the 1949 Housing Act. By 1950, 423 homes, 109 businesses and 22 factories had been condemned **[1 SRC: Wikipedia, citing Reuther Library]**. The Chrysler Freeway / **I-375** (1950s-60s) was laid over Hastings Street, and Lafayette Park (Mies van der Rohe) replaced the homes. Residents went to the Brewster-Douglass projects. The last Paradise Valley buildings were razed in 2000.
4. **Now:** Lafayette Park and the I-375 trench. In Sept 2022, a **$104M** federal grant was given to turn I-375 into a street-level boulevard.
5. **Sources:** Detroit Historical Society: https://detroithistorical.org/learn/encyclopedia-of-detroit/black-bottom-neighborhood · Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Bottom,_Detroit · City of Detroit I-375 project: https://detroitmi.gov/departments/planning-and-development-department/neighborhood-plans/central-design-region/i-375-reconnecting-communities-project
6. **Images:** none found of Black Bottom itself. Usable PD/CC0 people: `File:Joe Louis, Underwood & Underwood, active 1880 - c. 1950, from the National Portrait Gallery, Smithsonian Institution.tif` (CC0) · `File:Joe Louis - Max Schmeling - 1936.jpg` (PD). Detroit context (PD, 1942 Sojourner Truth housing, not Black Bottom): `File:We want white tenants.jpg`
7. **Film:** none found.

## 10. Overtown, Miami, Florida

1. **Thrived:** Laid out as "Colored Town" when Miami incorporated in 1896. Its main avenue was called the "Little Broadway" of the South (Lyric Theater, 1913). Count Basie, Ella Fitzgerald and Billie Holiday stayed in its hotels because Miami Beach barred them.
2. **People:** **D. A. Dorsey**, Miami's first Black millionaire, built the Dorsey House (1913).
3. **How erased:** In the 1960s, I-95, I-395/SR 836 and the Midtown Interchange were driven through the center. **[DISPUTED]** Population fell from about 40,000 to about 10,000 (University of Miami), or by about 50% by 1965 (CNU), or by 40% (UPenn paper). A 1957 city estimate expected 5,700 people displaced.
4. **Now:** Highway ramps over the old core. A few landmarks survive (Lyric Theater, Dorsey House), alongside gentrification and the "Underdeck" park plan.
5. **Sources:** University of Miami Libraries: https://library.miami.edu/ohp-communities-overtown.html · NPR (2010): https://www.npr.org/2010/08/28/129475747/paying-a-local-price-for-i-95s-global-promise · Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overtown_(Miami)
6. **Images:** none found as PD/CC0 (Commons Overtown photos are CC BY/BY-SA). Florida Memory (floridamemory.com) probably has public-record photos of Colored Town; check each one.
7. **Film:** none found.

## 11. Claiborne Avenue, Tremé / 7th Ward, New Orleans, Louisiana

1. **Thrived:** The main Black shopping street of New Orleans, with a wide live-oak neutral ground used for Mardi Gras Indian gatherings. Circle Food Store (1938) was the city's first Black-owned full grocery.
2. **People:** Circle Food Store founders. Alternatively, Mardi Gras Indian chiefs (needs one more source).
3. **How erased:** Work began in **February 1966** (the oaks are said to have been bulldozed around Ash Wednesday). The elevated I-10 opened 1968-69 **[DISPUTED year]**. **[DISPUTED counts]** Over 200 oaks (WWNO) or 400 (Streetsblog); 155 properties bought (WWNO) or about 500 homes (advocacy figure); more than 120 businesses on the corridor (WWL-TV).
4. **Now:** Concrete columns over parking lots. Artists painted oak trees on the pillars. Removal studies come and go.
5. **Sources:** WWNO / Historic New Orleans Collection "TriPod": https://tripodnola.hnoc.org/episodes/the-monster-claiborne-avenue-before-and-after-the-interstate/ · Smithsonian: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/documenting-history-iconic-new-orleans-street-and-looking-its-future-180977854/ · Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claiborne_Avenue
6. **Images (PD):** `File:ClaiborneFrenchmen1938B.jpg` (Claiborne Avenue in 1938, before the highway; a strong before/after shot)
7. **Film:** none found.

## 12. Hayti, Durham, North Carolina

1. **Thrived:** Founded by freedpeople right after the Civil War; flourished from the 1880s to the 1940s. It was the home district of "Black Wall Street" Durham (NC Mutual Life, Mechanics & Farmers Bank).
2. **People:** **John Merrick** and **Dr. Aaron McDuffie Moore**, NC Mutual founders. Later residents included painter Ernie Barnes and singer Shirley Caesar.
3. **How erased:** Urban renewal began in **1958**; the Durham Freeway (NC 147) was built in the 1960s-70s. About **4,000 families and 500 businesses** were displaced (BullCity150, Duke, City of Durham). **[DISPUTED]** One Syracuse paper says 5,000+ families and 1,000+ businesses. The promised rebuilding never came.
4. **Now:** Only St. Joseph's AME Church (now the Hayti Heritage Center) is left standing; the freeway runs through. Durham is studying a "Reimagine Durham Freeway" plan.
5. **Sources:** BullCity150 (Duke): https://www.bullcity150.org/uneven_ground/dismantling_hayti/ · ABC11: https://abc11.com/post/durham-hayti-community-freeway-history-black/10362504/ · Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hayti,_Durham,_North_Carolina
6. **Images:** none found historic. PD: `File:John Merrick - a biographical sketch (IA johnmerrickbiogr00andr).pdf` (1920 book; a portrait can be pulled from it). CC0 modern: `File:Sunset in Hayti, Durham.jpg`
7. **Film:** none found.

## 13. Fillmore District (Western Addition), San Francisco, California

1. **Thrived:** The "Harlem of the West" in the 1940s-50s. The city's Black population went from 4,836 (1940) to 43,460 (1950). Jazz at Jimbo's Bop City.
2. **People:** Jimbo Edwards (Bop City owner); Charlie Parker and Louis Armstrong played there.
3. **How erased:** San Francisco Redevelopment Agency projects. **A-1** (1956-73) covered 28 blocks, displaced about 8,000 people and destroyed about 6,000 units. **A-2** (1966-late 70s) covered about 70 blocks and displaced up to 13,500 people (4,522 households). Residents called it "Negro Removal." Only **4%** of the "certificates of preference" promising displaced people a way back were ever used (as of 2001).
4. **Now:** Geary Boulevard expressway and mixed redevelopment; jazz-history plaques.
5. **Sources:** KQED (2020): https://www.kqed.org/news/11825401/how-urban-renewal-decimated-the-fillmore-district-and-took-jazz-with-it · PBS/KQED *The Fillmore*: https://www.pbs.org/kqed/fillmore/ · Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fillmore_District,_San_Francisco
6. **Images:** none historical found as PD/CC0. `File:Western Addition neighborhood, San Francisco, California LCCN2013630354.tif` is PD but appears to be a modern Carol Highsmith photo.
7. **Film:** none found.

## 14. Kinloch, Missouri

1. **Thrived:** Founded in 1890; described as the oldest Black community incorporated in Missouri. Population 6,501 in 1960 (census peak shown).
2. **People:** Congresswoman **Maxine Waters**, actress Jenifer Lewis, Silicon Valley pioneer Roy Clay, and soul singer Ann Peebles grew up there.
3. **How erased:** From the 1980s, St. Louis bought out homes for a **Lambert Airport noise program**. The buyout cost over **$50M**, mostly FAA money; homes averaged under $25,000 **[1 SRC: Riverfront Times]**. Population went from 2,702 (1990) to 449 (2000). **[DISPUTED]** The airport calls it a legal noise program; critics say it was meant to dissolve the town.
4. **Now:** Mostly empty lots and woods. Population 263 (2020).
5. **Sources:** Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinloch,_Missouri · Riverfront Times, "Promised Land": https://www.riverfronttimes.com/?p=42185 · St. Louis Public Radio (2006): https://www.stlpr.org/other/2006-06-07/lambert-considers-selling-back-land-to-kinloch · Book: John A. Wright, *Kinloch: Missouri's First Black City* (2000)
6. **Images:** none found as PD/CC0 (Commons only has CC BY 2.0).
7. **Film:** none found.

## 15. Greenwood ("Black Wall Street"), Tulsa, Oklahoma (finale)

1. **Thrived:** Organized in 1906. Most of Tulsa's ~10,000 Black residents lived there in 1921.
2. **People:** **O. W. Gurley**, land buyer and owner of the Gurley Hotel, is usually named as Greenwood's founder **[PBS/Tulsa sources; Wikipedia names him only as a hotel owner]**. Also J.B. Stradford (Stradford Hotel). Survivor **Viola Fletcher** testified to Congress in 2021.
3. **How erased (twice):** On **May 31-June 1, 1921**, a white mob burned 35+ blocks. The Red Cross counted **1,256 houses burned** plus 215 looted, and about **10,000** people were left homeless. **[DISPUTED deaths]** The official count is 36; the Oklahoma Commission estimate is up to ~300; the Oklahoma Historical Society says "fifty to three hundred." Greenwood rebuilt, then **urban renewal and I-244** (planned from 1957, built late 1960s-early 1970s **[DISPUTED dates]**) cut through it: the "second destruction."
4. **Now:** I-244 overpass above Greenwood Avenue; Greenwood Rising museum (2021). A federal grant is funding a study of removing that stretch of I-244.
5. **Sources:** Oklahoma Historical Society Encyclopedia: https://www.okhistory.org/publications/enc/entry?entry=TU013 · Wikipedia: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulsa_race_massacre · Smithsonian, "Black Wall Street's Second Destruction": https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/black-wall-streets-second-destruction-180977871/
6. **Images (PD, Library of Congress copies):** `File:Smoke billowing over Tulsa, Oklahoma during 1921 race riots LCCN95517018.jpg` · `File:Panorama of the ruined area tulsa race riots.jpg` · `File:Rubble of houses in African American neighborhood in Tulsa, Okla. after race riots LCCN97503809.jpg` · newspaper: `File:19210602 Tulsa Dead Total 85 - race massacre - The Boston Daily Globe.jpg`. Many more are tagged Flickr "No known restrictions" (e.g., `Little Africa on Fire Tulsa Race Riot, June 1st, 1921 (14391817512).jpg`); use those only if that tag is acceptable.
7. **Film:** none confirmed PD.

## Gaps and cautions

- **Weakest picks:** Kowaliga (the town declined before the dam) and Kinloch (bought out, not destroyed by force). Possible swaps: the Hill District, Pittsburgh (Civic Arena, 1950s) or Jackson Ward, Richmond (I-95). Neither was researched here.
