## Post text

She ran away from George Washington. And he never stopped hunting her.

Ona Judge was born enslaved at Mount Vernon around 1773. Her mother, Betty, was enslaved by Martha Washington; her father was a white English tailor. As a girl she was brought into the mansion, and she became Martha Washington's personal maid.

When George Washington became President, she went with the household to New York, and then to the President's House in Philadelphia. Pennsylvania had a law that freed enslaved people who lived in the state for six months in a row. The Washingtons sent the people they enslaved back and forth to Virginia so the clock never ran out.

In the spring of 1796, she learned that Martha Washington planned to give her away to her granddaughter. Ona later said: "I knew that if I went back to Virginia, I should never get my liberty."

So on May 21, 1796, while the President and the First Lady sat at dinner, she walked out of the house. Her things had already been carried to friends in Philadelphia's free Black community. They got her onto a ship captained by John Bowles, bound for Portsmouth, New Hampshire.

A runaway ad in the newspaper offered ten dollars to bring her back.

That summer, a senator's daughter recognized her on a street in Portsmouth. Washington asked the Treasury Department for help, and the customs collector, Joseph Whipple, tried to get her onto a ship back. She refused. She offered to return if the Washingtons promised to free her one day. Washington called that "totally inadmissible."

In 1799 he tried again, sending his nephew, Burwell Bassett Jr., to seize her. But Senator John Langdon secretly warned her, and she hid until he left empty-handed.

Four months later, George Washington died. Ona Judge was never caught.

She married a free Black sailor, Jack Staines, had three children, and taught herself to read. Late in life, a newspaper asked if she regretted running away. She answered: "No, I am free, and have, I trust, been made a child of God by the means."

She died in Greenland, New Hampshire, on February 25, 1848. By law, she was still a fugitive.
