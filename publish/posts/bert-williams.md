## Post text

He was the highest-paid Black entertainer in America. And every night, before he walked on stage, he had to paint his face black.

Bert Williams was born in Nassau, in the Bahamas, in 1874. His family moved to the United States when he was a boy, and as a teenager he joined the traveling minstrel shows. In 1893, in San Francisco, he met another young performer, George Walker. They became a team.

There was a catch. In those days, Black performers were expected to wear blackface, just like the white minstrels who mocked them. Bert was light-skinned. So before every show, he burned cork and smeared it over his face. Williams and Walker even billed themselves as "Two Real Coons", to set themselves apart from the white men in burnt cork. Off stage, they wore sharp suits and posed for elegant photographs.

In 1903 their show In Dahomey, written, directed and performed by an all-Black cast, became the first all-Black musical to play a major Broadway theater. But inside that theater, the seats were segregated. Then the show went to London, and the company gave a command performance at Buckingham Palace. In Scotland, Bert became a Freemason, because American lodges would not admit Black men.

In those years he introduced the song that followed him for the rest of his life: Nobody. Audiences demanded it at almost every show.

In 1909 George Walker collapsed on stage. He died in 1911. Bert went on alone, and many white performers refused to share a bill with him. On tour, he usually had to travel, eat and sleep apart from the rest of the company.

Then, in 1910, Florenz Ziegfeld hired him for the Follies, the most famous show on Broadway. He would be the only Black star in an all-white cast. Some of the cast demanded he be fired. Ziegfeld refused. He reportedly told them: "I can replace every one of you, except him."

Bert became the sensation of the show, and one of the best-selling recording artists of his time. He was the highest-paid Black entertainer in America.

But fame did not open every door. At his hotel, he was told to ride the freight elevator. He told his friend Eddie Cantor: "It wouldn't be so bad, Eddie, if I didn't still hear the applause ringing in my ears."

In 1916 he filmed his famous poker routine: a whole card game with no cards, no table and no other players, just one man under a light, playing every hand with his face.

In August 1919, Broadway's actors went on strike, and the whole Follies cast walked out. Nobody told Bert. He showed up for work and found an empty theater. After more than twenty years on the New York stage, the actors' union had never invited him to join.

In his only known essay, he wrote that he had never found anything disgraceful in being a colored man. "But I have often found it inconvenient - in America."

In February 1922, sick with pneumonia, he collapsed on stage in Detroit. At first, the audience thought it was part of the act. Backstage, he reportedly said: "That's a nice way to die. They was laughing when I made my last exit."

He died in New York on March 4, 1922. Thousands lined up to see his coffin. And the Masons, whose American lodges had shut him out, gave him a Masonic funeral. He was the first Black man buried with Masonic honors.

W. C. Fields called him "the funniest man I ever saw, and the saddest man I ever knew."
