## Post text

They called it Black Wall Street. In less than a day, it was gone.

In the early 1900s, oil turned Tulsa, Oklahoma, into a boom town. But Tulsa was segregated, and Black residents were shut out of the white part of town. So they built their own, north of the Frisco railroad tracks. Its name was Greenwood.

Black landowners such as O. W. Gurley and J. B. Stradford bought land there and sold lots to other Black families. By 1921, about 10,000 people lived in Greenwood. It had two newspapers, a public library branch, churches, grocers, barbers, tailors, cafés, doctors, lawyers, and a movie theater, the Dreamland. Greenwood Avenue became known as the "Negro Wall Street."

On May 31, 1921, a 19-year-old Black shoe shiner named Dick Rowland was arrested after an encounter with a white elevator operator downtown. That evening, a white crowd gathered at the courthouse. Armed Black men, many of them World War I veterans, came to keep him from being lynched. A shot went off, and the violence began.

At dawn on June 1, thousands of armed white men crossed the tracks into Greenwood. They looted homes and set them on fire. By the time martial law was declared that morning, most of the district was burning.

When it was over, 35 city blocks had burned. 1,256 homes were destroyed, and about 10,000 people were left homeless. The official death toll was 36. Historians believe it may have been as high as 300. Thousands spent the winter in tents. Claims worth more than $1.8 million were turned down. No one was ever sent to prison for it, and the charges against Dick Rowland were dropped.

Greenwood rebuilt, house by house, with little help from the city. But for decades the massacre was left out of the history books. In 2001, an Oklahoma commission's report recommended reparations. In January 2025, the U.S. Justice Department called it a coordinated, military-style attack.

They built it, and then they built it again.
