## Post text

On the night of June 22, 1938, seventy thousand people packed Yankee Stadium. Millions more sat by their radios, in America and in Germany. In the ring were Joe Louis and Max Schmeling, the German the Nazi regime had made into a national hero.

It was over in two minutes and four seconds.

Joe Louis was born in Alabama in 1914, the seventh of eight children. His parents were sharecroppers, and both were the children of people who had been enslaved. When Joe was two, his father was committed to a mental hospital.

In 1926 the family moved north to Detroit. There Joe found a boxing gym. As an amateur he won fifty of his fifty-four fights.

When he turned professional, his managers gave him rules. Never be photographed with a white woman. Never gloat over a beaten opponent. The last Black heavyweight champion, Jack Johnson, had enraged white America, and for years Black fighters had been kept away from the title. They wanted to give no one an excuse.

In 1936 Louis fought Schmeling for the first time, and was knocked out in the twelfth round. It was the first loss of his career. In Germany, Schmeling was celebrated as proof of the regime's ideas.

A year later, in Chicago, Louis won the heavyweight title. He was the first Black heavyweight champion in twenty-two years. But he still had one loss on his record.

Then came the rematch. Louis came out punching and never stopped. Schmeling went down, and his corner threw in the towel. In Harlem, people poured into the streets and celebrated through the night.

Louis held the title for almost twelve years and defended it twenty-five times. Both are still heavyweight records.

When America went to war, he gave the purses from two title fights to the Navy and Army relief funds. Then he joined the Army, an army that still kept Black and white soldiers apart. He boxed in almost a hundred exhibitions for the troops. The government put him on its posters.

And then the government sent him the bill.

The tax collectors counted the money he had given away as his income. He owed tax on money he never kept. With interest and penalties, his debt grew past one million dollars.

He retired as champion in 1949. But the debt didn't retire. So he came back to the ring in his late thirties. He lost to Ezzard Charles. In 1951, Rocky Marciano knocked him through the ropes. He never boxed again.

He became a professional wrestler. Later he worked as a greeter at Caesars Palace in Las Vegas, shaking hands with tourists.

One old opponent helped him. Max Schmeling, the man he beat in 1938, became his friend.

Joe Louis died on April 12, 1981. He was 66. President Reagan waived the rules so he could be buried at Arlington National Cemetery with full military honors. Max Schmeling was a pallbearer. The next year, Congress awarded him its Gold Medal.

He beat the Nazis' champion. He gave his purses to his country. And his country taxed him for it for the rest of his life.
