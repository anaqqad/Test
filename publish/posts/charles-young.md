## Post text

In June 1918, a 54-year-old Army colonel saddled his horse in Wilberforce, Ohio, and started riding east. The Army had just told him he was too sick to lead men in the First World War. He set out to prove them wrong.

His name was Charles Young.

He was born enslaved on March 12, 1864, in Mays Lick, Kentucky. Within a year, his father escaped across the Ohio River and joined the Union Army, in the 5th U.S. Colored Heavy Artillery. After the war, the family settled in Ripley, Ohio. Charles learned French and German, played the piano and the violin, and in 1881 graduated with honors from Ripley's high school, the only Black student in his class.

In 1884 he entered West Point. White cadets used "silencing": they would not speak to Black cadets unless they had to. He failed mathematics and had to repeat it. Near the end he failed an engineering course, studied all summer, and passed. In 1889 he graduated, only the third Black graduate in West Point's history. The next would not come until 1936.

He joined the 9th Cavalry, the Buffalo Soldiers. He taught military science at Wilberforce University, where he became close friends with W. E. B. Du Bois. In 1903 he led his troopers into the Sierra Nevada and became the first Black superintendent of a national park, at Sequoia. His soldiers built the road that first brought wagons into the Giant Forest.

He was one of the Army's first military attachés, in Haiti, where he mapped much of the country. In 1912 he became the first Black major in the regular Army, and went to Liberia to help train its Frontier Force. On April 1, 1916, in Mexico, he led a cavalry pistol charge at Agua Caliente that drove back about 150 of Pancho Villa's men. His squadron did not lose a single soldier.

When America entered the First World War in 1917, he was the highest-ranking Black officer in the Army. But some white officers said they would not take orders from a Black man, and President Woodrow Wilson wrote to his Secretary of War about it. That summer, an Army medical board found high blood pressure. Charles Young was promoted to colonel and, in the same stroke, retired as medically unfit.

So on June 6, 1918, he left Wilberforce on horseback. He rode through Ohio, West Virginia, Maryland and Virginia, and on June 22 he reached Washington: 497 miles. The Crisis reported that he "arrived in first class condition." The Secretary of War did not change the decision.

He was called back to duty on November 6, 1918, five days before the war ended. In 1920 he returned to Liberia. On January 8, 1922, he died in Lagos, Nigeria, of a kidney infection. A year later his body came home, and on June 1, 1923, he was given a funeral in the Memorial Amphitheater at Arlington, only the fourth soldier so honored. W. E. B. Du Bois said his life was "a triumph of tragedy."

In 2022, a century after his death, the Army promoted him to brigadier general.

Born enslaved. West Point graduate. Colonel. And a man who rode 497 miles to prove them wrong.
