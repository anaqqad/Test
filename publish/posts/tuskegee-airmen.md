## Post text

In 1925, a study at the Army War College claimed that Black men did not have what it takes to lead in the military, or to fly aircraft of any kind.

Twenty years later, Black American pilots in red-tailed fighters were escorting bombers all the way to Berlin.

In the 1930s, the Army Air Corps did not accept Black pilots. But Black Americans were already flying. Charles Alfred "Chief" Anderson earned his license in 1929, and in 1932 he became the first African American to hold a commercial pilot certificate.

In January 1941, the War Department announced the first all-Black flying unit in American history, the 99th Pursuit Squadron, to be trained at Tuskegee, Alabama. It was a test: could Black Americans lead, fly military aircraft and fight?

That spring, First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt visited Tuskegee and went up in a plane with Chief Anderson.

On July 19, 1941, the first class began. Thirteen men started and five finished. One of the five was Captain Benjamin O. Davis Jr. At West Point, the other cadets had refused to speak to him for four years. He had no roommate and he ate his meals alone. He still graduated 35th in a class of 276.

Nearly a thousand Black pilots trained at Tuskegee. And the Airmen were not only pilots. They were navigators, technicians, mechanics and parachute riggers, men and women.

In April 1943, the 99th shipped out for North Africa. That September, their own group commander reported that they lacked discipline, teamwork and aggressiveness. The whole program was at risk.

So the War Department ran a formal comparison. The report came out on March 30, 1944. The 99th had performed as well as the white squadrons it flew with.

In June 1944, the 99th joined the 332nd Fighter Group at Ramitelli, Italy, under Colonel Davis. They painted the tails of their planes red, and they went to work protecting American bombers.

You may have heard they never lost a bomber. That is a legend. The real record: 179 escort missions, bombers lost on seven of them, 27 bombers in all. Other P-51 groups in the same air force lost an average of 46.

On March 24, 1945, they flew about 1,600 miles there and back to Berlin, and they shot down three German jets.

In all, they flew 1,578 missions and 15,533 combat sorties. They destroyed 112 enemy aircraft in the air and 150 on the ground. More than 60 pilots were killed in action or in accidents, and 32 became prisoners of war.

And the other war was still going on at home.

In April 1945, at Freeman Field in Indiana, the commander set up two officers clubs, one white and one Black. Black officers of the 477th Bombardment Group began walking into the white club in small groups. Over two nights, 61 were arrested.

Then the Army ordered every officer to sign a new base regulation. 101 Black officers refused. They were arrested and flown to Kentucky. After pressure from the NAACP, the National Urban League and the Black press, they were released with reprimands. One officer, Roger Terry, was convicted and dishonorably discharged. He was pardoned in 1995.

On July 26, 1948, President Truman signed Executive Order 9981, ordering equal treatment in the armed forces. Davis went on to become the first Black general in the U.S. Air Force.

On March 29, 2007, about 350 Tuskegee Airmen and their widows received the Congressional Gold Medal at the U.S. Capitol.

They said Black men could not fly. So they flew.
