## Post text

On the night of February 29, 1940, Hollywood's biggest stars filled the Cocoanut Grove at the Ambassador Hotel in Los Angeles for the Academy Awards. One nominee was not seated with the rest of her film's stars. She sat at a small table against the far wall.

Then they read out her name. Hattie McDaniel had just become the first Black person ever to win an Oscar.

She was born in Wichita, Kansas, in the 1890s, the youngest of 13 children. Both of her parents had been born into slavery. Her father, Henry, had fought for the Union in the Civil War.

In the early 1900s the family moved to Denver. Hattie loved to perform. At East Denver High School she won a gold medal in a recitation contest. Her father and brothers started a minstrel show, and soon she was on the road with them, singing and dancing. She and her sister Etta even started a show of their own.

In the 1920s she toured with George Morrison's orchestra and sang on Denver radio. She is often called the first Black woman to sing on American radio.

Then her luck ran out. A touring show she was in went broke, and she was stranded in Milwaukee with no show and no money. The only work she could find was at Sam Pick's Club Madrid, a nightclub outside the city. Not on the stage, which was closed to Black performers, but in the washroom, as an attendant.

She talked the owner into letting her sing anyway. She became a regular.

In 1931 she moved to Los Angeles to try the movies. Hollywood had one kind of part for her, again and again: the maid, the cook, the mammy. In 1934 John Ford cast her opposite Will Rogers in Judge Priest, her first major role, and she sang a duet with him. She appeared in hundreds of films. In most of them, her name wasn't even on the screen.

When people criticized her for the roles she took, her reported answer became famous: why complain about making $700 a week playing a maid, when otherwise she'd be making $7 a week being one?

In 1939 she won the part of Mammy in Gone with the Wind. The film premiered in Atlanta on December 15, 1939, at Loew's Grand Theatre. Under Georgia's segregation laws the theater was for white audiences only, so none of the film's Black actors could go. Clark Gable was so angry he threatened to boycott the premiere. She convinced him to go anyway.

She was nominated for Best Supporting Actress, in the same category as her co-star Olivia de Havilland. The hotel where the ceremony was held did not normally admit Black guests. She was let in, but seated apart, on the far side of the room. She wore white gardenias in her hair.

In her speech she said she hoped she would always be a credit to her race and to the motion picture industry. Her award wasn't the golden statuette. Supporting winners then received a plaque. It would be half a century before another Black woman won an acting Oscar.

Not everyone celebrated. The NAACP and its leader Walter White criticized her for roles that kept racist stereotypes alive. She kept working. During World War II she led the Negro division of the Hollywood Victory Committee, bringing entertainment to Black soldiers.

By then she owned a large house in West Adams, a neighborhood so popular with Black stars that it was nicknamed Sugar Hill. In 1945 some white neighbors went to court to use racial covenants to force the Black homeowners out. She helped rally her neighbors to fight back, and the judge threw the case out.

In 1947 she took over the lead in the radio comedy Beulah, and later played Beulah on television too. But she managed only a few episodes. She had breast cancer.

Hattie McDaniel died on October 26, 1952. She was 59. About 3,000 mourners came to her funeral.

In her will she made one last request: to be buried in Hollywood Cemetery. The cemetery refused. It did not bury Black people. She was buried across town at Rosedale Cemetery.

She left her Oscar plaque to Howard University. Around 1970 it disappeared. It has never been found.

In 1999 the cemetery that refused her had a new owner, who offered to move her there. Her family said no, so a memorial to her was put up instead. In 2010, when Mo'Nique won the same Oscar, she wore a blue dress and a gardenia in her hair, as Hattie McDaniel had 70 years before. And in 2023 the Academy gave Howard University a replacement for her lost Oscar.
