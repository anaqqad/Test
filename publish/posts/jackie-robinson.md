## Post text

On July 6, 1944, an Army bus driver in Texas told a young Black lieutenant to move to the back of the bus. The lieutenant said no. Three years later, the whole country knew his name.

His name was Jackie Robinson. He was born on January 31, 1919, in Cairo, Georgia, the youngest of five children of sharecroppers. His father left the family, and his mother, Mallie, moved her children to Pasadena, California, and raised them on her own.

Sports ran in the family. His older brother Mack won the silver medal in the 200 meters at the 1936 Berlin Olympics, finishing right behind Jesse Owens.

Jackie went to UCLA and became the school's first athlete to earn varsity letters in four sports: baseball, basketball, football and track.

In 1942 he was drafted into the Army. Black soldiers like him were kept out of officer candidate school until the heavyweight champion Joe Louis, then stationed at the same base, protested. Robinson was commissioned a second lieutenant.

Then came the bus. When he refused to move, military police were called. He was court-martialed. In August 1944, a panel of nine white officers heard the case and found him not guilty. Later that year he left the Army with an honorable discharge.

In 1945 he played shortstop for the Kansas City Monarchs in the Negro leagues. That August, Branch Rickey of the Brooklyn Dodgers called him in for a meeting that lasted about three hours. Rickey said he needed a Black player "with guts enough not to fight back."

Robinson signed. In 1946 he played for the Montreal Royals and led the International League in hitting.

On April 15, 1947, he took the field for the Brooklyn Dodgers. Baseball's color line was broken.

It was brutal. On April 22, the Philadelphia Phillies manager Ben Chapman and his players shouted racial slurs at him from the dugout. Hate mail poured in. So did death threats.

He did not fight back. He hit, he ran, and he led the National League in stolen bases. At the end of the season he won baseball's first Rookie of the Year award. In 1949 he was the league's Most Valuable Player, and in 1955 the Dodgers won the World Series.

In 1962 he was elected to the Baseball Hall of Fame, the first Black player inducted.

Off the field, he kept fighting. In 1963 he marched on Washington with his son beside him.

In October 1972, he threw out the first pitch at the World Series. He said he would be prouder the day he saw a Black manager in baseball. Nine days later, on October 24, 1972, he died of a heart attack at his home in Connecticut. He was 53.

In 1997, fifty years after his first game, Major League Baseball retired his number, 42, for every team. Today, every April 15, every player in the major leagues wears it.
