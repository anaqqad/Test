## Post text

On June 12, 1956, one of the most famous men in the world sat in front of a committee of the United States Congress. They wanted to know about his politics, his travels and his passport, which the government had taken away six years earlier.

One of them asked why he didn't just go and stay in Russia.

Paul Robeson answered: "Because my father was a slave, and my people died to build this country, and I am going to stay here, and have a part of it just like you."

He was born in Princeton, New Jersey, on April 9, 1898. His father, William Drew Robeson, had escaped slavery as a teenager and became a minister. When Paul was six, his mother died in a fire.

In 1915 he won a scholarship to Rutgers. He was only the third Black student ever to enroll there, and the only one at the time.

At football tryouts, his own teammates broke his nose and dislocated his shoulder. He made the team anyway. He was named an All-American twice, joined Phi Beta Kappa, and graduated as class valedictorian.

He went on to law school at Columbia, paying his way by playing professional football on weekends. After graduating in 1923 he joined a New York law firm. But a white secretary refused to take dictation from him. He quit. Not just the job, but the law.

So he turned to the stage. In 1924 he starred in Eugene O'Neill's plays in New York. In 1925 he made his first film, Body and Soul, directed by Oscar Micheaux, and gave his first concert of spirituals.

His deep bass voice made him a star. In Show Boat he sang Ol' Man River, and it became his song for the rest of his life. In 1930 he played Othello in London. In 1943 he played it on Broadway, and the production ran 296 performances, still a record for Shakespeare on Broadway. He starred in films in America and Britain, including The Emperor Jones in 1933.

He used that fame. He sang for workers in shipyards, and spoke out against lynching and segregation. He also praised the Soviet Union, and in Cold War America, that made him a target.

In 1949 rioters shut down his concert in Peekskill, New York.

In 1950 the State Department cancelled his passport. He could no longer perform abroad. At home he was blacklisted. His records and films were pulled, and his income fell from over a hundred thousand dollars a year to almost nothing.

In January 1952 he tried to cross into Canada to sing for a miners' union in Vancouver. No passport was needed to enter Canada back then. He was turned back at the border anyway.

So the union came to him. On May 18, 1952, at the Peace Arch on the border, he stood on a flatbed truck on the American side and sang across the line to tens of thousands of people on both sides.

Four years later came the hearing in Congress. He refused to say whether he was a Communist. And before it was over, he told the committee: "You are the un-Americans, and you ought to be ashamed of yourselves."

In 1958, after a Supreme Court ruling, he finally got his passport back.

But his health broke down, and his career never recovered. He died in Philadelphia on January 23, 1976. He was 77.

In 1995 he was finally named to the College Football Hall of Fame.
