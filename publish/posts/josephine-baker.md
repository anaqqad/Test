## Post text

She carried secrets for France past the Nazis, written in invisible ink on her sheet music. Years later, in New York, a nightclub would not serve her dinner.

Her name was Josephine Baker. She was born Freda Josephine McDonald on June 3, 1906, in St. Louis, Missouri. Her family was poor, and as a child she worked cleaning houses for white families.

In 1917, when she was eleven, she watched East St. Louis burn from across the Mississippi, as white mobs attacked the Black people who lived there. She never forgot it.

She was married at thirteen. By fifteen she had married again, to Willie Baker, and she kept his name for the rest of her life.

She wanted to dance. She tried out for Shuffle Along, the hit all-Black musical on Broadway, and was turned down, reportedly as too young, too skinny and too dark. She took a job as a dresser instead, learned every routine, and danced her way into the chorus line.

In 1925, at nineteen, she sailed for Paris. On October 2 she opened in La Revue Nègre at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, and Paris went wild.

A year later she was the star of the Folies Bergère, dancing in her famous skirt made of bananas. Ernest Hemingway called her the most sensational woman anyone ever saw. She became the most successful American entertainer in France. In 1937 she became a French citizen.

Then, in 1939, war came. French military intelligence recruited her as an "honorable correspondent". A famous star could travel, attend embassy parties and cross borders without suspicion.

She wrote secret information in invisible ink on her sheet music. Other notes she pinned inside her underwear. Who would search a star?

She joined the women's auxiliary of the Free French Air Force as a sub-lieutenant and sang for Allied troops in North Africa. After the war, France gave her the Resistance Medal, and later the Croix de Guerre and the Legion of Honour.

But in her own country, fame did not protect her. In October 1951, at New York's Stork Club, she said the staff refused to serve her. She publicly criticized the powerful columnist Walter Winchell, who was there that night, and the NAACP picketed the club.

That same year, a club in Miami Beach offered her a big contract. She said she would only perform if Black and white fans could sit together. The club agreed, and she played to an integrated audience.

On August 28, 1963, at the March on Washington, she stood at the microphone in her Free French uniform, wearing her medals. She was the only woman on the official speaker list.

After Martin Luther King was killed in 1968, Coretta Scott King reportedly asked her to take his place at the head of the movement. She said no. Her children, she said, were too young to lose their mother.

She had adopted twelve children from around the world. She called them her Rainbow Tribe, and raised them at Les Milandes, a castle in the south of France. But the costs ruined her. In 1969 she was thrown out of her own home.

Her friend Princess Grace of Monaco gave her a place to live. In April 1975, with Grace's help, she opened a new show in Paris, celebrating fifty years on stage. Four days later she died. More than twenty thousand people filled the streets for her funeral, and France gave her full military honors.

In 2021 France placed her in the Panthéon, the resting place of its heroes. She was the first Black woman ever honored there.
