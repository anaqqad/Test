## Post text

On the night of July 17, 1944, at Port Chicago, California, two explosions destroyed a Navy ammunition pier in seconds. Three hundred twenty people were killed. Two hundred two of them were Black sailors.

The Navy was segregated then. Black sailors were barred from nearly all jobs at sea. At Port Chicago, most of the enlisted men were Black and the officers were white. Those Black sailors had the dangerous work of moving bombs and shells out of rail cars and into the holds of ships, with little training in handling explosives.

In April 1944, a new commander, Captain Merrill Kinne, took over. Officers pushed crews to load faster. Divisions competed against each other, and the tonnage was kept on a chalkboard.

On July 13, the Liberty ship E. A. Bryan tied up at the pier. At 10:18 p.m. on July 17, there was a blast on the pier. Between five and seven seconds later came a much larger explosion. An Army pilot reported a ball of fire three miles wide. The pier and the E. A. Bryan were gone, and what remained of a second ship, the Quinault Victory, lay in the bay.

White sailors were given leave afterward. Black sailors were ordered back to work, to clean up the wreckage. A Navy board of inquiry laid the blame on the way the enlisted men had handled the ammunition.

On August 9, 1944, at Mare Island, 328 survivors were ordered to load ammunition again. They had been told nothing clear about what caused the explosions and had been given no new safety training. Two hundred fifty-eight refused.

They were held on a prison barge. Admiral Carleton Wright warned that continued refusal would be treated as mutinous conduct. Most went back to work, and 208 were convicted of disobeying orders in summary courts-martial.

Fifty were singled out and charged with mutiny, a crime that could carry the death penalty. They became known as the Port Chicago 50.

The trial began on September 14, 1944, on Yerba Buena Island in San Francisco Bay. It was the largest mutiny trial in U.S. Navy history. All 50 pleaded not guilty.

On October 9, Thurgood Marshall of the NAACP came to watch. He argued that these men were not mutineers but victims of gross negligence and racial injustice, and he called for a government investigation.

On October 24, all 50 were found guilty. They were sentenced to 8 to 15 years of hard labor, with dishonorable discharges.

Marshall took their appeal to Washington in April 1945. The convictions were reaffirmed. In January 1946, after the war ended, the Navy announced that 47 of the 50 were being released. That February, the Navy began to desegregate its forces.

But the convictions stood. A campaign in Congress failed in 1990, and the Navy refused again in 1994. In 1999, President Clinton pardoned one of the men, Freddie Meeks.

On July 17, 2024, exactly 80 years after the explosion, Navy Secretary Carlos Del Toro announced that the Navy was exonerating 256 of the sailors, including the Port Chicago 50. The Navy's lawyers found that the men had been tried together, had no meaningful right to counsel, and had been tried before the investigation of the explosion was finished.

None of the 50 was still alive to hear it.

They asked why. It took 80 years to answer.
