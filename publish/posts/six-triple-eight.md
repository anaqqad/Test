## Post text

In February 1945, warehouses in Birmingham, England, held about 17 million letters and packages that no one had delivered. They were addressed to American soldiers fighting in Europe, and some had been sitting there for months. The Army believed it was hurting morale.

The Army estimated the job would take six months. It sent 855 Black women to do it. They did it in three.

They were the 6888th Central Postal Directory Battalion, the Six Triple Eight.

Black women served in the Women's Army Corps, but for most of the war the Army kept them at home. Mary McLeod Bethune and First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt pushed for a role for them overseas. In 1944, the War Department created the 6888th, led by Major Charity Adams, the highest-ranking Black woman officer in the Army.

They sailed on February 3, 1945, landed in Scotland, and went on to Birmingham. The battalion ran itself, with its own cooks, mechanics, medics and military police.

The mail was stacked in cold, dark buildings with blacked-out windows. Rats had gotten into food packages. Letters were addressed to a first name, a nickname, or just "Junior, U.S. Army." About 7 million Americans were in Europe, and they kept moving. By one account, 7,500 of them were named Robert Smith.

So the women built a system of index cards with serial numbers and units, eventually 7 million cards. They worked three eight-hour shifts, seven days a week, about 65,000 pieces of mail per shift. Their motto: "No mail, low morale."

When a visiting general threatened to send a white first lieutenant "to show you how to run things," Adams answered: "Over my dead body, sir." Her staff found an official memo against language that stressed racial segregation, and the general backed down. When the Red Cross tried to put the women in a separate hotel in London, she refused that too.

By May 1945, the Birmingham backlog was gone. The battalion moved to Rouen, France, marched in a parade honoring Joan of Arc, and cleared the mail there too. In July 1945, three of the women, Mary Bankston, Mary Barlow and Dolores Browne, were killed in a jeep accident. They rest at the Normandy American Cemetery.

In October the unit moved to Paris. In February 1946 the women came home. There was no parade.

Many went to college on the GI Bill and became teachers, nurses and civil rights workers. Charity Adams left the Army as a lieutenant colonel and wrote her memoir, One Woman's Army, in 1989.

The country remembered slowly: a monument at Fort Leavenworth in 2018, a unit commendation in 2019, and in 2022 a House vote of 422 to 0 for the Congressional Gold Medal. In 2023, Fort Lee in Virginia was renamed Fort Gregg-Adams, in part for her. On April 29, 2025, the medal was presented at the U.S. Capitol, and her son accepted it on her behalf.

No mail, low morale. They made sure the mail got through.
