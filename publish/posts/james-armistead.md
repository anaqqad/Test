## Post text

In 1781, a British army under Lord Cornwallis was marching through Virginia, and an American army under the Marquis de Lafayette was trying to stop it. Lafayette needed to know what the British were planning.

One of the men he relied on was enslaved. His name was James.

James was born around 1748, most likely in New Kent County, Virginia, and was enslaved by William Armistead. In the summer of 1781 he went to serve Lafayette. On paper he was a servant. In fact, he was a spy.

Most accounts say he posed as a runaway, guided British troops on the local roads, and reported where they were posted and what arms they had. Many say he even served at Cornwallis's headquarters as a double agent. The surviving papers don't confirm that part, but his own petition says he went into the British camp again and again, at the risk of his life, carrying Lafayette's secret messages.

In late August, Lafayette learned that Cornwallis had moved his army to Yorktown. There, Washington, Rochambeau and a French fleet trapped him. On October 19, 1781, Cornwallis surrendered.

After the war, Virginia freed enslaved men who had served as soldiers. James had not carried a gun. He had been a spy. He stayed enslaved.

So on November 21, 1784, Lafayette wrote for him: "His intelligences from the enemy's camp were industriously collected and most faithfully delivered." His first petition still failed. In 1786 he tried again, and on January 9, 1787, Virginia set him free. He took a new last name: Lafayette.

He farmed about 40 acres in New Kent County and raised a family. In 1824, during Lafayette's farewell tour, the old general saw him in a crowd, called him by name, and embraced him. James Lafayette died in 1830.

He helped win the war, and then had to win his own freedom.
