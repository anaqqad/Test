## Post text

In the 1920s, she was the biggest blues star in America. When she died, her grave got no headstone. It stayed that way for thirty-three years.

Bessie Smith was born in Chattanooga, Tennessee, in the early 1890s. Her father, a part-time Baptist preacher, died when she was too young to remember him. By the time she was nine, her mother was dead too, and her older sister Viola raised the children.

There was no money for school. So Bessie and her brother Andrew went to the streets. He played the guitar, and she sang and danced for pennies in front of the White Elephant Saloon.

In 1912 she joined a traveling show as a dancer. The star singer was Ma Rainey, the Mother of the Blues. The two stayed friends for life.

In 1923, Columbia Records signed her. Her very first record, Downhearted Blues, sold about 780,000 copies in six months. She went on to make about 160 records, with musicians like Louis Armstrong.

She became the highest-paid Black entertainer of her day, earning up to $2,000 a week, and toured the South in her own 72-foot railroad car.

In July 1927, her tent show was playing in Concord, North Carolina, when hooded Klansmen came to pull up the tent stakes. Her musicians ran. Bessie walked out to the Klansmen alone, cursed them, and told them to leave. They did. Then she went back inside and finished the show.

In 1929 she made her only film, St. Louis Blues. It is the only time we can see and hear her at once.

Then the Depression hit. In 1931 Columbia dropped her, and she had to sell her railroad car. But she kept singing, and by 1937 people were talking about a comeback.

On September 26, 1937, her car hit a truck on Highway 61 in Mississippi. Her right arm was almost torn off. She died that morning at the Black hospital in Clarksdale. The story that a white hospital turned her away was later shown to be false.

In Philadelphia, about ten thousand people filed past her coffin. But her estranged husband, Jack Gee, blocked every attempt to buy a headstone, and reportedly pocketed money raised for one.

In 1970, Janis Joplin, who idolized her, split the cost of a stone with Juanita Green, who had done housework for Bessie as a girl. It reads: "The Greatest Blues Singer in the World Will Never Stop Singing."

Two months later, Janis Joplin was dead.
