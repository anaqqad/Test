## Post text

On the morning of December 7, 1941, Doris Miller was collecting laundry on the battleship USS West Virginia at Pearl Harbor. He was 22 years old, and the Navy had trained him to serve meals.

Before the morning was over, he was firing a machine gun at Japanese planes.

He was born in Waco, Texas, on October 12, 1919, one of four sons in a farming family. He played fullback in high school, but he left without graduating. His family needed the money, and there were few good jobs around Waco for a young Black man.

On September 16, 1939, two weeks after war broke out in Europe, he signed up with the Navy in Dallas. He was 19.

The Navy had a separate place for men like him. Black recruits went into the messman branch, where they fed and served the white officers. White sailors were not allowed in that branch, and Black sailors had almost no way out of it.

In January 1940 he joined the West Virginia. He became the ship's heavyweight boxing champion. If the alarm ever rang, his job was to go below decks to an ammunition magazine.

Then came that Sunday morning.

Torpedoes hit the West Virginia and water poured in. Miller ran to his battle station and found it destroyed, so he started helping injured men. An officer sent him to the bridge, where the ship's captain, Mervyn Bennion, had been badly wounded. Miller and others carried him to a safer spot. The captain did not survive.

Near the conning tower stood .50-caliber anti-aircraft machine guns. Miller was told to help feed them ammunition. Then he took one of the guns himself.

He had never been trained on it. He fired until the ammunition ran out.

Later, he made it sound simple: "I just pulled the trigger, and she worked fine."

How many planes he hit is still argued over. He believed he hit one.

The West Virginia settled onto the bottom of the harbor, and Miller helped pull sailors from the water. More than a hundred men of his ship were lost.

Soon a story spread about a Black sailor who had manned a machine gun. On January 1, 1942, the Navy commended him, but it did not give his name.

Black Americans wanted to know who he was. The Pittsburgh Courier, one of the country's leading Black newspapers, went looking. In March 1942 it printed the name: Dorie Miller. The name stuck.

Bills went to Congress asking for the Medal of Honor. Navy Secretary Frank Knox recommended against it. Instead, on May 27, 1942, Admiral Chester Nimitz pinned the Navy Cross on him aboard the carrier USS Enterprise. Miller was the first Black sailor to receive it.

He was now a national hero, and he was still a mess attendant.

The Navy sent him on a tour to speak and help sell war bonds. In January 1943 he stood before the first class of Black recruits to graduate from Camp Robert Smalls at Great Lakes, Illinois. That year his face went on a Navy recruiting poster.

Then he went back to sea, on the new escort carrier USS Liscome Bay, promoted to cook, third class.

On November 24, 1943, off Makin in the Pacific, a Japanese submarine torpedoed the ship. The bombs aboard exploded, and she sank in about 23 minutes. More than 600 men were lost. Dorie Miller was one of them. He was 24, and his body was never recovered.

Four months later, the Navy commissioned its first 13 Black officers.

In 1973 the frigate USS Miller was commissioned in his name. In 2020 the Navy announced that a future aircraft carrier would be named USS Doris Miller. In 2026, reports said the Navy was weighing a new name for that ship and that the Medal of Honor was under consideration. So far, nothing has been announced.

The first bills for his Medal of Honor went to Congress in 1942. People are still asking.

Nobody had trained him for that gun. He fired back anyway.
