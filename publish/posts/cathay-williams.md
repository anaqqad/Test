## Post text

On November 15, 1866, a new recruit enlisted in the U.S. Army in St. Louis, Missouri, for three years. The name on the papers was William Cathay. The medical exam was only a quick look.

William Cathay was a woman. Her name was Cathay Williams.

She was born in Independence, Missouri, in the 1840s, to a free father and an enslaved mother, so under the law she was enslaved too. In 1861 Union troops occupied Jefferson City, and she went to work for the Union army as a cook and laundress, at about seventeen. Later she worked for the staff of General Philip Sheridan.

After the war she was free, poor and on her own. Women could not enlist. So she enlisted as a man, in the 38th U.S. Infantry, Company A, one of the six new Black regiments Congress created in 1866, the soldiers who became known as the Buffalo Soldiers. Only two people in the regiment knew: "a cousin and a particular friend."

Why did she do it? "I wanted to make my own living and not be dependent on relations or friends."

Her company marched more than 500 miles from Kansas to Fort Union, New Mexico, then on to Fort Cummings. She was sick often: four hospitals, five stays. No one found out.

In 1868, at Fort Bayard, New Mexico, the post surgeon did. On October 14, 1868, she was discharged with a certificate of disability. The paper never said she was a woman. It still called her "he."

She worked as a cook at Fort Union, then as a laundress and seamstress in Trinidad, Colorado. In 1876 she told her story to the St. Louis Daily Times.

In June 1891, old and sick, she applied for an Army pension. A Pension Bureau doctor found her toes had been amputated and she could walk only with a crutch. Her claim was rejected. No one knows when she died, or where she is buried.

Today a bronze bust honors her in Leavenworth, Kansas. She is the only woman known to have served as a Buffalo Soldier.

On Veterans Day, remember Private Cathay Williams, 38th U.S. Infantry.
