## Post text

On Easter Sunday, April 9, 1939, Marian Anderson stood on the steps of the Lincoln Memorial and faced about 75,000 people. She was there because a concert hall in the same city had refused to let her sing.

She was born in Philadelphia on February 27, 1897. Her family belonged to Union Baptist Church, and she sang there as a child.

In the early 1920s she applied to the Philadelphia Music Academy. The school turned her away because she was Black.

She kept going anyway. Her church community raised money for private lessons, and she went on to study with the voice teacher Giuseppe Boghetti.

Through the 1920s and 1930s she sang regularly in Europe. In 1930, in Scandinavia, she met the pianist Kosti Vehanen, who became her accompanist for the next decade. By the end of the 1930s she was a celebrated singer on both sides of the Atlantic.

In 1939, Howard University wanted her for its concert series in Washington, D.C. She was too popular for a campus venue, so the university turned to the city's largest hall: Constitution Hall, owned by the Daughters of the American Revolution.

The DAR said no. The hall had a rule that only white artists could perform there.

Howard then tried the auditorium at Central High School, a whites-only school. That fell through as well.

Now the story was bigger than one booking. Activists organized protests and petitions. On February 26, First Lady Eleanor Roosevelt wrote to the DAR and resigned her membership, telling them they had a chance to lead and had failed to take it. The next day she brought the issue to readers across the country in her newspaper column, My Day.

The NAACP's Walter White and Anderson's manager, Sol Hurok, pushed for something no one could ignore: an open-air concert at the Lincoln Memorial. Interior Secretary Harold Ickes made it happen. It would be free.

Anderson did not agree easily. She was a private person, and she wanted to sing, not lead a fight. But she understood what the moment had made of her. In her memoir she wrote: "I had become, whether I liked it or not, a symbol, representing my people."

On Easter Sunday, the crowd spread out across the National Mall, Black and white together. Millions more listened on the radio. Ickes introduced her and spoke against prejudice in America.

Then, with Kosti Vehanen at the piano, she began with "My Country, 'Tis of Thee."

She sang an aria by Donizetti and Schubert's "Ave Maria," then spirituals, and she finished with "Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen."

The story did not end on those steps.

On January 7, 1943, she finally sang at Constitution Hall, at the DAR's invitation, in a wartime benefit. She agreed on one condition: the audience had to be integrated. Eleanor Roosevelt came.

Twelve years later to the day, on January 7, 1955, she walked onto the stage of the Metropolitan Opera in New York as Ulrica in Verdi's "Un ballo in maschera." She was 57, and she was the first Black American to sing with the company.

She later served with the U.S. delegation to the United Nations. In 1963 she sang at the March on Washington, and that December she received the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

Marian Anderson died in Portland, Oregon, on April 8, 1993, at the age of 96.

One hall closed its doors to her. She never needed it. The whole country came to her instead.

## Fact notes

- Birth, death, Met debut, 1943 and 1963 dates: Wikipedia and AP (key events timeline, 2024).
- Music school refusal: Wikipedia and AP. The often-quoted wording of the refusal is left out: only one source had it.
- Church fundraising: sources differ on who led it, so the post says "her church community".
- 1943 concert: sources disagree on the charity (Red Cross, United China Relief), so the post says "wartime benefit". Integrated audience as her condition: NPS; integrated audience: AP.
- Central High School: DAR archives, Howard records and others give a messy timeline (superintendent refused, a conditional offer, then withdrawn), so the post says "fell through".
- Program: Library of Congress and PBS; the closing "Nobody Knows the Trouble I've Seen": Library of Congress and National Archives.
- Memoir quote: Library of Congress and Newsweek/Milestone Documents, from "My Lord, What a Morning".
- Left out, single source or disputed: the Toscanini quote, the Ickes quotes (wording not confirmed), Martin Luther King hearing the broadcast, concert length.
