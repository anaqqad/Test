## Post text

In 1937, a young actress left her husband in Austria and fled, first to Paris and then to London. Within a few years she would be one of the most famous faces in Hollywood. And she would also hold a patent for a secret military invention.

She was born Hedwig Kiesler in Vienna, the only child of a wealthy, assimilated Jewish family. Her father was a banker. Her mother was a concert pianist.

At eighteen she starred in a Czech film called Ecstasy. Its nude scenes caused a scandal. It was banned in the United States and in Germany.

That same year, 1933, she married Fritz Mandl, a rich arms manufacturer with ties to Mussolini's Italy and to the Nazis.

He was jealous and controlling. He made her stop acting, and tried to buy up every copy of Ecstasy.

But at his dinner table she listened. He talked weapons with scientists and military men, and she was learning.

In 1937 she ran. In London she met Louis B. Mayer, the head of MGM. He signed her and gave her a new name: Hedy Lamarr.

Hollywood sold her as the most beautiful woman in the world. She starred opposite Clark Gable and Spencer Tracy in Boom Town, and opposite James Stewart in Come Live With Me.

When America went to war, she wanted to do more than act. She teamed up with a friend, the composer George Antheil.

Radio-guided torpedoes had a weakness. An enemy could jam the signal and send them off course. Her idea was to make the signal hop from frequency to frequency, in a pattern known only to the sender and the torpedo.

Antheil had synchronized player pianos for his music. So they used something like a piano roll to keep both ends hopping in step, across 88 frequencies, as many as the keys on a piano.

They filed the patent in June 1941. On August 11, 1942, U.S. Patent 2,292,387, "Secret Communication System," was granted, under her married name, Hedy Kiesler Markey.

They offered it to the Navy. The Navy shelved it. The device was reportedly called too bulky to fit inside a torpedo.

Instead, she was told she could help the war effort by selling war bonds. She sold millions of dollars' worth.

She kept making films, like Samson and Delilah in 1949.

The patent expired. Then the military began using frequency hopping, including on ships during the Cuban Missile Crisis in 1962. Today frequency hopping, in newer forms, is part of Bluetooth, and it was used in early Wi-Fi.

She never earned a cent from it.

In 1997, more than fifty years after the patent, she was finally honored with a Pioneer Award from the Electronic Frontier Foundation. When she heard, she reportedly said: "It's about time."

Hedy Lamarr died in Florida in January 2000. She was 85. Part of her ashes were scattered in the Vienna Woods, near the city she had fled. In 2014 she was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame, and the rest of her ashes were laid in an honorary grave in Vienna.
