## Post text

On October 22, 1926, in a dressing room at the Princess Theatre in Montreal, a McGill student asked Harry Houdini a question. Was it true that punches to the stomach didn't hurt him?

Then, before Houdini could brace himself, the student struck him, again and again.

Nine days later, on Halloween, the most famous escape artist in the world was dead. But to understand his last years, you have to go back to a séance, and to his mother.

He was born Erik Weisz in Budapest on March 24, 1874, the son of a rabbi, Mayer Samuel Weisz. When he was four, the family sailed to America and settled in Appleton, Wisconsin, where his father led a small Reform congregation.

Then his father lost the job. The family fell into poverty, and the boy went to work to help feed them.

He loved magic. As a young performer he took a new name from his French hero, the magician Robert-Houdin. Erik Weisz became Harry Houdini.

In Europe he became the Handcuff King. He dared the police to lock him in their jails, and he walked out. He escaped from straitjackets while hanging upside down. In Berlin in 1912 he introduced his Water Torture Cell: locked upside down in a tank of water.

He became one of the highest-paid entertainers in the world, and starred in his own films, like The Master Mystery, The Man from Beyond and Haldane of the Secret Service.

But the person he loved most was his mother, Cecilia. On July 17, 1913, while he was touring Europe, she died. When the news reached him, he reportedly fainted.

He later wrote that losing her sent him searching for a medium who could truly reach the dead. Some historians doubt it was the whole reason, but it is how he told it.

In 1922, in Atlantic City, his friend Arthur Conan Doyle, the creator of Sherlock Holmes and a devoted spiritualist, arranged a séance for him. Lady Doyle wrote out a message she said came from his mother.

The message began with a sign of the cross. His mother was a rabbi's wife. And it was written in fluent English, a language she had barely spoken.

Houdini was sure it wasn't her. The friendship with Conan Doyle ended. And Houdini became the mediums' worst enemy.

He went to séances in disguise. On stage, he showed audiences how the rapping, the moaning voices and the floating objects were faked.

He sat on a Scientific American committee that offered a cash prize to any medium who could prove real powers. No one ever won it, not even Mina Crandon, the famous Boston medium known as Margery.

In 1926 he testified before Congress in support of a bill against fortune-telling for money in Washington, D.C.

Then came Montreal. After the punches he was in terrible pain. A doctor found appendicitis and urged surgery. He went on with the show. On October 24, at the Garrick Theater in Detroit, he took the stage with a fever of 104 degrees.

His appendix had burst. Harry Houdini died of peritonitis at Grace Hospital in Detroit on October 31, 1926. He was 52. Doctors still argue whether the punches caused it.

He was buried in the family plot at Machpelah, a Jewish cemetery in Queens, New York. The New York Times reported that his head was pillowed on the letters his mother had written him.

Before he died, he and his wife Bess had agreed on a secret code: "Rosabelle, believe." Every Halloween for ten years, Bess held a séance to hear it. In 1936, after the last one, she said Houdini had not come through.
