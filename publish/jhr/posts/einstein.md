## Post text

In January 1933, Albert Einstein was in California, working at Caltech in Pasadena. Back in Germany, Adolf Hitler had just taken power.

Einstein never went home again.

He was born in Ulm, Germany, on March 14, 1879, into a secular Jewish family.

After university, he couldn't find a teaching job. So he became a clerk at the Swiss patent office in Bern. And in 1905, still working there, he published four papers that changed physics, including his theory of relativity.

In 1919, a British expedition photographed a solar eclipse, and its measurements backed his new theory of gravity. Almost at once, he became the most famous scientist in the world. Soon after, he won the Nobel Prize in Physics.

But in Germany, he was also a Jew.

In August 1920, a far-right activist named Paul Weyland filled the Berlin Philharmonic hall for a rally against relativity. Antisemitic pamphlets and swastika pins were handed out.

He spent the winters from 1930 to 1933 at Caltech. That is where the news found him in 1933.

Back home, the Nazis seized his property. They even took his sailboat.

That March, when his ship reached Belgium, he went to a German consulate, handed in his passport and gave up his German citizenship.

In May 1933, his writings were thrown onto the bonfires of the Nazi book burnings. A Nazi publication reportedly printed his photograph among the regime's enemies, with the caption "not yet hanged."

That October he settled in Princeton, New Jersey, at the new Institute for Advanced Study. In 1940 he became an American citizen.

In 1939 he had signed a letter to President Franklin Roosevelt, warning that Germany might build an atomic bomb. But in 1940 the Army denied him a security clearance because of his pacifist views. He played no part in building the bomb.

The country that took him in did not fully trust him either.

J. Edgar Hoover's FBI had opened a file on him in December 1932, before he even moved to America. It began with a letter from the Woman Patriot Corporation, a group that accused him of radicalism and asked that he be barred from the country.

Hoover suspected him of being a communist. In 1950, after Einstein spoke out on television against the hydrogen bomb, Hoover had agents hunt for proof that he was a Soviet spy. He reportedly even wanted Einstein stripped of his citizenship and deported.

They never found any proof. By the time Einstein died, his file ran to more than 1,400 pages.

In 1952, the young state of Israel offered him its presidency. He said no. He wrote that he was deeply moved, but that he lacked the natural aptitude and the experience to deal properly with people.

Albert Einstein died in Princeton in April 1955. He was 76.

Today his FBI file is public. Anyone can read it.

Had you heard this side of his story?
