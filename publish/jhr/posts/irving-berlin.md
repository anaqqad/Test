## Post text

On November 10, 1938, the singer Kate Smith stepped up to a radio microphone and sang a song nobody had heard before. It was called "God Bless America."

The night before, synagogues across Germany had been set on fire. It was Kristallnacht.

The man who wrote the song was a Jewish immigrant. His name was Irving Berlin.

He was born Israel Beilin in 1888, in the Russian Empire. His father was a poor cantor, who sang the prayers in the synagogue. To escape the pogroms, the family left everything behind.

In September 1893 they landed at Ellis Island. Israel was five years old.

Home was a crowded tenement on Cherry Street, on the Lower East Side of New York. In 1901 his father died. The boy sold newspapers and sang in the streets and saloons.

As a teenager he worked as a singing waiter at a cafe in Chinatown. In 1907 his first song was published. The sheet music misspelled his name as "I. Berlin." He kept it.

He never learned to read music. He picked out his tunes on the black keys, in a single key, F sharp, and later used special pianos with a lever that shifted the keyboard into any key he wanted. Others wrote his melodies down for him.

In 1911, "Alexander's Ragtime Band" made him famous.

The next year he married Dorothy Goetz. On their honeymoon in Havana she caught typhoid fever. Within six months of the wedding, she was dead. His grief became his first ballad, "When I Lost You."

In 1918, newly an American citizen, he was drafted. At Camp Upton, in Yaphank, New York, he wrote an all-soldier show, "Yip Yip Yaphank," and played a grumbling soldier who hated the morning bugle.

He also wrote a solemn hymn for the finale. He decided it was too solemn, and put it away. For twenty years.

In 1926 he married Ellin Mackay, a wealthy Catholic socialite. Her father opposed the match. They married anyway.

By 1938, war was coming again, and Kate Smith needed a song for Armistice Day. Berlin took out the old hymn and changed some of its words. The 1918 draft had asked God to make America "victorious on land and foam." Now, with Europe sliding toward war, it became a song of peace. Within months, people were calling it a second national anthem.

Then came the backlash. The Ku Klux Klan and American Nazi groups called for a boycott of the song. Their reason: the man who asked God to bless America was a Jewish immigrant.

Berlin's answer was to give the song away. In 1940 he set up the God Bless America Fund, so that all of its royalties would go to the Boy Scouts and Girl Scouts. They still do.

When America went to war again, he wrote a new soldier show, "This Is the Army." It opened on Broadway on July 4, 1942, with a cast of about 300 real soldiers, and the profits went to Army Emergency Relief. In the 1943 film, at 55, he put on a World War One uniform and sang his old morning song himself. He followed the show to the war zones, from London and North Africa to Italy and the Pacific, singing it at every performance. The show and the film raised about ten million dollars for the Army.

Congress later gave him a gold medal for his patriotic songs, "God Bless America" among them.

And in 1949, the boy from Ellis Island set the words on the Statue of Liberty to music: "Give me your tired, your poor." They were written by another American Jew, the poet Emma Lazarus.

Irving Berlin died in New York in 1989. He was 101.

A cantor's son who never read a note. A refugee from Ellis Island. And when he wrote his new country a prayer, there were people who said he had no right to.
