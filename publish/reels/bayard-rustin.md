Post text:
Martin Luther King Jr. had the dream. Bayard Rustin built the march.

In 1947, years before the Freedom Rides, Rustin joined the Journey of Reconciliation, riding buses through the South to challenge segregation on interstate travel. He was arrested in North Carolina and sentenced to a chain gang. His account of it helped end the chain gang there.

During the Montgomery bus boycott, Rustin went to Alabama and became a close adviser to a young Martin Luther King Jr., helping him put the ideas of nonviolence into practice.

But Bayard Rustin was gay. In 1953 he had been arrested in Pasadena, California, and convicted under laws used against gay men. Some civil rights leaders feared his private life would be used to attack the movement, and they kept him in the background.

In the summer of 1963, he was given less than two months to organize the March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom. On August 28, 1963, more than 250,000 people came to Washington. It was one of the largest demonstrations the country had ever seen.

Days before the march, Senator Strom Thurmond attacked Rustin on the Senate floor, calling him a communist, a draft dodger and a homosexual. Rustin's official title was deputy director, and for decades his name stayed in the shadows.

In 2013, fifty years after the march, he was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom. And in 2020, the governor of California pardoned him for his 1953 conviction.

King had the dream. Bayard Rustin built the march.
