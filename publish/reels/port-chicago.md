Post text:
They refused to load bombs, and faced mutiny.

Port Chicago, California, 1944. The Navy was segregated. Black sailors, with little training, were given the job of loading bombs onto ships.

On July 17, 1944, the pier exploded. 320 people were killed. 202 were Black sailors. White sailors got leave. Black sailors did not.

Three weeks later, the survivors were ordered to load ammunition again. 258 refused. Most went back. Fifty were tried for mutiny, a crime that could carry the death penalty.

Thurgood Marshall came to watch the trial, and called them victims of negligence and racial injustice. All fifty were found guilty, and sentenced to as much as 15 years of hard labor.

Most were released in 1946, and that year the Navy began to desegregate. But the convictions stood.

On July 17, 2024, eighty years to the day, the Navy finally exonerated them. None of the fifty was still alive.

They asked why. It took eighty years to answer.

Had you heard of the Port Chicago 50?
