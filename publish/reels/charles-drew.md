Post text:
He banked America's blood. Then they segregated it.

Dr. Charles Drew was an American surgeon. In 1940 he earned a doctorate at Columbia University for a study called "Banked Blood," on how to store blood. He was the first African American to earn that degree there.

That same year, Britain was at war and short of blood. Drew became medical director of the Blood for Britain project in New York. Nearly 15,000 people gave blood, and the plasma was shipped across the ocean.

In 1941 he became director of the first American Red Cross Blood Bank, and he set up trucks to collect blood, the idea later known as bloodmobiles.

Then came the rule: blood from Black donors had to be kept separate. There was no scientific reason for it. Blood is blood. Drew objected, and in 1942 he resigned.

He went back to Howard University and Freedmen's Hospital to teach surgery. He died after a car crash in North Carolina in 1950, at 45.

And the story that a white hospital refused to treat him? Historians call it a myth. He was treated; his injuries were too severe.

He built the blood bank, and he would not accept its color line.
