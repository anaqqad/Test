Unbought. Unbossed. The first Black woman in Congress.

Shirley Anita St. Hill was born in Brooklyn on November 30, 1924, to immigrant parents: her mother from Barbados, her father from Guyana. She spent part of her childhood with her grandmother in Barbados, then came home to Brooklyn, graduated from Brooklyn College in 1946 and became a nursery school teacher and a director of day care centers.

In 1964 she won a seat in the New York State Assembly. In 1968 she ran for Congress in Brooklyn's 12th district under one slogan, "Unbought and Unbossed", and won: the first Black woman ever elected to the United States Congress.

The House leadership put the congresswoman from Brooklyn on the Agriculture Committee. She refused the assignment, took her protest to the Speaker and the Democratic caucus, and was moved to Veterans' Affairs. In 1971 she was a founding member of the Congressional Black Caucus and of the National Women's Political Caucus.

On January 25, 1972, she announced she was running for president: the first Black candidate to seek a major party's nomination, and the first woman to seek the Democratic one. She had little money and little support from the party, but at the convention in Miami Beach she won 152 delegate votes, about ten percent.

She served seven terms in Congress, until 1983, working to expand food stamps and helping to create the WIC nutrition program for mothers and children. She died on January 1, 2005. In 2015 President Obama awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom.

"Unbought and unbossed." That was Shirley Chisholm.
