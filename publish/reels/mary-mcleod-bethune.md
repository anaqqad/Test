Post text:
She started a school with $1.50. Today it's a university.

Mary McLeod Bethune was born near Mayesville, South Carolina, on July 10, 1875, the fifteenth of seventeen children of parents who had been enslaved. By the age of nine she could pick 250 pounds of cotton a day.

She fought for an education, graduated from Scotia Seminary in North Carolina, studied in Chicago, and became a teacher. In October 1904, in Daytona, Florida, she rented a small house and opened a school for Black girls. She had $1.50. Her first students were five little girls, and her own young son. Within two years the school had 250 students.

Her school later merged with the Cookman Institute, a college for men, and became Bethune-Cookman. She led it for decades.

In 1935 she founded the National Council of Negro Women. A year later President Franklin D. Roosevelt brought her into his government, at the National Youth Administration, where she became the highest-ranking African American woman in the federal government. She led his informal "Black Cabinet" and became a close friend of Eleanor Roosevelt. In 1945 she was the only woman of color at the founding conference of the United Nations in San Francisco.

She died in 1955. In 2022 her statue was placed in National Statuary Hall in the U.S. Capitol, the first state statue of an African American there.

$1.50 and five girls. Today: Bethune-Cookman University.
