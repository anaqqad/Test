## Facebook

Title:
The Night Harriet Tubman Freed More Than 700 People

Post text:
In 1863, a woman led a military raid that freed more than 700 enslaved people in one night.

During the Civil War, Harriet Tubman worked as a Union scout and spy in South Carolina. Her scouts mapped
the Combahee River and found where the Confederates had hidden mines. On the night of June 1, 1863, she
guided Union gunboats carrying about 300 soldiers, most of them Black, up the river. Enslaved families ran
and swam to the boats, and by morning more than 700 people were free. In ten years on the Underground
Railroad she had led about 70 people out. That night, ten times as many. Yet she never got a soldier's
pension, only a widow's.

Did you know this part of Harriet Tubman's story?

## Notes before posting

- Count: sources give 727, 750, 756 or "more than 700"; the reel says "more than 700".
- "Led": the US House history office says she was the first African American woman to lead troops in a
  military operation; she guided the raid with Col. James Montgomery. The popular claim "first woman in US
  history to lead a military raid" is not used.
- "About 70" Underground Railroad passengers follows modern scholarship (Britannica); the older "300"
  figure comes from Sarah Bradford's 1868 biography and is considered exaggerated.
- The Harper's Weekly page also shows the scarred back of a man named Gordon; that part is not used
  (no graphic violence). Only the top engraving of the raid is used.
- No quotes are used.
- Images: H. Seymour Squyer, c. 1885 (National Portrait Gallery); Benjamin F. Powelson, 1868-69; Tubman
  late in life, c. 1911; Harper's Weekly, July 4, 1863 (Library of Congress); Union Army map of the
  Combahee, 1865 (National Archives); Beaufort encampment, 1861; freedpeople near Beaufort, 1862.
  All public domain.
