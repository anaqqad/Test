Enslaved, she sued her master. And won.

Her name was Bett, and everyone called her Mum Bett. Born around 1744, she was enslaved for nearly thirty years in the household of Colonel John Ashley in Sheffield, Massachusetts.

One day Mrs. Ashley swung a red-hot kitchen shovel at a young enslaved girl. Bett stepped in and took the blow. She carried the scar for the rest of her life.

In 1780 Massachusetts adopted a new constitution. Serving at the Ashley table, Bett heard the men discussing its opening words: all men are born free and equal. She decided those words applied to her too.

So she went to a young lawyer in Stockbridge, Theodore Sedgwick, and asked him to sue for her freedom. Because a woman's legal standing was limited, an enslaved man from the same household, Brom, joined the suit. On August 22, 1781, a jury in Great Barrington ruled that Brom and Bett were not Ashley's property, and awarded them 30 shillings in damages. Ashley dropped his appeal.

Her case, together with the Quock Walker cases that followed, helped end slavery in Massachusetts by 1783.

She chose a new name for herself: Elizabeth Freeman. She worked for wages in the Sedgwick household, became a respected midwife, healer and nurse, and bought a house of her own. She died on December 28, 1829, and was buried in the Sedgwick family plot in Stockbridge, the only person buried there who was not a Sedgwick.

Enslaved, she sued her master. And won.

Had you heard of Mum Bett before?
