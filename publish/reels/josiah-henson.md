The real "Uncle Tom" led 118 people to freedom.

Josiah Henson was born enslaved in Charles County, Maryland, in 1789. As a boy he watched his family sold apart at auction.

He grew up to run his enslaver's farm, and in 1825 he was sent to lead eighteen enslaved people to Kentucky. He had been promised his freedom, and he saved 350 dollars to buy himself. His enslaver changed the price to 1,000. Then Henson learned he would be sold to New Orleans, away from his wife and children.

In 1830 he ran. He crossed the free states with his wife and four children, carrying the two smallest on his back, and on October 28, 1830, they reached Upper Canada. Free.

Near Dresden, Ontario, he helped build the Dawn Settlement, a community and a school for people who had escaped slavery. And he kept going back. By his own count, he guided 118 people to freedom.

In 1849 he published the story of his life. Harriet Beecher Stowe's Uncle Tom's Cabin came three years later, and many believe Henson's story inspired it.

Today "Uncle Tom" is used as an insult. The man behind the name was anything but.
