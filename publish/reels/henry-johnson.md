Wounded 21 times. He kept fighting.

Henry Johnson was a railroad porter at Union Station in Albany, New York. In June 1917 he enlisted in the 15th New York, an all-Black National Guard regiment from Harlem that became the 369th Infantry, the Harlem Hellfighters.

The U.S. Army kept Black units out of front-line combat, so in March 1918 the regiment was lent to the French army.

In the early hours of May 15, 1918, Johnson and Private Needham Roberts were on sentry duty at a small outpost when a German raiding party attacked, at least a dozen men by the official count. Roberts was badly wounded. Johnson threw grenades until he had none left. His rifle jammed, so he used it as a club, then his bolo knife. He would not let the raiders carry Roberts away as a prisoner, and they pulled back.

Johnson had been wounded 21 times. France awarded him the Croix de Guerre with palm.

His own army sent him home with discharge papers that did not mention his wounds, and he received no disability pay. He died in 1929, at 36, with almost nothing.

In 2015, 97 years after that night, President Barack Obama awarded Henry Johnson the Medal of Honor.
