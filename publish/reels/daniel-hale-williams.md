## Facebook

Title:
He Operated Beside a Beating Heart.

Post text:
In 1893, he opened a man's chest and stitched beside a beating heart. The man walked out alive.

Daniel Hale Williams was born in Hollidaysburg, Pennsylvania, in 1856, the son of a barber. As a young man he was a shoemaker's apprentice and a barber himself, before he trained in medicine and graduated from Chicago Medical College in 1883.

In Chicago, Black patients were turned away from hospitals and Black women could not train as nurses. So in May 1891 Williams opened his own: Provident Hospital and Training School for Nurses, with Black and white doctors working side by side.

On a July night in 1893, a 24-year-old man named James Cornish was brought in with a stab wound to the chest. There were no X-rays and no blood transfusions. Williams opened the chest and found that the knife had torn the pericardium, the sac around the heart. He sewed it closed while the heart beat beneath his hands.

Fifty-one days later, Cornish walked out of Provident Hospital. He lived for more than twenty years.

It was one of the first operations of its kind in America (a surgeon in St. Louis, Henry Dalton, had repaired a pericardium two years earlier, but had not yet published it). Williams went on to run Freedmen's Hospital in Washington, D.C., helped found the National Medical Association for Black doctors, and in 1913 became the first Black member of the American College of Surgeons. He died in 1931.

A barber's son stitched beside a beating heart. His patient walked out alive.

## Notes before posting

- NOT "the first open-heart surgery". Williams repaired the pericardium (the sac around the heart) in 1893; Henry Dalton did a similar repair in 1891, and the first suture of the heart muscle itself was in 1896 (Ludwig Rehn). The reel says "one of the first operations of its kind in America".
- Birth year: 1856 (most sources) or 1858 (Columbia).
