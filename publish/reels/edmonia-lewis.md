## Facebook

Title:

Post text:
Her masterpiece ended up on a horse's grave.

Edmonia Lewis was born in upstate New York around 1844, to a free Black father and an Ojibwe mother, and was orphaned as a child. At Oberlin College in 1862, two white classmates accused her of poisoning them. She was beaten by a mob of white vigilantes. The charges against her were dropped.

She became a sculptor, and moved to Rome, where she carved white marble alongside the leading American sculptors of her day, often taking Black and Native American subjects.

In 1876 she sent her largest work, The Death of Cleopatra, to the Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia. Lewis boldly showed the queen already dead, seated on her throne. One newspaper reported that it drew more admiration and bigger crowds than the other works in the hall.

Then it disappeared. It was used as decoration in a Chicago saloon. A gambler and racetrack owner known as "Blind John" Condon bought it to mark the grave of his racehorse, also named Cleopatra, at the Harlem racetrack outside Chicago. For nearly a century it was lost, covered in graffiti, and finally left in a salvage yard.

The Historical Society of Forest Park, Illinois, rescued it and had it restored: a new nose, a new chin, the graffiti removed. In 1994 they gave it to the Smithsonian American Art Museum, where you can see it today.

Her masterpiece ended up on a horse's grave. Now it's in the Smithsonian.

Had you heard of Edmonia Lewis?

## Notes before posting

- The statue's weight: about two tons (Yale teaching unit) or nearly three (Wikipedia); the reel says "two tons".
- When it was found in the yard: "late 1970s" (Smithsonian Magazine) or "the 1980s" (Forest Park Review, SAAM label), so no year is given.
- Oberlin: Smithsonian Magazine says she was acquitted, Wikipedia says the charges were dismissed; the reel says "the charges were dropped".
- Not used: Boy Scouts painting the statue (Forest Park Review only); her death in London in 1907 and her grave, found and restored in 2017 (one fetched source).
- No Wikimedia photos of the statue were used: their licences were unclear. Both statue photos are the Smithsonian's own, CC0.
