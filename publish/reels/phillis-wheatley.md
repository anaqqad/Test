## Facebook

Title:
They Didn't Believe She Wrote Her Own Poems.

Post text:
They didn't believe she wrote her own poems.

Around 1761, a girl of about seven was kidnapped from West Africa, most likely from the region of today's Senegal or Gambia, and shipped to Boston on a slave ship called the Phillis. John Wheatley bought her for his wife, Susanna. They named her after the ship: Phillis Wheatley.

The Wheatley children taught her to read. Within a few years she was reading the Bible and the Greek and Latin classics, and in 1767, at about fourteen, she published her first poem.

By the early 1770s she had enough poems for a book. But printers and readers wanted proof that an enslaved African girl had really written them. So the book opened with a signed statement, "To the Publick", from eighteen of the most powerful men in Boston, among them the governor, Thomas Hutchinson, and John Hancock. It reads: "We whose Names are under-written, do assure the World, that the Poems... were (as we verily believe) written by Phillis, a young Negro Girl."

"Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral" was published in London in September 1773, the first book of poetry published by an African American.

Soon after, she was freed. In 1775 she sent a poem to George Washington; he wrote back and invited her to visit him. In 1778 she married John Peters, a free Black man. Her children died young, the family fell into poverty, and Phillis Wheatley died on December 5, 1784, at about thirty-one.

They didn't believe she wrote her own poems. She did.

Have you read any of her poems?

## Notes before posting

- The 18 signatures are printed in the book itself (the governor, the lieutenant-governor, John Hancock, ministers, and her owner John Wheatley). The attestation says she "has been examined by some of the best Judges". The popular scene of a formal oral exam before all 18 men is debated by historians, so the reel says only that they signed a statement.
- Washington wrote back (February 1776) and invited her; whether she actually visited him is uncertain (the National Women's History Museum says she likely never accepted), so the reel stops at "He wrote back."
- "The first African American to publish a book of poetry": National Women's History Museum wording ("broadly recognized as the first...").
